# **Background** 

Power distribution is a key link in the power sector value chain. Power distribution segment comprises supply and distribution of power purchased from generating companies across the regions to end-use entities such as domestic consumers, industries, commercial users, agricultural consumers, railways, and public utilities among others through different voltage levels including 11kV/33kV/66kV. The distribution segment is thus the ultimate delivery point of service and is the originating source of collection and liquidity for power sector. 

Over the years, the financial and operational performances of the power distribution sector in India (particularly the state-owned power distribution companies [DISCOMs] which dominate the distribution space in term of area covered and energy supplied) have been below satisfactory at the aggregate level. In fact, the deterioration has been particularly more prominent in the last decade leading to roll out of number of schemes by the government of India to improve the sector. Few of the notable initiatives were the Ujwal DISCOM Assurance Yojana (UDAY) to achieve operational turnaround and debt restructuring, Deendayal Upadhyaya Gram Jyoti Yojana (DDUGJY) for feeder segregation to provide reliable power across categories of load, Integrated Power Development Scheme (IPDS) for network upgradation and implementing IT systems for efficient auditing and billing systems. In COVID19 lockdown and thereafter, the working capital support through Power Finance Corporation (PFC) and Rural Electrification Corporation (REC) under the Atmanirbhar Bharat scheme temporarily improved the liquidity of the discoms. 

Ministry of Power notified Electricity (Late Payment Surcharge and Related Matters) Rules, 2022 which allow settlement of outstanding dues including principal and LPS through monthly instalments of 12 to 48 months depending on quantum of overdues without further imposition of LPS. This has enabled reduction in dues to power generating companies as deviation from the payment schedule may curtail the access of discom to power exchanges. Ministry of Power has also notified subsidy accounting rules for timely payout of subsidy on quarterly basis. Financial stability in the sector can be achieved only once the DISCOMs post significant improvement in aggregate AT&C losses and bridge ACS-ARR gap through successful implementation and commercialisation of network upgradation plans, regular tariff hike by the regulator to ensure maximum pass-on of higher cost and rationalisation of overheads through organisational restructuring. Accordingly, the Ministry of Power has also rolled out result-oriented Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme to address structural changes in discoms. 

This rating methodology covers the essential credit perspectives analysed for assessment of a distribution licensee (DL) and a distribution franchisee (DF). A distribution licensee is responsible for procuring power directly from the generating companies (gencos) and other sources and sells it to consumers across different categories. On the other hand, a DF is appointed by a DL to sell and collect the revenue from consumers on its behalf. In DF model, the power procurement function lies with DL. 

In India, the DL business is predominantly being owned by the state-controlled entities with the presence of private players in few states and union territories. On the other hand, the DF business is being operated by private entities only.

# **Rating methodology** 

CARE Rating Limited (CareEdge Ratings) analyses the credit risk of DISCOMs by evaluating the following broad parameters for arriving at the rating: 

- Consumer category mix 

- Operational performance 

- Cost structure analysis and quality of service 

- Regulatory compliance and support 

- Extent of Government support 

- Financial risk 

- Quality/ adequacy in accounting compliance and disclosures 

- Management risk 

- Structural risk (in case of distribution franchisee) 

- Environmental, Social and Governance risk 

# **Consumer category mix** 

Within its licensed area, the DISCOM supplies power to variety of customers, including domestic consumers, commercial users, industrial users, agricultural users, and railways among others. Higher proportion of power sales to commercial and industrial consumers is seen favourably against other consuming categories since such consumers have higher paying capacity and increase in power procurement cost can be passed on to consumers without relying on subsidies from government. It has been seen that there is moderate inverse correlation between the proportion of industrial/commercial load and Aggregate Technical and Commercial (AT&C) losses. Due to better metering, lower incidence of thefts, billing and collection efficiency is generally observed to be better for industrial and commercial (C&I) customers. Industrial load comprises high tension (HT) consumption which tend to reduce the line loss upon supply at higher voltage. 

Licensed areas with higher agricultural load generally have issues related to unmetered sales which increases the DISCOM’s reliance on tariff subsidy support from the state government. This exposes the DISCOM to the vagaries of state finance and timeliness and adequacy of subsidy realisation (subsidy realised versus booked). Tariffs in these categories are also cross subsidised by industrial and commercial consumers. Cross-subsidisation makes commercial & industrial (C&I) tariff less competitive, thus exposing the high-credit quality C&I clients to switch to open access of power from other sources. States with stringent open access norm with high switching cost will have lower churning of C&I clients. Licensed area with high C&I load mix and consumption growth outpacing domestic and agricultural load in that area reduces the cross subsidisation issues of the DISCOM and its reliance on tariff subsidy from the state government. 

# **Operational performance** 

The most important operational efficiency parameter for a distribution licensee/franchisee is the AT&C loss. It considers billing efficiency and collection efficiency of the licensee/franchisee. Billing efficiency indicates the fraction of energy purchased that is ultimately billed to the customer. It is a function of coverage and accuracy in metering, reading, high-voltage distribution, feeder segregation, network up-gradation to curb line loss and effectiveness of energy audit. Collection efficiency indicates the fraction of revenue collected from consumers upon sale of power and from the state government (in the form of tariff subsidy and grants if applicable) to the revenue billed by the entity. It depends on the awareness and recovery initiatives of the DISCOM, digitisation of payment and ability to realise subsidy on time. If AT&C losses of both DISCOMs are similar, DISCOM B will incur higher loss for every unit supplied in its licensed area in comparison to DISCOM A. 

Efficient power procurement strategy improves the cost competitiveness for the DISCOM. It is desirable to have the actual average power purchase cost to be aligned with the average power purchase cost approved by the regulator. The DISCOMs are also provided renewable purchase obligation/hydro power purchase obligation (RPO/HPO) target by the regulator. More efficient DISCOMs will have better compliance to RPO targets through procurement of power from renewable sources at competitive rates. 

Operations and maintenance (O&M) expenses and employee cost: It is desirable that DISCOMs have lower overhead cost per unit sold over the years. O&M cost depends on the geographical spread of the licensed area and the load density. One of the two DISCOMs with similar aggregate loads may have lower O&M cost if one has more urban load centers where the load density is much higher. Apart from O&M cost, employee cost is also an important part of the total cost for DISCOM and is analysed for its efficiency. 

DISCOMs can rationalise its employee cost through higher outsourcing of non-core activities. At instances, the existence of DF in a licensed area is seen as a positive considering the operational efficiency (in terms of higher billing/ collection efficiency, reduction in O&M cost and employee cost) brought in by the DF to the licensed area. 

From quality of service perspective, reliability of power supply, extent of release of new connections/ modification in existing connections through transparent and prompt manner and the extent of adoption of digitisation by the DISCOMs can be considered.

# **Regulatory risk** 

The DLs operate in regulated cost-plus regime where they have the opportunity to earn fixed return and incentives upon over-achievement of few normative parameters. ARR’s different components are power purchase cost, employee cost, operation and maintenance expenses, general and administrative expenses, interest on term loan and working capital loan, depreciation and post-tax return on equity. In the cost-plus regime, the DISCOM also faces the risk of under-recovery of actual cost incurred if this was due to shortfall in meeting normative parameter considering operational inefficiencies. 

The existence of MYT with the current period of appraisal being included in the control period provides more clarity in terms of revenue, expenses and capex trend for the DISCOM. Typically, in a MYT regime, the regulatory commission defines the trajectory for financial and operational parameters for the control period (for example: T&D, collection efficiency, capex, interest rate, O&M cost). The distribution licensees have to regularly file petition for tariff with the respective State Electricity Regulatory Commission (SERC) which reviews the actual cost incurred in the past and estimates an Aggregate Revenue Requirement (ARR) for the next fiscal year. CareEdge Ratings analyses the timeliness in filing of tariff petition by the DISCOM over the years and the timely release of tariff order by the SERC and major disallowance in cost against claimed by discoms. Typically, for the tariff for the ensuing year, the DISCOM files petition for true-up of past audited accounts and tariff for the projected year by November 30. The SERC recognises the actual accounts for the last year and sets tariff for the next financial year by March 30. 

Non filing of tariff petition by the DISCOM or non-issuance of tariff order by SERC within the stipulated timeframe is viewed negatively from the credit perspective. Similarly, tariff order without true-up of previous year audited account or significant underestimation of the revenue gap by the regulator for the past period of true-up and for the projected period is negatively factored. 

The regulator may only allow gradual recovery of the revenue gap to avoid tariff shock to the customers over several years. In such case, the DISCOM may book regulatory income during the year which is expected to be recovered in medium-to-long term. The DISCOM has to arrange for financing of such assets at reasonable borrowing cost and tenor to avoid liquidity mismatch. In such a case, the regulatory asset liquidation plan, the source of financing of such assets and the extent of carrying cost on the regulatory asset allowed by SERC may be evaluated. 

At times, DISCOM can challenge the SERC’s disallowance of tariff parameters while arriving at the ARR, in judicial and regulatory forums. Due to this, the DISCOM may recognise revenue gap in its books of account which may significantly vary from that recognised by the SERC. This revenue gap more than the quantum recognised by SERC is viewed negatively. 

Regular tariff hike is desirable to absorb the increasing power purchase cost and the escalating O&M cost. Since a significant amount of expenses (capacity charge paid to gencos, interest, depreciation, and overheads among others) of the DISCOM are fixed, a cost-reflective tariff will have higher proportion of fixed tariff. The availability of provision for Fuel Price and Power Purchase Adjustment Charges (FPPPAC) mechanism and timely issuance of FPPPAC order to factor in seasonal fluctuation in power purchase is factored favourably. It has been observed that the implementation of the framework is either absent or done with a time lag in several states.

# **Extent of Government Support** 

The extent of support from the state government is derived by understanding few important aspects –quantum of tariff subsidy burden, financial flexibility of the government, its past track record of releasing of subsidy, political environment in the state and arrangement of finance for the DISCOM. Credit profile of the state government is also analysed to determine the ability to support discoms. Budgetary allocation of subsidy to be paid to distribution utilities is viewed positively. 

High tariff subsidy component is undesirable as the timely realisation would depend on the state finances and would thus affect cashflows of the entity. The ability of the state to pay the entire tariff subsidy booked on time fairly depends on the cash flow position and leverage of the state. Lower realisation of subsidy booked affects the AT&C loss of the DISCOM. 

If the political environment is hostile to tariff hikes, it may impact the tariff determination process. Such situation where tariffs are not cost reflective and are not transparent in cost pass through to consumers would be negative from the credit perspective. 

The extent of government support to the DISCOM can also be gauged through the financial support provided by the same to the DISCOM in the past. State governments having demonstrated support through infusion of equity, loss funding arrangement of discoms, grants and loans with cumulative interest and longer tenor are viewed favourably for the DISCOM. 

# **Financial risk** 

DISCOM’s financial risk profile is gauged through the following parameters:- 

Revenue gap is the difference between the average cost of supply (ACS) and average revenue realised (ARR). Increase in the revenue gap over years would be negative from the credit perspective while negative revenue gap would be favoured. ARR considers collection from consumers and subsidy paid by state government. High dependence on subsidy is viewed as a negative since amount or timing of subsidy payment may be different from the subsidy booked by the distribution utility. 

Leverage is important since DISCOMs have capex and working capital intensive business model. Due to its low profitability and frequent funding requirement, DISCOMs also tend to have higher creditor level. The utilisation of addition in debt or creditor during the year for working capital purpose, funding of loss or for capex is to be analysed. 

The trend of creditor days for DISCOM over the years indicates liquidity and ability of clearing dues on time. Accumulation of overdue creditor would attract higher late payment surcharge (LPSC) by the genco. Payment to creditors on a timely basis aligned with PPA terms is viewed positively. 

DISCOMs may have high debtor days due to long pending dues from several government departments. Deviations or delays considering receivables would put pressure on the financial condition of the utility. Receivable days measures the amount of days it takes for the utility to collect the receivables. A shorter receivable period would be viewed positively by CareEdge Ratings.

Discoms have moderate to high capital expenditure requirement to meet the increase in demand of power by consumers and strengthening of distribution infrastructure to improve the operational performance. Funding mix of such capital expenditure through grants from state government, internal accruals, consumer deposits or debt is evaluated to gauge dependence on incremental debt. Discoms may depend on debt for working capital requirements as well considering delay in tariff orders or limited tariff hikes. Increased reliance on working capital debt is viewed negatively. 

The adequacy of projected free cash flow of the DISCOM post meeting the capex requirement in meeting the debt obligation, that is the coverage metrics is checked. Cash flows could be adversely affected due to many reasons such as inadequacy of revenue considering average cost of supply which may be due to lower tariff, lower recoverability of dues due to higher AT&C losses and delays considering payment of dues from government. CareEdge Ratings assesses the company’s overall liquidity profile based on its methodology on “Liquidity Analysis of Non-Financial Sector Entities” on our website www.careratings.com 

# **Quality/adequacy in Accounting Compliance and Disclosures** 

CareEdge Ratings reviews accounting policies adopted and disclosed in the audited financial statements, qualifications and legal disputes highlighted by the auditor, significant accumulation of statutory dues, non-payment of employee-related liabilities. Instances of auditor not able to express fair opinion on the financial statement are negatively factored. 

CareEdge Ratings also analyses the contingent liabilities and off-balance sheet commitments as a percentage of total net worth and monitors significant devolvement of such liability which may adversely affect the utility’s liquidity. 

# **Structural risk (in case of Distribution Franchisee)** 

For DFs, CareEdge Ratings reviews the key terms of DF agreement such as tenor, annualised input rate to the DF, payment terms, minimum annual capex commitment and its period. The lengthier the tenor of agreement, greater is the headroom in garnering benefits of capex for network upgradation (particularly reducing AT&C loss and thus improving the cash accrual). The DF’s cash flow is highly sensitive to billing efficiency and collection efficiency. 

# **Management evaluation** 

The evaluation of quality of management is an important part of all credit rating assessments. CareEdge Ratings evaluates the quality of management from different perspectives such as experience in the industry, financial capabilities, and accounting policies among others. Management with strong commitment to the business, ability of the promoter to provide financial support in stress situations and the extent of such support and payment track record and dividend policy is also examined. 

In case of entity providing regular upstream of cash flows to the parent which adversely affects the entity’s financial and liquidity position, it would be viewed negatively by CareEdge Ratings.

# **Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risk** 

ESG risk assessment considers the factors which may have bearing to sustainability of operations. While stricter control on this risk parameters and compliance generally does not positively affect the discom’s credit rating, material ESG risk which is not efficiently controlled may constrain the rating. 

# Environmental risk: 

Discoms have commitment to purchase a certain fraction of power from renewable sources such as RPO/ HPO as cited above. In the past, the discoms have been reliant largely on fossil fuel based power sources which accounted for vast majority power purchase. Such power sources have an adverse impact on the environment and hence reducing reliance on such power source form a critical aspect of evaluation of environmental risk for the discoms. CareEdge Ratings evaluates the variance between the actual power purchase from cleaner power sources against the targeted RPO/HPO trajectory. 

# Social risk: 

Discoms’ social risk profile is mitigated to some extent by their ability to provide uninterrupted power supply to all strata of consumers in the licensed area considering universal supply obligation defined in the Electricity Act 2003. 

# Governance risk: 

Governance risk in a corporate is determined by a host of factors, which includes and is not limited to the following, including financial reporting, corporate practices, adherence to code of conduct, board composition, whistle-blower policies, and investor relations among others. CareEdge Ratings tries to form a broad opinion from the available information regarding the governance standards of the entity being evaluated. CareEdge Ratings also assesses composition of the company’s board of directors, level of compliance with policies, quality of financial reporting and adequacy in disclosure from the perspective of corporate governance. 

# **Conclusion** 

The rating outcome is ultimately an assessment of fundamentals and probabilities of change in fundamentals. The rating committee of CareEdge Ratings analyses each of the above factors and their linkages to arrive at the overall assessment of credit quality of an issuer based on its holistic judgement.
