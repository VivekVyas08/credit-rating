# **Background** 

The auto dealership industry forms a critical link in the automobile value chain by facilitating sales of auto original equipment manufacturers (OEMs). Auto dealerships play an instrumental role in shaping consumer perception of OEM brands, as customer experience is largely defined by the quality of interaction and service at the dealership level. Accordingly, dealerships are required to make substantial investments in establishing an appropriate showroom ambience that aligns with the OEM’s brand positioning, expenditure on employee training, leasing retail space at prime locations, and provision of ancillary offerings such as vehicle financing and insurance. In addition to salesrelated infrastructure, significant investments are also required for service workshops for vehicle maintenance and repairs, and for warehousing facilities for inventory storage. Considering that the gestation period for a new dealership may extend from less than a year to several years, dealerships need to demonstrate adequate financial flexibility to sustain operations during the initial phase of limited revenue generation. Operating an auto dealership, therefore, necessitates a strong financial profile, capacity and willingness of promoters to support operations in the early stages. Prudent working capital management and stringent control over fixed costs are essential, given the inherently thin operating margins in the business. 

Fortunes of auto dealerships are directly linked to the performance of their respective OEMs. Dealerships have to deal with unexpected shifts in buyer preferences towards newer models launched by competitor OEMs, impacting their revenues. 

# **Industry risk** 

Given the trading nature of the business, recurring investments required are low, and initial investment is restricted to outgo towards acquiring office spaces and on interiors or fit-outs. Hence, entry barriers in terms of investment or technology are low. The key success factor is to operate a dealership in a location that has a good ‘catchment area’, which would ensure an optimal level of revenues. Dealerships of OEMs in medium and heavy commercial vehicles (MHCVs) and tractors are typically outside cities. MHCV dealerships tend to display higher cyclicality of revenues in line with the OEM. 

Dealerships are expected to offer insurance and financing solutions through tie-ups with insurance companies and banks or non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) as part of the deal. The ability to offer car servicing and maintenance at the same or alternate location is critical, as OEMs seek to maximise their service network. Increasingly, dealers have to be able to buy back used vehicles of buyers, to facilitate sales of new vehicles. 

Given the steady increase in leads generated in online portals of auto OEMs in the last few years, CARE Ratings Limited (CareEdge Ratings) assesses dealer’s preparedness, ability or proficiency to handle online queries, and to convert them into sales. CareEdge Ratings notes that the dealership has a limited role to play in case the buyer has decided to purchase the vehicle online from the OEM, with responsibilities restricted to completing balance formalities related to the sale. On the other hand, if the OEM has passed on an online lead to the dealer, the onus of pursuing the lead and converting it into sales lies with the dealer.

# **Demand drivers** 

Key demand drivers for auto dealership revenues would be the same as for OEMs. In case of passenger vehicles (PV) and two-wheelers (2W), rising disposable income, inadequacy of public transport, and overall cost of ownership are key demand drivers that influence purchasing decision. Especially, in the case of 2W, rising rural incomes and increasing traffic congestion in urban centers continue to support demand, while rapid expansion of e-commerce and last-mile delivery services emerged as an additional structural driver—boosting new purchases and faster replacement cycles, particularly for entry-level motorcycles and electric two-wheelers. In case of MHCVs, the level of industrial activity and agricultural output play a key role in driving demand. Demand for buses would be from government and private companies, educational institutes, municipal and state public transport departments, and from private bus operators for intra-state and long-distance routes. As light commercial vehicles (LCVs) are used for last mile connectivity, including door-to-door transportation of lesser loads, its demand is partly driven by the level of regional industrial and commercial activities. Strong growth emerging in online retail has also been a contributing factor determining LCV sales. Demand for small buses from educational institutes, tourism services, and hotels is also a factor driving LCV sales. In case of tractors, as the demand is largely rural, monsoons and level of agricultural output remain key driver of sales. Apart from these factors, sales of automobiles would be impacted by favourable or unfavourable changes in regulations, for example, scrappage policy in conjunction with stricter emission norms will support sales of new vehicles, while upward revision in axle load norms for trucks was detrimental to sales. 

# **Methodology** 

CareEdge Ratings has standard methodologies for rating companies in manufacturing and services sectors. It encompasses an assessment of risk factors that could potentially affect credit risk of an entity, such as economy and industry risk analysis, business risk, financial risk, and management quality. However, considering the size and diversity of the services sector, CareEdge Ratings has devised methodologies specific to industries within the sector. These methodologies attempt to point out factors, above those mentioned in the broader methodology devised for the service sector, which are considered while analysing entities belonging to a particular industry. Although strictly falling under trading activity, the auto dealership business is closely aligned to the auto sector, and hence, drivers for auto sales in a particular year and the forecast period would be analysed to understand overall prospects for auto dealerships. This would address industry risk parameter. Other broad parameters that would be analysed include the management, business or operational and financial risk factors, in addition to project risk (if any) and environmental, social, and governance (ESG) risk factors. 

Following is a list of such additional factors under ‘business or operational risks’, ‘financial risks’ and ‘ESG risks’, and their analytical implications, considered by CareEdge Ratings while arriving at ratings of players that operate in the automobile dealership sector. 

# **Business or operational risks** 

- **Market share of OEMs in India and share of dealers in OEM sales** 

Given that top three auto OEMs in each segment dominate the market, their dealerships generate high revenues, which would also be fairly predictable. Market disruptions due to introduction of popular models by competitors could sway volumes away from market leaders in the short term. However, market leaders are expected to largely maintain their position by introduction of new models and upgrades of their existing models on a regular basis. Hence, fortunes of auto dealerships move in tandem with those of their principals. Dealers accounting for a higher share of OEM volumes receive preferential treatment from the OEMs, typically in terms of choice of more popular models manufactured, favourable delivery timelines, and other incentives. A key risk for automobile dealerships- particularly those associated with OEMs, having relatively small market shares—arises

from potential exit of the OEM from the Indian market  from sustained weak sales performance. In such a scenario, dealer’s investments in infrastructure and branding may need to be written-off, unless the dealership is able to swiftly secure an arrangement with another OEM. CareEdge Ratings views dealership of leading OEMs as positive, as it translates into enduring a competitive advantage. If a dealership has consistently managed to register strong revenue growth, achieving a material share of OEM’s volumes, this too would have a positive bearing on the rating. On the other hand, inability of a dealership to sell even popular models of the OEM would point to certain inherent deficiencies in the dealership, such as improper location or lack of service infrastructure. 

# • **Characteristics of catchment area in terms of location, size, buying power and competition** 

CareEdge Ratings positively views dealerships operating in prime locations with a large ‘catchment’ area, and hence, enjoying higher sales. CareEdge Ratings also assesses whether buying power of the local population is suited to the nature of products offered. For instance, a showroom for luxury cars in a middle-class neighbourhood may not necessarily do well, but a dealership of two-wheelers and entry level cars would. Similarly, whether a dealership is present in a metro, Tier-I or Tier-II town or village will determine the nature of products to be offered based on the market being catered to. 

If an OEM finds that a market in a particular region is attractive and not being served adequately by a single dealership, it may choose to authorise additional dealerships there. This is typically the case where existing dealership is unable to quickly expand operations vide multiple showrooms to cover the entire region. In such instances, revenue potential of the existing dealership declines sharply, while the OEM benefits from its multiple dealerships competing with each other to push sales. 

# • **Diversification of revenues** 

CareEdge Ratings evaluates historical revenue sources of an auto dealership and potential ancillary revenue streams to gauge stability of its total operating income (TOI). Companies with showrooms at multiple locations, catering to clientele in different regions, would benefit through a larger catchment area. A dealership that derives significant revenues from sale of spares, servicing, and repair shop services would report higher profit before interest, lease rentals, depreciation and taxation (PBILDT) margins, given the inherently higher margins in these revenue segments. Revenue diversity can also be achieved by handling agencies of non-competing products of multiple OEMs. 

Almost all auto dealerships also offer insurance policies and auto loans vide tie-ups with insurance companies and lenders. In the last few years, many auto dealers have branched out in the used vehicle market as well, vide their OEMs (who themselves may have an established used vehicle business), or through their own network of used car dealers. By offering to buyback used vehicles of potential buyers, a dealership enhances the prospects of generating sales of new vehicles. These complementary revenue streams boost sales volumes by allowing the dealership to project itself as a one-stop-shop for OEM offerings, and help supplement the dealership’s revenues through commissions earned on insurance policies issued, loans sanctioned, and usedcar deals facilitated within its premises. 

# • **Cost structure and break-even level of sales** 

Given the fragile operating margins in the business and the high proportion of fixed costs (typically, lease rentals and employee costs) in the overall cost structure, achieving a certain scale (in terms of total operating revenues) is important to be profitable.

CareEdge Ratings studies overall cost structure of a dealership to assess the level of fixed costs in the cost structure. CareEdge Ratings also analyses break-even level of sales to understand whether a dealership’s operations are inherently profitable or not. Breakeven level of sales and payback depend on multiple factors, such as the popularity of the OEM’s vehicles, the dealership location, whether owned or leased premises (determines total investment made), and also size of the service network of a dealer, which is a key driver of purchase decisions for automobiles. Based on the industry study, CareEdge Ratings believes that dealerships that are favourably placed in terms of these factors can breakeven in less than a year, whereas those dealerships entailing higher investment and higher cost structure relative to sales could take significantly more time. A significant delay in achieving breakeven would point to deficiencies in the company’s business model. 

# • **Working capital management** 

As debt raised by auto dealerships is mostly to fund working capital; tight control is key to ensuring adequate debt servicing capability and to protect net margins. Working capital requirement of a dealership is driven largely by the annual business plan drawn up by the OEM, which lays down sales volumes that individual dealers are expected to achieve in the financial year. The number of days inventory remains unsold in the dealership less the credit period offered by the OEM, together with the inventory value, are essentially the determinant of working capital for the dealership. Dealerships of OEMs with popular models will have lower working capital requirement with fast-moving inventory. 

The working capital position could vary across auto dealerships depending on credit terms allowed by the OEM. While luxury brands demand typically require full advance from dealers before shipment of inventory, most OEMs in India provide a credit period to their dealers. Credit policy of OEMs would also differ depending on whether they are new entrants in the market, in which case they tend to offer longer credit, as dealers would otherwise be unwilling to do business with the OEM whose offerings are untested in the Indian marketplace. As products gain traction in the market, OEMs normally reduce credit period to align with market practices. 

CareEdge Ratings notes added benefits that dealers of leading OEMs have in terms of reduced interest rates from lenders due to intervention of the OEM. Banks offer working capital lines for funding the inventory of dealerships under the ‘Channel Financing’ scheme of the respective OEMs. The lender draws comfort from the fast-selling nature of the OEM’s products, and sanction terms usually mandate channelling payment from buyers into specific accounts, with covenants to prioritise debt servicing. Channel financing is a popular means of funding temporary surge in working capital requirements, especially during the festive season. In these instances, auto dealerships have to avail channel financing prudently to fund the purchase of most popular models of the OEM, as loans availed would have to be repaid within a short time frame, normally less than a month. 

CareEdge Ratings considers factors such as market position of the OEM and demand for its key models to assess the likely impact on working capital and profitability of dealer’s operations. 

The inventory turnover ratio is also relied upon to assess efficiency of operations of the dealer.

# • **Transition Risk from Rising EV Penetration** 

The Indian auto dealership industry faces transition risk as electric vehicle (EV) adoption accelerates. Driven by cost advantages, government incentives, and aggressive expansion by EV-focused OEMs, the shift towards EV vehicles is gradually reducing demand for internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles. As a result, dealerships heavily reliant on ICE portfolios are susceptible to slower inventory turnover and increased working capital requirements. Those aligned with OEMs that have a strong EV roadmap and diversified product offerings are better positioned, while dealerships with concentrated ICE portfolios face heightened business and liquidity risks as EV penetration continues to rise. 

# **Financial risk** 

CareEdge Ratings’ assessment of overall financial risk profile of an auto dealership is based on financial indicators, in line with its methodology for the credit rating of non-financial sector companies. Please refer to CARE Ratings’ criteria ‘Financial Ratios – Non Financial Sector’ on our website www.careratings.com for additional details. 

While assessing the leverage, CareEdge Ratings considers that auto dealerships could follow either an asset heavy model, where dealerships own showrooms, or an asset light model, where they lease them. They could also use a mixed business model, where they partly own few showrooms and lease the balance. In case of leased premises, CareEdge Ratings considers adjusted debt factoring in the present value of payments in the lease period and considers lease payments as part of interest costs (in line with IND-AS 116). CareEdge Ratings notes that generally, capex requirements are low for auto dealerships, unless it involves setting up a new showroom on owned premises. Hence, debt normally comprises working capital borrowings. Given the low margins and resultantly slower build-up of net-worth, a spike in borrowings results in sharp increases in leverage. 

# **Liquidity cushion** 

CareEdge Ratings assesses available sources of liquidity for a dealership to support debt servicing on a timely basis. These include unutilised bank lines, cash balances, liquid investments, and credit standing of promoters or corporate group together with a track record of support in the past. CareEdge Ratings studies profiles of owners and the corporate group to assess resourcefulness of the group, which assumes significance in situations such as funding of a new showroom or fund infusion in instances of tight liquidity. The group’s presence in other dealerships would suggest a diversified cash flow stream across the group. Primary source of liquidity, although will have to be quantum of operating profits against quantum of debt servicing annually during the forecast period. 

# **Enverionmental, social and governance (ESG) risks** 

In its overall credit rating framework, CareEdge Ratings, analyses critical ESG risks and their impact on credit profiles of entities. CareEdge Ratings analyses materiality of ESG risk factors and mitigating factors, if any, being implemented by the entity. 

**Environment risks-** While sales activities of a dealership do not involve polluting activity, repair and service operations entail replacement of engine or gear box oil in the vehicles and replacement of oil and air filters on a regular basis. Auto dealers have to ensure the use of environment-friendly refrigerant gas, while topping up or recharging gas in the auto air-conditioners. CareEdge Ratings notes that the auto OEMs generally set standards for service activities undertaken by the dealer; however, it is up to the dealer to ensure that these standards are being adhered to.

**Social risks-** Auto dealers have to ensure safe working conditions for their staff (particularly those employed in the service centres). They also have to comply with labour laws and provide adequate safety for workers to ensure longterm sustainability of the business. 

**Governance risks-** CareEdge Ratings looks at the composition of board of directors and track record of legal and statutory compliance by its management or directors to understand and assess corporate governance levels. Quality of disclosure related to financial statements, related party transactions, and transparency in sharing information with stakeholders are also helpful to gauge corporate governance practices. 

# **Conclusion** 

Considering intrinsic linkage between auto dealerships and the auto industry, fortunes of auto dealerships are largely driven by performance of the auto industry in general and that of the OEM in particular. Assessment of factors enumerated above related to an individual dealer would also form a key input in the rating process. The final rating would be an overall assessment of all aspects of the borrower, including business, financial, and management risk. CareEdge Ratings analyses each of these factors and their linkages to arrive at the overall assessment of credit quality of an issuer based on its holistic judgement.
