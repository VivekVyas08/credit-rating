### **ANALYST CONTACTS** 

This rating methodology updates and supersedes ICRA's earlier methodology document on this subject, published in February 2023. While the revised version incorporates a few modifications, ICRA's overall approach to rating security receipts (SRs) remains materially similar. 

This rating methodology discusses the approach that ICRA adopts to assign recovery ratings (RR) to SRs issued against non-performing assets (NPAs). It highlights the main factors impacting the redemption of SRs and explains how they are incorporated by ICRA in its analysis while assigning ratings to these instruments. 

# **Overview** 

Trading in NPAs commenced with the formation of special purpose entities commonly referred to as asset reconstruction companies (ARCs). These entities are established based on the benefits of enabling legislation like the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002 (SARFAESI), which provides special powers to creditors to enforce the securities held by them. 

The preferred route for transactions involving distressed assets has been to float a trust with the ARC serving as the trustee or asset manager. The trust holds the NPAs acquired by the ARC and issues SRs against the same to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs). In certain instances, the selling bank or financial institution (FI) could itself be the investor in the SR. The SRs are required to be valued at their net asset value (NAV) declared by the trust that has issued the SRs.

As per the Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) regulations, the valuation of SRs is linked to the RR assigned to these instruments by an authorised credit rating agency. As per regulations, the SRs must be rated within a period of six months from the date of their issuance and then reviewed twice a year. The RR is based on the assessment of the net present value (NPV) of the recovery expected from the underlying NPAs visà-vis the face value of SR outstanding. SRs do not have any defined payment schedule and payments are made to SR investors from the recovery proceeds as per a predetermined cashflow waterfall. Thus, while assessing the RR, the NPV of all payments that the SR investor might expect over the life of the instrument<sup>1</sup> is considered. As per regulations, the declared NAV would be within the recovery band as defined by the assigned rating of the instrument (refer to Annexure 1 for the recovery rating scale).

# **Rating Methodology** 

Assigning a recovery rating involves two broad elements: 

## **I. Assessing collections from the underlying NPAs acquired by the trust** 

   - Evaluation of the possible resolution strategies and the expected recovery in terms of quantum and timelines 

- **II. Assessing recovery on SRs:** 

   - Estimation of the likely appropriation of the recovery proceeds among the multiple claimants based on their relative position in the hierarchy of claims 

   - Assessment of the recovery for the various tranches of SRs, given their relative seniority in claims on the recovery proceeds (as defined in the cash flow waterfall for the trust) and the defined payouts (for instance, management/trustee fees and recovery incentives if any). However, the SR’s rating does not incorporate any view on the other resolution costs and expenses likely to be incurred in the future.  These expenses are eventually paid out of the trust funds and hence are to the account of the investors in the SRs 

The broad elements mentioned above are discussed in greater detail in the following sections: 

- **I. Assessing collections from the underlying NPAs acquired by the trust** 

The rating of SRs entails the assessment of the recovery from the underlying NPAs acquired by the trust. The analysis takes into account the potential resolution strategy: asset sale, restructuring/relief with the borrowing entity continuing to be operational, negotiated settlement with existing promoters, change of management, etc. In this context, ICRA analyses the likely outcome of the proposed resolution strategy, i.e. the expected amount of collections and the timing of the same, as well as the uncertainty associated with the whole process. 

NPAs are the debt obligations of distressed businesses and are already in default for varying lengths of time. The financial health of such borrowers (or the degree of distress) is highly variable. Besides, a class of wilful defaulters also exists. The analysis of the recovery from NPAs, therefore, depends on the following: 

- Quality and liquidity of security or collateral 

- Assessment of financial position and business prospects of the borrowing entity and the quality of its management 

- Share of the ARC in the total debt outstanding of the borrower (debt aggregation), whether the promoter/other lenders are cooperating in the recovery process 

Based on this assessment, ARCs evaluate various possible resolution strategies, including operational-cum-financial restructuring, management change, infusion of additional funds by the ARC and the promoter or other lenders, and sale of assets of the borrowing entity. Broadly, there are three debt resolution approaches: 

- One-time settlement (OTS) approach 

- Liquidation approach 

- Going concern approach 

## **OTS approach** 

In many instances, the borrowing entity/borrower itself makes a one-time settlement (OTS) offer to the lenders/ARC. An OTS offer could be for the payment of all the money upfront or over a period of time, in accordance with a defined payment schedule. The other two approaches are used under different circumstances. There could also be a combination of both, wherein a part of the business or assets of the entity are sold to reduce the debt. If the promoter is not cooperating, then the ARC has the option to file a petition under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC), which aims to achieve the resolution of distressed assets in a timebound manner. 

# **Liquidation approach** 

The liquidation approach is generally used when the borrower’s business is no longer viable. The business may not be able to generate positive cash flows on a sustainable basis because of management failure, product obsolescence, adverse regulatory change, competitive scenario or some other factors. Typically, in such a case, the lenders concerned would be able to make a higher recovery if the entity’s assets were to be liquidated instead of the entity being revived. 

ICRA’s approach for assessment of the liquidation value takes into account the estimated value of the underlying collateral for the debt<sup>_2_</sup> , ease of liquidation, and the estimated time for the realisation of the recovery proceeds. Relevant adjustments/haircuts may then be taken, depending on the time of valuation and the nature of the underlying security (for example, value of plant & machinery may decline from the latest available valuation report owing to depreciation or lack of maintenance), to arrive at the recovery estimate. Varying degrees of comfort (amount and recovery timeframe) can be drawn from different types of securities. In the Indian context, recovery from fixed assets (like land) is usually better compared to current assets or machinery/equipment. Similarly, well-maintained industrial assets are usually easier to sell through buyout deals. 

Under the liquidation approach, recovery is usually the quickest if the borrower cooperates with the lenders in the recovery process. Under forced sale realisation, the recovery can happen through the SARFAESI route or through Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT). The recovery time under the legal route reduces as the extent of debt aggregation goes up (for example, consent from 60% of the secured lenders is required for taking SARFAESI action against the entity concerned). Recovery can be a timeconsuming process if the entity’s debt is fragmented across multiple lenders that are not cooperating with each other in the recovery process. Delays on account of legal issues related to the property or with the borrower could also result in a protracted recovery process. The adoption of IBC has provided ARCs/lenders with another mechanism for the recovery process – in the event a resolution plan in not successful, liquidation can be ordered under IBC. 

# **Going concern approach** 

The going concern approach is used when the ARC/lenders are of the view that the entity concerned can be revived. Typically, in such a case, recovery from the entity as a going concern is expected to be higher than that through liquidation. 

As per ICRA’s approach, the recovery assessment involves the evaluation of the business prospects of the borrower entity and its financial profile, post the proposed restructuring.   The assessment is carried out to determine the recovery prospects for the debt in terms of the amount as well as the timing. The historical context is also important: the reasons for the entity’s default, efforts made in the past to restructure/revive the entity, and the extent of success/failure of these efforts. Another critical input in assessing recovery through this route is the borrowing entity’s need for fresh funds (especially working capital financing) and its ability to tie up these funds.

For some cases, specifically at the time of the initial rating, it may happen that the lenders/ARC are yet to finalise a plan for various reasons, including pending negotiations with the borrower. If more than one approach is possible, ICRA evaluates the possible resolution strategies and rates the SRs based on the most likely scenario. 

# **II. Assessing recovery on SRs:** 

Analysis for recovery for SRs is done as below: 

- Liabilities of the borrowing entity and recovery accruing to the trust 

- SR structure and cash flow waterfalls 

# **Liabilities of the borrowing entity and recovery to the trust** 

An entity may have various forms of liabilities: statutory liabilities, secured creditors, trade creditors and other forms of unsecured creditors, subordinated debt, convertible bonds, and preference shares. The claim of each class of creditors, on the enterprise assets, has been laid down by law. 

ICRA’s assessment includes a review of all the liabilities of the borrowing entity, key covenants of the loan facilities parked in the trust issuing the SRs, and the terms of any arrangement arrived at between the borrower and the lenders based on the information available. This determines the likely recovery for the loans parked in a particular trust. The recovery could be constrained by the security rights/seniority/amount of the lender’s claim even if the recoverability from the collateral, or otherwise, is higher. For instance, if the recovery from the sale of collateral is estimated at Rs. 10 crore, while the trust’s claim, after factoring in its seniority in the waterfall is Rs. 8 crore, the collections that will be considered for assessing the recovery will be Rs. 8 crore. 

# **SR structure and cash flow waterfall** 

Distribution/payments to SR investors are made only if the proceeds are actually received by the trust/ARC. There is no predefined payment schedule for SR investors. In this context, while assigning the RR, ICRA considers the cash flow waterfall defined in the transaction documents. Resolution expenses and trustee fees are generally senior to the payouts to the SR investors. Sometimes, the cash flow waterfall allows a recovery incentive payable to the ARC, which can also be senior to the redemption of the SRs. At times, a single trust can issue multiple series of SRs, which may have different priority of payment. Accordingly, the recovery estimates for multiple series of SRs may be different. 

The RR incorporates all payments expected to be made to the SR investors over a period starting from the time of the rating and ending with the expected maturity _3_ of the SRs or the five years (extendable to eight-years) period starting from the date of acquisition<sup>4</sup> , whichever is earlier. 

## **Key determinants for likely recovery from NPAs** 

- Resolution strategy and likelihood of success 

- Nature of collateral and ease of recoverability 

- Promoter cooperation in the recovery process 

- Extent of debt aggregation and cooperation from other lenders of the entity 

- Recovery steps initiated in the past by the ARC/seller bank and the current status 

- Extent of statutory dues and contingent liabilities (based on available data) 

- Impending legal hurdles, if any

The RR indicates the present value (PV) of the anticipated recovery divided by the face value of the SRs outstanding (net of any redemption). SRs do not carry any promised yield to the investor. ICRA uses a discount rate of 9% p.a. to arrive at the PV of the anticipated recoveries. The discount rate assumed by ICRA is the same across all transactions and across time periods to enable a like-to-like comparison of the recovery rate estimates. The uncertainty regarding the recovery amount and the timing is incorporated in the recovery assumptions itself, with the use of a constant discount rate aimed at minimising the estimation bias arising from forming a view on the risk-free rate. 

## **Surveillance/Monitoring of RR** 

Rating reviews (reaffirmation, upgrade, or downgrade) are undertaken at least twice every year (as mandated by the RBI regulations) or more frequently if the situation so warrants. 

Some factors that can impact the rating of SRs at the time of surveillance are as follows: 

- Any significant development not envisaged at the time of the initial rating/previous surveillance exercise (for example, an OTS offer made by the borrower or an external investor showing interest in buying out the debt from the lenders) 

- Progress made in the recovery process vis-à-vis what was expected at the time of initial rating/previous surveillance exercise (for example, delay in taking possession or sale of the property, or adverse or favourable court rulings). As the rating considers the discounted value of the estimated recovery relative to the face value of the SRs outstanding, the rating may not be impacted even in case of a delay in recovery (from the initial estimate) if the discounted value of the estimated recovery is still within the balance allowable period for the recovery of the SRs and the recovery band allowed by the rating 

- Actual realisation of recovery proceeds being different from what was envisaged earlier 

- Part redemption of the SRs 

- Macroeconomic and regulatory factors impacting the business of the borrower or the valuation of the collateral security 

# **Environmental and Social Risks** 

While SRs do not face direct physical climate risks, they are exposed to environmental risks indirectly through the underlying pool of entities which are part of the SR. If the entities which are a part of the SR, face business disruptions because of physical climate adversities, or if they face climate transition risks because of technological, regulatory, or customer behaviour changes, it could translate into recovery risks for the SRs. Likewise, the exposure of SRs to social risks depends on the nature of the sectors and/ or entities in the SR. 

# **Summary** 

This methodology document explains the general considerations which are applicable in most SR transactions; however, depending upon the nuances for each transaction, other principles may be incorporated to effectively evaluate the risk associated with the transaction. The approach used by ICRA involves evaluation of the possible resolution strategies, assessing the recovery for various tranches in the SRs based on the seniority of claims and the expected recovery in terms of quantum and timelines for the trust.  The assumptions for a particular rating may change from time-to-time based on changes in the SARFAESI act, IBC, RBI regulations, market or economic conditions, and resolution strategies.

# **Annexure I: ICRA’s Recovery Rating Scale** 

ICRA uses the following scale for assigning RRs to SRs: 

|**Recovery**<br>**Rating**|**Implied**<br>**Recovery**|**Rating Definition**|
|---|---|---|
|RR1+|> 150%|The rating of RR1+ indicates that the present value of anticipated recoveries<br>is more than 150% of the face value outstanding of the SRs.|
|RR1|100% - 150%|The rating of RR1 indicates that the present value of anticipated recoveries<br>is in the range of 100-150% of the face value outstanding of the SRs.|
|RR2|75% - 100%|The rating of RR2 indicates that the present value of anticipated recoveries<br>is in the range of 75-100% of the face value outstanding of the SRs.|
|RR3|50% - 75%|The rating of RR3 indicates that the present value of anticipated recoveries<br>is in the range of 50-75% of the face value outstanding of the SRs.|
|RR4|25% - 50%|The rating of RR4 indicates that the present value of anticipated recoveries<br>is in the range of 25-50% of the face value outstanding of the SRs.|
|RR5|0% - 25%|The rating of RR5 indicates that the present value of anticipated recoveries<br>is in the range of 0-25% of the face value outstanding of the SRs.|



The face value of the SRs or the purchase consideration of the loans parked in the trust is, in most cases, lower than the claim outstanding against these loans. Therefore, it is possible for the present value of the anticipated recovery to be higher than the face value of the SRs. This is adequately captured by the upper end of the rating scale.
