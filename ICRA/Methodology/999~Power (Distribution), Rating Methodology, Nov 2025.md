### **ANALYST CONTACTS** 

This rating methodology describes ICRA’s approach to assess the credit risk of power distribution utilities. It aims to help issuers, investors and other market participants understand ICRA’s approach to analysing risks that are likely to affect the rating outcomes of the distribution utilities. This rating methodology updates and supersedes ICRA's earlier methodology document on this subject, published in November 2023. While this revised version incorporates a few additional clarifications and editorial changes, ICRA's overall approach to rating entities in the sector remains materially similar. 

# **Overview** 

The Indian power sector is governed by the Electricity Act of 2003, the enactment of which ushered in significant structural changes in the sector – such as reorganisation of the erstwhile State Electricity Boards (SEBs) on functional lines, de-licensing of thermal generation and grant of open access in transmission and distribution (T&D) with the intent of introducing wholesale competition. Post unbundling of the erstwhile SEBs, the successor entities have been constituted in most states along functional lines, i.e. for generation (GENCO), distribution (DISCOM) and transmission (TRANSCO). These entities are governed by the State Electricity Regulatory Commissions (SERCs) at the state level and the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) at the national level. In addition, the Act also provided for the constitution of the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL) as an independent authority to hear appeals against the orders issued by the CERC/SERCs. 

The power distribution utilities (discoms) supply electricity to consumers in a specified geographic area under the distribution licence granted by the SERC. The SERCs also determine tariffs for supply by discoms to the various consumers. The power distribution segment in India is predominantly state-owned, with privateowned distribution utilities catering to consumers in a few urban areas such as Mumbai, Kolkata, Noida, Delhi, Ahmedabad, Surat and all of Odisha.

This in turn affects the payment cycle of the discoms , resulting in payment delays to the power-generating companies. The Ministry of Power on June 3, 2022 notified the Electricity (Late Payment Surcharge and Related Matters) Rules, 2022, with the objective of instilling payment discipline in discoms, while also providing them adequate time to clear the pending dues.

# **Industry Risk Drivers** 

### **Regulatory Risk** 

Given the regulated nature of the business, the distribution utilities are governed by the cost-plus based tariff determination process by the SERCs. Under the cost-plus tariff principles, the power purchase cost, operating costs (employee, operation and maintenance (O&M) and general and administrative (G&A)) and capital costs (interest and depreciation) along with regulatory returns on equity for the discoms  are passed through to the consumers, subject to certain operating efficiency targets being met. Therefore, the transparency, predictability and consistency of the regulatory process have a key influence on the cash flow of any entity operating in a regulated environment. The availability of a multi-year tariff (MYT) regulatory framework, along with the timely issuance of tariff orders by the SERCs for a pre-defined control period (typically three or five years) and timely true-up for previous years is viewed favourably. The true-up process involves review of the actual performance (operating efficiency and cost parameters) against the earlier approved levels in the MYT order. Further, the implementation of the fuel and power purchase cost adjustment (FPPCA) framework would enable the discoms to pass on the variations in power purchase cost to the consumers in a timely manner (monthly or quarterly). Under the MYT regulations, the utility is required to submit a tariff petition<sup>2</sup> inclusive of provisional/final<sup>3</sup> true-up petition for the previous years, against which the SERCs issue a tariff order. # **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Operating Efficiency** 

**AT&C losses:** The most important efficiency parameter for a distribution licensee is the ability to keep the AT&C<sup>4</sup> losses in line with the regulatory targets set by the SERCs<sup>5</sup> . The AT&C loss, besides the distribution loss (technical losses, theft and billing losses), incorporates the collection efficiency. 

The focus here is to assess the track record and ability of the utility to keep the AT&C loss level in line with the regulatory target. The progress towards lowering the AT&C loss level remains linked to achieving metering at all levels, augmenting the distribution infrastructure, strict implementation of anti-theft laws, use of information technology (IT) systems and separation of agriculture and non-agriculture feeders. The tariffs approved by the SERCs consider a specific performance level on AT&C losses. Any negative variation in AT&C loss level against the regulatory target leads to an under-recovery of power purchase cost, which in turn has an adverse impact on the cash flows and returns for the utility. On the other hand, a lower-thanapproved loss level would lead to gains for the utility. The Union Government, through the RDSS scheme, is incentivising the state discoms to replace conventional meters with smart meters, with the objective to reduce AT&C losses.

# **Timeliness and Adequacy of Tariff Determination** 

ICRA does an assessment of the track record in timeliness of tariff determination by the SERC for the utility and adequacy of tariff in relation to the supply cost for the utility. In case of any revenue deficit at the approved tariff level, ICRA reviews the amortisation trajectory approved by the SERC for recovery of the deficit. While such revenue gaps are necessitated by the SERCs to avoid any tariff shock to the consumers, it leads to dependence on borrowings by utilities to fund such unrecovered costs (RA). As a result, time-bound recovery of such RA in line with the trajectory suggested by APTEL as well as the Supreme Court (i.e. over a 3-4-year period, although longer recovery periods have been observed in a few states) remains critical from the credit perspective. Further, any delays in the tariff determination process (which can be attributed to either delays in tariff petition filing by utility or issuance of tariff order by SERC) or non-implementation (or roll-back by state government) of either the tariff order or the fuel and power purchase cost adjustment framework is a credit concern for the distribution utilities, given that it has an adverse impact on the cash flows of the utilities. 

**FPPCA framework:** Given that the cost of power purchase is an uncontrollable<sup>6</sup> cost variable and accounts for 75-80% of the cost of supply of a distribution utility, a regular pass-through of variation in power purchase cost through tariff remains crucial for the utility’s financial health. The implementation of the FPPCA framework enables the discoms  to recover such cost variations through tariff in a timely manner. ICRA assesses the availability and adherence to the FPPCA framework, given that the application of the framework is either absent or is being done with a time lag across most states. 

**Political willingness and track record to hike tariffs:** Traditionally, political willingness and proximity to the elections has also played a role in timely filing of tariff petitions by the utilities and the subsequent issuance of tariff orders approving tariff hikes by the SERCs. ICRA assesses the track record of the state government to enable the timely issuance of tariff orders and allowing tariff hikes. Regular tariff revisions, in line with the changing cost structure of the utilities, are considered a positive from a credit perspective. 

## **Cost Coverage Ratio** 

The key parameter for assessing the tariff adequacy of a distribution utility is the cost coverage ratio, which measures the coverage of its costs by the cash collections from the consumers and the subsidy payments received from the state government. While ICRA factors in the subsidy support received from the state government in the cost coverage ratio, an excessive dependence on such subsidy support is a negative from the rating perspective, given the concerns over sustainability and timeliness of subsidy inflows.

# **Consumer Profile** 

The consumer segment mix in the licence area of a distribution utility determines the quality of cash flows, distribution loss levels<sup>7</sup> , level of subsidy support required from the state government as well as the extent of a likely threat of competition from captive sources or competition from third-party, open-access sources. The discoms  having a consumer mix with a higher proportion of commercial and industrial (C&I) customers are viewed favorably, as it indicates better paying ability, higher metering and collection efficiency and lower reliance on subsidies, unlike a higher agricultural share which often implies greater subsidy burden and higher distribution losses. The key aspects that form an analysis of the consumer segment mix are as follows: 

**Extent of agricultural consumption** : Given the subsidised tariff rates or free supply prevalent in many states for the agriculture segment, a higher proportion of sales to agricultural segment inevitably implies greater burden of subsidy payment on the state government, as well as relatively higher levels of cross-subsidisation of tariffs charged to C&I category consumers, which in turn leads to increased risk of C&I consumers shifting to open-access procurement. Moreover, a larger share of agriculture consumption in a licence area reflects a greater mix of unmetered sales, which in turn leads to high distribution loss levels, as seen for utilities in some states. 

**C&I consumers:** A higher proportion of C&I consumers in comparison with other consumer segments in the overall consumer mix for a distribution utility is viewed favourably, given that these consumer segments generally enable high metering, billing and collection efficiency for the utilities along with better prospects for timely pass-through of changes in cost structure and low dependence on subsidy support from the state government. On the other hand, DISCOMs remain vulnerable to competition from third-party power producers supplying through open access to C&I consumers or through rooftop solar projects, especially in view of the significant reduction in the cost of solar and wind power generation. ICRA reviews the steps being taken by the discoms to minimise the impact of high-paying consumers switching from the incumbent licensee to openaccess procurement. For instance, utilities in some states have provided incentives to High Tension (HT) industrial consumers through time-of-day tariffs as well as special rebate schemes. Also, the cross-subsidy surcharge approved by the SERCs for levy on open-access procurement is witnessing a rising trend with the increase in the cost of supply. Further, the SERCs in a few states have approved the levy of additional surcharge on such open-access procurement, citing stranded cost of power

# **Support from State Government** 

Power supply is an integral function of the state government, and the extent of funding support offered by the state government to the state power utilities, along with intrinsic credit quality of the state government is analysed while assessing the credit quality of the state-owned distribution utilities. A high dependence on state government support results in the credit profile of the utility being driven by the state’s own credit profile, while the weak financial position of the power distribution utilities can be a constraining factor in the credit profile of the state government itself. The rating of a distribution utility, however, may not necessarily be constrained by the rating of its respective state government. The utility’s rating may be higher than that of the state government’s rating based on the operational and the financial strength of the utility at a standalone level, when the utility has limited dependence on subsidy receipts from the state government and when the state government’s actions are not expected to financially weaken it. 

The state government’s support to power distribution utilities is primarily through subsidy payments in lieu of subsidised supply to certain sections of consumers, loss funding to support the state discoms as agreed under the various schemes of the Government of India and in the form of equity and grants for the various capex programmes. 

**Subsidy support:** As certain sections of the consumers – mainly agriculture consumers or the below poverty line (BPL) section of residential/rural consumers – are provided power at highly subsidised rates or free of cost, the distribution utilities remain dependent on the subsidy support from their respective state governments to compensate for the cost incurred under such supplies. ICRA evaluates the timeliness and adequacy of subsidy support from the state government to the distribution utilities, given that any delay is likely to impact the liquidity position of these entities. High dependence on subsidies from a state government with a relatively weak credit profile is not considered favourable for the overall financial health of the entity. 

This apart, ICRA assesses the willingness and the proactive steps taken by the governments in terms of implementation of reforms, which in turn plays a key role in improving the performance of the DISCOMs.

# **Financial Risk Drivers** 

## **Adequacy of Future Cash Flows** 

Since the prime objective of the rating exercise is to assess the adequacy of the company’s debt-servicing capability, ICRA draws up projections on the likely financial position of the company, in addition to reviewing the past financials. ICRA draws up cash flow projections to assess the adequacy of a utility’s cash flows in relation to the debt-servicing obligations. The key variables that have an impact on a utility’s debt-servicing ability are a) adequacy of tariff in relation to cost of supply, b) ability to achieve the AT&C loss targets specified by the SERC and c) adequacy and timeliness of subsidy release by the state government. Future cash flows are projected based on the tariff structure approved by the SERC, expected demand growth in the licence area of the utility, power purchase arrangements, subsidy requirements, O&M cost, interest cost, debt repayment schedule, working capital funding requirements, capex and the available funding options. These cash flows enable the formation of an opinion on the entity’s future debt-servicing capability. 

ICRA evaluates the financial position of a distribution utility as reflected by its profitability, capitalisation and debt-servicing indicators and overall liquidity profile. ICRA also carries out a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of the key variables such as the extent of tariff revision in relation to the changes in cost structure and AT&C losses on the various financial metrics. 

## **Profitability** 

The profitability for a distribution utility can be influenced by factors such as the adequacy of the tariff approved in relation to the cost of supply, performance on the AT&C loss parameter against the target approved by the regulator and ability of the utility to keep the other operating and capital costs within the regulator-approved levels. From a rating perspective, both the level as well as the stability in profitability metrics matter. A consistent track record of higher profitability shown by an entity compared with its peers reflects a superior ability to pass on the cost variations through tariffs and to keep the operating efficiencies and costs within the benchmarks approved by the regulator. 

The various ratios which are typically used by ICRA to analyse an entity’s profitability are presented in the table below. ICRA reviews the return metrics for the entity, like return on capital employed (RoCE) to assess the profitability of a utility. A subdued RoCE indicates that either the utility is not meeting the normative operating efficiency norms or there is a delay in pass-through of cost variations to consumers. Subsequently, this lag is impacts the ability of the utility to generate the regulatory returns under the cost-plus tariff regime. RoCE for distribution companies has been modest despite the regulated cost-plus framework intended to ensure stable returns. While private utilities generally achieve better profitability due to lower AT&C losses and timely cost recovery, state-owned discoms  often struggle with delays in tariff revisions, under-recovery of power purchase costs and mounting RA—factors that significantly compress returns. Sustained improvement will depend on cost-reflective tariffs, efficient operations and stronger regulatory discipline.

# **Leverage and Coverage Indicators** 

Financial leverage is a measure of an entity’s dependence on borrowed funds. The lower the dependence on borrowings, the lower (better) the leverage. When an entity borrows, it is obliged to pay both interest as well as principal to the lenders as per a defined schedule. This increases the fixed cost burden on the borrowing entity and in the limiting case, raises the default risk. The leverage level for a distribution utility remains dependent upon the extent of capex and working capital requirement, including the borrowings to meet the unrecovered revenue gap, if any. Given that the AT&C loss level for utilities in many of the large states is above 20% and the fact that progress in loss reduction has remained limited so far, capex plans for utilities remain sizeable. The key focus of capex is towards system-strengthening schemes with the main objective of reduction in the distribution loss levels and to meet the energy demand of a growing consumer base in the licence areas. The major sources of funding are consumer contribution<sup>8</sup> , equity from the state governments, internal accruals, funding available under various schemes notified by the Government of India and long-term debt from banks and financial institutions. While the normative debt-to-equity ratio of 70:30 (after adjusting for grants) is allowed by the SERC for funding of capex in tariff estimation, ICRA factors in the support available from the central/state governments for partial funding of such capex schemes, which is either in the form of a grant or soft loans over a longer maturity or equity capital. 

Coverage is a measure of an entity’s debt-servicing ability and is calculated as the ratio of profits to the debt-servicing obligations in a given time period. Utilities with healthy coverage ratios have a higher cushion in terms of meeting the debtservicing obligations. The debt coverage indicators that are examined include interest coverage ratio and debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). The ratios typically used to measure a company’s leverage and coverage metrics are:

# **Liquidity** 

Liquidity is the measure of an entity’s ability to meet its short-term cash obligations from various internal or external resources. For a distribution utility, internal resources include cash flows from sale of power to consumers, subsidy payments from the Government and free cash balances. External resources include undrawn lines of credit, Government grants or equity capital. The short-term obligations include power purchase payments, operating costs, debt-servicing obligations, working capital requirements and capex. The higher the cushion available between the resources available (especially internal resources) and the obligations, better is the liquidity profile of an entity. # **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Financial Flexibility** 

An entity’s financial flexibility (or the lack thereof) is reflected in its ability to access the capital or the money markets at short notice, attract diverse and marquee investors and enjoy the confidence of banks, financial institutions and intermediaries. A strong financial flexibility allows an entity to raise fresh borrowings or refinance existing ones in quick time and whenever required. Financial flexibility for utilities could arise from factors such as an entity's large scale of operations with strong financials, long tenure of the licence period or linkages with the state government in case of state-owned utilities/strong parentage for private utilities. 

In contrast, among the various measures of an entity’s depleting financial flexibility, one relates to a high share of pledged promoter shareholding. A sign such as this may imply that the entity might be persuaded to distribute high dividends or support the promoter group through other means to the detriment of its own credit profile. If the promoters fail to repay their loans (availed by pledging of shares) or top up collateral when required, the lenders could sell the pledged shares. In some cases, this could trigger a change-of-control clause in the rated entity’s bond indentures or loan documents and require it to redeem its debt ahead of schedule, creating a liquidity squeeze, besides affecting fresh capital-raising ability. Financial flexibility could also be adversely impacted by industry or regulatory developments, weakening business profile or management & governance concerns, either at the entity or the group level. These could translate into a sharp decline in market capitalisation or spike in bond yields and consequently constrain an entity’s ability to raise fresh capital or materially increase its cost of capital. 

## **Tenure Mismatches and Risks Relating to Refinancing** 

ICRA evaluates the dependence on short-term borrowings to fund power purchase costs or long-term investments, which can impact the overall debt profile of a utility and expose it to significant refinancing risks, especially during periods of tight systemic liquidity. ICRA evaluates the extent of such mismatches and the mitigating factors therein. The existence of adequate buffers of liquid assets/ bank lines to meet short-term obligations is viewed positively. Another source of mitigation could be the entity’s strong financial flexibility to garner fresh funds at a short notice or a potent ability to refinance. 

## **Foreign Currency-related Risks** 

The foreign currency risk can arise from unhedged foreign currency liabilities pertaining to funding of capex and/or working capital. The focus here is on assessing the hedging policy of the issuer concerned in the context of the company’s entire cash flows being denominated in the domestic currency. ICRA’s analytical focus is on assessing the magnitude of such exposure, relative to the entity’s profits. Hedging, if not for the entire tenure of debt, then at least for the near-term foreign exchange repayment commitments, is viewed positively. 

## **Accounting Quality** 

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts and auditors’ comments that are part of the annual report of the rated entity. Any deviation from the accounting standards is noted and the financial statements of the entity are adjusted, where feasible, to reflect the impact of such deviations. Significant deviations may be indicative of weak corporate governance practices in the entity. 

## **Contingent Liabilities/Off-balance Sheet Exposures** 

The likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/off-balance sheet exposures and the financial implications of the same are evaluated, while factoring the mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion.

## **Event Risks** 

ICRA recognises the possibility of events such as mergers and acquisitions, litigations, equity infusion and refinancing, which could have a material impact on the credit profile of an entity. Incorporating the impact of such discrete events in the credit rating, from the beginning, is often difficult. Depending on whether and when such events occur, the rating opinion could be substantially different. To take rating decisions in such cases, ICRA applies its analytical judgment based on the rated entity’s track record, the credibility of the management and the experience of having seen similar situations played out in other entities. However, given the nature of such events, it is possible that the rating may undergo a material change later, upon the occurrence of the event. 

## **Parentage** 

While the credit rating of an entity is a function of its standalone credit profile, in certain cases, the entity’s credit quality can also be driven by the relationship with its parent or the promoter group (henceforth referred to as the parent). If the parent’s credit profile is relatively stronger than the rated entity, ICRA assesses the ability and the likelihood of the parent extending extraordinary support to the entity. Support here means financial support from the parent expected to be available to the entity in the form of loans, equity, etc. in times of credit or liquidity stress on the entity. ‘Support’ does not mean the ongoing or committed financial support from the parent, such as the capital infusion to fund the rated entity’s expansion plans or support to maintain a targeted capital structure, but the extraordinary support which cannot be foreseen, is intended to avoid a default and thus cannot be factored in while assessing the notional standalone rating of the entity. In case of the state government-owned utilities, ICRA takes note of the high strategic importance of these utilities to the state government as these provide a public service of high importance and are seen as an extended arm of the Government. Further, ICRA considers the past track record of the state government in extending timely support to the utilities, while uplifting the rating based on the parent support. 

# **Management Quality Assessment** 

In addition to the industry, business and financial risk analysis, all credit ratings incorporate an assessment of the quality of the rated entity’s management and the financial policies. 

## **Quality of Management and Financial Policies** 

As a part of its process, ICRA undertakes discussions with the rated entity’s management to understand its views on past performance as well as its future plans and strategies, besides the outlook on the industry. Some of the points assessed are: 

- Experience of the promoter/management in the line of business concerned 

- Commitment of the promoter/management to the rated entity 

- Risk appetite of the promoter/management and risk mitigation plans 

- Policies on leveraging, managing interest rates and currency risks 

- Management’s past success in introducing new projects and managing changes in the external environment 

- Management’s plans on new projects, acquisitions, expansion, etc. 

- Track record of balancing the interests of shareholders, creditors and other stakeholders 

Periodic interactions with the management help in ascertaining the shifts, if any, in the issuer’s financial policies.

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environment (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the subcomponents of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, but their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence, these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or carbon transition risks, such as those arising from changes in regulations or other environmental and social risks and seeks to understand the various mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks. 

Power distribution utilities in India meet a major portion of their power procurement from fossil fuel-based sources that tend to have an adverse impact on the environment and society at large. Amid the policy, demographic and societal trends in favour of clean energy, the distribution utilities are focussed on increasing the share of power procurement from solar and wind energy sources. This is also supported by the improving competitiveness of tariff offered by these sources. However, given the intermittent nature of the solar and wind sources, the higher share of supply from these sources would necessitate the need to maintain sufficient balancing reserves by the discoms at an additional cost, till the cost of energy storage becomes economical. While the discoms would be able to claim such balancing cost under the regulatory mechanism, it could adversely impact the cost competitiveness of the tariffs offered by them. Distribution networks remain essential regardless of the power source, and carbon transition does not eliminate their inherent role. 

This apart, the discoms face social risks in the form of resistance against tariff hikes from the political establishments and the public, especially from the household and agriculture consumer segments. This manifests as instances of deferment or rollback of the tariff hikes, thereby adversely impacting the profitability and cash flows of the discoms.

The composition of an entity’s Board, its involvement in strategic decision-making and the entity’s compliance with the legal and regulatory requirements are factored in during credit assessments. # **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the company’s business and financial risks, likely cash flows over the life of the instrument being rated and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis its debt-servicing obligations. 

As this note highlights, the credit quality of a distribution utility is dependent upon several factors, including the timeliness and adequacy of the tariff determination process, adequacy of subsidy support from the state government and the ability to maintain operating efficiency and cost structure in line with regulatory targets. ICRA notes that the credit quality of stateowned distribution utilities also typically remains linked with that of the concerned state government, given the significant dependence on state government support by virtue of the subsidy dependence and the strategic importance of the power sector to the state government.
