# **Overview** 

Over the past two decades, several road projects have been executed through the Public Private Partnership (PPP) mode under the Built-Operate-Transfer (BOT) model, through the BOT-Toll, BOT-Annuity or BOT-Hybrid Annuity Mode (HAM) basis<sup>1</sup> . This note provides an overview of ICRA’s methodology for rating BOT-Annuity road projects. These road projects are implemented through project-specific special purpose vehicles (SPVs) whose main source of revenue is the annuity payment from the project owner<sup>2</sup> , which is used to meet the contractual obligations, including operations and maintenance (O&M) expenses (routine and major maintenance), statutory dues and debt servicing obligations. 

_<u>Key risk factors and rating considerations for BOT annuity road projects</u>_ 

From a credit perspective, many of the challenges involved in developing road projects are similar to those faced by other infrastructure projects. The key ones being execution challenges like acquisition of land, securing necessary clearances, financial closure and revenue risks. 

Under-construction BOT annuity road projects are exposed to execution risks (delays in securing right of way (ROW), MOEF<sup>3</sup> clearances, EPC contractor’s ability to timely complete the project etc), funding risks if the debt and equity are not fully tied up and interest rate risks. Although acquisition of the right of way (RoW) is the responsibility of the project owner (generally the National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) or the state road development authority), delays in timely handing over of the required right of way, post the start of construction, can result in time and cost overruns in project implementation as well as loss of annuities in case of delayed project completion. For an under-construction road project, execution and funding risks are prominent and can lead to cost and time overruns in case of delayed project completion. The likelihood of an under-construction project taking recourse to the sponsor for financial support is generally higher than in the case of an operational project. Operational annuity-based projects are exposed to operation and maintenance risks as the quantum of annuity is linked to lane availability (as per the service level requirements stated in the concession agreement), counterparty credit risk, which has a bearing on the timeliness of annuity payments, and interest rate risk if the borrowings are with a floating interest rate.

Annuity road projects undergo significant changes in their risk profile once they achieve the commercial operations date (COD). While permitting and execution risks dominate the risks in the pre-COD period, the primary risk in the post-COD period pertains to counterparty credit risk (timeliness of annuity payments) and O&M risk as the annuity payment is linked to lane availability (as per the service level requirements stated in the concession agreement). For a project with a satisfactory track record of two to three years, the risk arising from deductions in annuity due to lane unavailability or timeliness of annuity is mitigated to an extent. Further, the assumptions on lane availability can be relatively more reliable compared to a project, which has not achieved a COD as there is no history of O&M on the stretch.

The list of rating drivers covered in this methodology is not exhaustive by itself but provides an overall perspective on the rating considerations that are usually considered the most important. For analytical convenience, the key factors are grouped under the following broad heads— Business Risk Assessment, Financial Risk Assessment, Debt Structure and Sponsor Risk Assessment.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Execution Risks** 

### `o` **Permitting risk** 

A key component of the completion risk is the permitting risk, which refers to a project’s ability to acquire all statutory clearances prior to the commencement of construction activity. Typically, for a road project this would include a RoW acquisition, rehabilitation and resettlement, clearances from the MoEF, the Pollution Control Board and the Railways, and clearances for shifting all utilities that lie in the project area. The project owner usually assumes the permitting risk and undertakes to indemnify the project SPV against losses arising out of any disputes with respect to acquisition of land. The permitting risks are usually low where the project involves upgradation of an existing operating stretch. However, such risks could be significant where projects involve construction of entirely new stretches (including bypasses), where considerable amount of land needs to be acquired. 

- **Cost and time overruns** 

   - **Cost overrun:** The project cost is dependent on several variables, which include the nature of the surface (bituminous versus concrete), the number of lanes, the terrain of the project, and the number of structures (bridges, culverts, rail over bridges), which need to be constructed. In addition, ICRA looks at the variation between the authority cost estimate and the developer-determined project cost to assess the likelihood of a cost overrun in case of aggressive bidding. ICRA evaluates the vulnerability of the project to cost and time overruns, and the SPV’s financial flexibility to fund these overruns. This assumes significance, given that in such BOT projects, cost escalations need to be funded by the project company, which could affect the project’s financial leverage and hence credit risk for lenders and investors. 

   - **Time overrun:** Road construction projects often face challenges related to the mobilisation of labour, equipment and key raw material availability (aggregate, sand, etc). Further, factors such as difficult terrain, inadequate investments in construction equipment, weather and labour problems contribute to construction delays and cost escalations. 

Some of these risks are partially mitigated through fixed-price and fixed-time EPC contracts with strong counterparties with liquidated damage provisions for non-performance. In such cases, ICRA assesses the financial and operational capabilities (including past track record) of the EPC contractor, and its ability to meet the contractual commitments.

In case of the BOT road projects, the CA stipulates providing for at least 80% of the RoW before the commencement of the concession of the project. Further, there is a clause of provisional completion if at least 75% of the project stretch is completed and the remaining work is pending due to reasons attributable to the authority. Upon attaining the provisional completion certificate, the concessionaire has the right to receive annuity payments from the authority, mitigating the risk of revenue loss in case of delays in achieving 100%<sup>_4_</sup> completion. These provisions protect the concessionaire from loss of revenues on account of delays in handing over of the required RoW and mitigate the project execution risks to an extent. 

# **Funding Risks** 

The ability of the sponsors to infuse equity (promoter’s contribution) in a timely manner is assessed. The financial strength of the sponsor is a key credit determinant during the pre-COD stage, given that apart from contributing to the promoter’s share in the form of equity capital and/or subordinated debt, the sponsor is directly or indirectly responsible for ensuring the financial closure of these projects. Also, the projects are evaluated on whether interest during construction (IDC) is sufficient for the project construction period. In case of any delays in execution, the buffer available in the IDC and the extent of contingencies provided in the projects is evaluated. In cases where a part of the funding is given by the authority as grant, ICRA tries to understand the budgetary allocations/ funding sources for grant payments for assessing the timeliness of these grants. 

# **Revenue risk** 

Post completion of construction activity, annuity road projects are primarily exposed to risks associated with the O&M (which determines the lane availability) and the counterparty, which would determine the quantum and timeliness of the annuity payments from the authority. While annuity payments from the NHAI have been largely timely, a few state authorities have delayed the payments in the past. Therefore, an assessment of the financial risk profile of counterparty authority remains critical. For lesser prominent authorities with limited past track record of annuity payments, ICRA evaluates the budgetary allocations/funding sources for annuity payments for assessing the timeliness of annuity payments. ICRA’s past experience while rating state annuity road assets suggests that despite making adequate budgetary allocations, the change in priorities for the state governments during the year or constrained finances of respective states/authorities could result in delays in annuity payments. The credit profile/rating of the counterparty in annuity projects typically acts as an overall cap on the standalone rating of the project (without factoring parent or group support). 

# **Operating risk** 

<mark>The project not conforming to the required performance parameters over the period of the concession agreement exposes the project to operating risk. Timely and adequate O&M activity is crucial for a project to remain eligible for receiving the annuity payments from the authority. This risk, if not addressed, could manifest in the form of reduced revenues, higher operating costs, and increased major maintenance at a later date.</mark> 

<mark>The road is required to be maintained as per the service level requirements stated in the concession agreement. Noncompliance with the performance parameters may be an ‘event of default’ and impinge on the annuity payments from the authority. Therefore, the concessionaire’s ability to fairly assess operating expenses and lay down a proper maintenance schedule is important to avoid any annuity deductions on account of lane unavailability.</mark> 

<mark>O&M expenses for road projects are primarily of two types:</mark> 

- <mark>Periodic maintenance, which involves the relaying of the bituminous-concrete (blacktop) once every five years or as the case may be, depending on the service level requirements as per the CA.</mark>

- <mark>Routine maintenance, which involves repairing of cracks, replacement of safety girders along the highway, clearance of debris following accidents, ensuring functionality of signposts, maintenance of a security set-up, and such other activities as per the CA.</mark> 

ICRA assesses the track record and experience of the O&M contractor and studies the O&M contract to assess the responsibilities of the contractor and the mitigants available (such as liquidated damages) in case of any underperformance. Apart from evaluating the operating capabilities and the financial strength of the O&M contractor, the other aspects, which ICRA assesses while evaluating O&M risks in BOT road projects are: 

- **The mechanism by which the scheduled O&M expenses would be funded:** O&M outflows are likely to peak during the year in which major maintenance activity falls due, when the relaying of the top surface would need to be carried out. ICRA, therefore, believes that the cash flow waterfall should provide for an O&M reserve that would get built up gradually, either from the annuity receipts or through an external liquidity support mechanism (standby LCs, BGs, etc) from a third party (typically a financial institution), so that adequate funds are available for these peak outflows. 

- **Inflation sensitivity of the O&M estimates:** The O&M estimates would be highly sensitive to inflation, and would, therefore, be difficult to budget over a 20-year period. Most of these projects are likely to enter into fixed cost O&M contracts, possibly with the sponsors themselves. However, if the actual expenses exceed the budgeted costs, the incentive for the O&M contractor to maintain the road in accordance with the terms could diminish. 

ICRA’s assumptions on expenses towards O&M (including major maintenance) are based on empirical costs observed in other similar projects. Additionally, the assumptions attempt to have adequate in-built buffers to account for the expected rise in expenses (owing to inflation). 

# **Event risks** 

Given the limited ability to negotiate increased cost/revenue disruptions, the adverse effect of any event risk is more pronounced for SPVs when compared to corporate entities. Upon occurrence of events like force majeure, changes in law and concessionaire/authority event of default, the nature of the event and its impact on the cash flows is assessed. This is largely a post-facto assessment as factoring in event risks at the outset may not be entirely possible.

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

## **Stability of cash flows** 

The stability of cash flows is higher for annuity road projects when compared with toll road projects. The quantum of annuity payments from the authority critically hinges on the quality of the O&M activity – poor maintenance would result in lower lane availability (than contractually stipulated) and thereby result in annuity deductions. 

Here emphasis is laid on the predictability of cash flows and the SPV’s ability to exercise better control over operating costs. Assets whose operations are stabilised, i.e., with at least two to three years of operational track record are positioned favourably from a cash flow predictability perspective. The effectiveness of the O&M activity and lane availability track record would be established. On the expenses front, the concessionaire’s ability to manage O&M costs within the budgeted levels remains critical. 

The key sensitivity scenarios include time and cost overruns during the construction phase and variability in lane availability, lag in annuity payments, increase in O&M expenses or interest rates post completion. 

## **Interest rate and forex risks** 

Projects with fixed rate of interest are expected to have higher stability of cash flows compared to those with flexible interest rates as the annuity payments are fixed in nature and not linked to interest rate movement. The extent to which an entity would be impacted by movements in interest rates is evaluated by carrying out an interest rate sensitivity for SPVs that have floating rate borrowings. Further, higher coverage metrics for flexible interest rate projects is desired such that the buffer would remain comfortable during the periods of unfavourable rates. Similarly, the foreign currency risk can arise from unhedged liabilities, especially for entities with unhedged foreign currency borrowings, which could pertain to part-funding of the project cost. As there is limited scope for natural hedge, the focus here is on the hedging policy of the issuer to mitigate such risk for net foreign currency exposure. That said, foreign currency denominated funding is rarely seen in BOT-Annuity projects. 

## **Liquidity and debt service coverage metrics** 

<mark>Annuity road assets are usually funded with a considerable reliance on external debt. Unlike toll road projects that start generating cash flows from the COD, for annuity projects, the annuities would typically start flowing in after six months from the COD. Therefore, projects should have adequate funding – to meet the first six months O&M expenses and interest payments (if applicable). ICRA also evaluates the time gap available between the scheduled date of annuity receipt and the scheduled date of repayment.</mark> 

<mark>The Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR), minimum DSCR and cumulative DSCR are the key coverage ratios that are analysed. The DSCR measures the cushion between the debt servicing obligation and the CFADS</mark><sup>_5_</sup> <mark>in any given period (typically semiannual but may be quarterly/ yearly to match with debt repayment frequency). In addition, unencumbered cash balances and DSRA provide liquidity support and help the entity tide over any delays or deductions in annuities from the authority. ## **Inherent profitability of the projects** 

Given the highly capital-intensive nature and finite life of the road projects and low operational expenses, the emphasis is on the internal rate of return (IRR) instead of the conventional profitability indicators. Aggressive bidding leads to sub-optimal IRR eventually. The sponsors may not have an economic incentive for supporting the project with poor IRRs, in which case there is a possibility of invocation of concessionaire event of default clause and the lenders exercising their substitution rights.

# **Structural factors** 

In most cases, the BOT annuity road projects are funded through the SPVs, without a recourse to the sponsors for debt servicing. Annuity payments from the authority are the only sources of revenue available to the project SPV. Thus, apart from assessing the counterparty credit risk and O&M risk for the project, ICRA evaluates certain structural aspects of these projects. In case of funded DSRA, ICRA evaluates the replenishment mechanism of the reserve (if dipped into) from the subsequent operating cash flows. In case of DSRA guarantee, ICRA evaluates the risks associated with renewal of such guarantees. Similarly, creation of a major maintenance/capex reserve (MMR) for undertaking the scheduled major maintenance activities also supports the ratings. 

Presence of the escrow mechanism and the ring fencing of cash flows to prevent any leakage of funds are some of the structural considerations. In circumstances where the project level debt holder’s position is weakened by the existence of a weak sponsor or upstream leverage, if adequate ring-fencing measures are not in place and/or if there are no/ weak dividend lock-up triggers, the rating could be lower, ceteris paribus. 

Other forms of credit support include senior-subordinate debt tranching, trapping surplus cash flows on activation of triggers, etc. The ratings on the tranched debt can be distinguished only where there are no cross-default clauses, clearly established through specific documented mitigants under the project’s transaction documents and legal framework to support such a conclusion that the default on one tranche will not result in a payment default on other senior tranches. 

ICRA also reviews the minimum debt coverage ratio requirements that must be met prior to payment of dividends, and creation of minimum liquidity buffers, for meeting the revenue shortfalls due to disruptions in operations. In addition, guarantees from the sponsor for the O&M and major maintenance cost overruns are also assessed favourably. 

# **Amortisation structure and refinancing requirement** 

While in most cases, the debt structure is fully amortising, there could be situations where some portion of the debt involves a bullet repayment and hence is exposed to a refinancing risk. ICRA’s assessment of refinancing risk includes but is not limited to determining (a) the quantum and certainty of residual cash flows at the time of refinancing vis-a-vis the amount to be refinanced (b) resilience of the project’s debt coverage metrics to refinancing risk under different scenarios of refinancing costs (including ability to withstand a significant step-up in coupon at the time of refinancing) (c) transaction features to mitigate refinancing risks through cash sweeps, and reserve creation well ahead of the debt maturity (d) available liquidity with the issuer in the event of market disruptions. 

Entities with a long tail period, or, in other words, high project life coverage ratio (PLCR) are better equipped to raise additional debt/refinance the existing debt with elongated tenor should there be a requirement in the project. That said, there is a risk of promoters leveraging projects with a high tail period to support other group projects.

## **Covenant package** 

ICRA draws comfort from the restrictive debt covenants, which include prohibitions/tests on additional indebtedness or liens, restrictions on the acquisition and sale of assets, limitations on mergers and consolidations, limitations on investments (permitted investments), restrictions on upstreaming to sponsor, change of control/ownership, especially if the sponsors are important to the project. Further, frequent and regular reports of lenders’ independent engineer to validate the base case assumptions and compliance with contractual and financial obligations, along with presence of lenders’ step-in rights to replace/ terminate material contracts (for instance, O&M contractor in case of non-performance) are viewed favourably. 

# **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Accounting quality** 

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts, auditors’ comments and other disclosures that are parts of the Annual Report of a rated entity. Deviations, if any, from the accounting standards/ practices are assessed and the financial statements of the entity are adjusted to reflect the impact of such deviations. 

## **Contingent liabilities and Off-balance sheet exposures** 

ICRA reviews the contingent liabilities and off-balance sheet exposures as disclosed by the entity in its Annual Report and evaluates the likelihood of their devolvement and the financial implications of the same. 

# **Sponsor Risk Assessment** 

All debt ratings necessarily incorporate an assessment of the quality of the issuer’s management, as well as the strengths/weaknesses arising from the issuer’s being a part of a group. Usually, a detailed discussion is held with the sponsor of the issuer to understand its business objectives, plans and strategies, and views on past performance. Some of the other points assessed are: 

- a. Experience of the sponsor in the line of business concerned 

- b. Commitment of the sponsor to the line of business concerned 

- c. The sponsor’s policies on leveraging, rationale for debt raising, balance between leverage and returns, interest risks and currency risks 

- d. The ability and willingness of the sponsor to support the issuer through measures such as fund infusion, if required 

Despite the non-recourse nature of the SPV, the financial strength of the sponsor is a key credit determinant during the preCOD stage, given that apart from contributing to the promoter’s share in the form of equity capital and/or subordinated debt, the sponsor is directly or indirectly responsible for ensuring the financial closure of these projects. Further, during the pre-COD stage, most of the annuity projects take recourse on the sponsor for cost overrun support and debt servicing in the event of termination as there is no termination payment from the project owner during the construction period. Post the project completion, while many SPVs enter into O&M contracts with the sponsors, the financial linkage is relatively lower during the post-COD period. 

While the weak financial health of the sponsor constrains its ability to support the project SPVs in the time of need, the presence of a financially-strong sponsor need not necessarily mean assured support to fund SPVs in case of any funding shortfall. In the absence of explicit support (guarantees/ shortfall undertakings), the sponsors’ willingness to support the operational projects is determined based on the strength of the project cash flows and duration of the tail period (difference between concession end date and debt maturity date). Also, ICRA considers the past track record of the sponsor in extending timely support to any of its other SPVs and gauging the intent of the sponsor to extend extraordinary support to the rated SPV, based on interactions with the sponsor.

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environment (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly segregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to segregate the sub-components of E&S risks fully or precisely in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, but their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or other environmental and social risks (including resettlement and rehabilitation risks) and seeks to understand the various mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks. Notwithstanding the above, as an example, it is possible that even if an entity A has a higher carbon footprint than entity B, it does not materially affect ICRA’s credit opinion on entity A. This is because ICRA’s credit opinion on an entity considers a wide gamut of credit-relevant factors, and the E&S factors are only one among those. 

The road sector is critical to economic growth and is closely linked to the transport sector, which is one of the major contributors to the greenhouse gas emissions. Alternative modes like rail and inland waterways are relatively less polluting; however, the travel time and the last mile connectivity remains a challenge for these modes. Road developers remain exposed to natural disasters (such as cyclones, floods), or availability related issues pertaining to aggregates/sand or environmental regulations, which could interrupt construction activity or result in an increase in maintenance costs in case of any damage to carriageway, post the COD. However, the availability of insurance for most projects mitigates the adverse effects of these circumstances. Given the large execution targets set by the Government for the road sector, there have been challenges in completing land acquisition, and social risks manifest when there are disagreements over compensation between the developers and the landowners. Thus, the road developers remain exposed to E&S risks arising from the aforementioned factors.

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the issuer‘s business and financial risks, its likely cash flows over the life of the instrument being rated and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis its debt-servicing obligations and other funding requirements. 

For BOT (Annuity) road projects, execution and funding risks assume importance during the implementation phase, while post COD, the revenue and operating risks assume greater significance. ICRA has a more favourable opinion on projects, which have been conservatively funded, awarded by a strong counterparty, promoted by strong sponsors, and which have demonstrated satisfactory O&M track record. ICRA draws comfort from the presence of a strong counterparty and allocation of O&M-related risks to an experienced and creditworthy O&M contractor, with requisite experience in the O&M of road stretches. ICRA draws comfort from healthy DSCR ratios, along with a long tail period to facilitate refinancing if required. The presence of structural features like adequately-funded DSRA, contingency and O&M reserves with restrictive covenants on additional debt are credit positives.
