# **Overview** 

This methodology document describes the key factors considered by ICRA in assessing the credit risk profile of automobile dealers. This methodology does not include an exhaustive treatment of all factors that are reflected in ratings but enables the reader to understand the rating considerations that are usually the most important. 

Automobile dealer is a prominent part of the automobile supply chain, which acts as an interface between Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) and customers. It provides market access to the OEMs and plays a crucial role in enhancing customer experience. The automobile dealership industry in India is highly fragmented, as barring a select few entities, most of the automobile dealers operate in a limited geographical area and thus have limited scale of operations. The fragmented nature of the industry limits the bargaining power of automobile dealers with OEMs, wherein the latter enjoy strong control over pricing and discounts offered by automobile dealers on the sale of vehicles, spare parts as well as service income. 

Revenue from vehicle sales (both new and pre-owned) drives the revenues of automobile dealers, followed by sale of spare parts, services and body-shop income, income from incentives from the principal/ OEMs, and payout from financing and insurance companies. A favourable financing environment is an enabler for automobile sales. The spare parts and services business is one of the most profitable and fastest growing business segments for automobile dealers. Owing to stiff competition and the resultant impact on profitability of the vehicle retailing business, automobile dealers are focusing on the lucrative spare parts and services business to support profitability.

# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Competitive Landscape** 

Automobile dealer industry in India is highly fragmented with relatively low entry barriers, thus creating stiff competition from automobile dealers of the same as well as competitor OEMs in their region of operations. Automobile dealer’s presence in favourable locations characterised by adequate demand for vehicles, healthy purchasing power of the consumers in the catchment area, and limited competition is critical for its growth prospects. In the services business, an automobile dealer workshop faces competition from unorganised players (i.e., road-side garages and standalone OEM authorised service stations) as well as from organised multi-brand workshops, which have gradually started gaining customer acceptability. Similarly, in the spare parts and accessories business space, there is stiff competition from the unorganised segment. 

## **Growth Prospects** 

The performance of automobile dealer business mirrors the overall trend in the domestic automobile industry. Relatively low penetration levels, strong replacement demand, industrial growth, improving availability of finance and rising affordability levels are the key propellants for growth in the domestic automobile industry. Healthy rural economic activity is critical for segments such as tractors and select two-wheelers (2Ws). The Indian automobile industry has a significant long-term growth potential across all sub-segments like 2Ws, passenger vehicles (PVs), commercial vehicles (CVs), three-wheelers (3Ws), construction equipment, and tractors, with particularly strong potential for electric vehicles (EVs) in 2Ws, PVs, 3Ws, and buses. Moreover, increasing premiumisation across most of the sub-segments has led to increased vehicle prices, supporting revenue growth of automobile dealers. Given the long-term growth prospects, several global OEMs continue to set up their base in India, which along with the rising demand for automobiles and focus on the expansion of the distribution networks, led to the launch of automobile dealer outlets by incumbents as well as new OEMs. The automobile dealer industry is also exposed to the inherent cyclicality in the automobile industry. However, presence across multiple automotive segments mitigates some impact of this cyclicality. 

Although most automobile OEMs carry out their sales operations through their network of automobile dealers, select OEMs in select sub-segments, especially in the electric vehicles (EVs) space, have opted for ‘direct-to-customer’ model, thereby bypassing the third-party dealers from their supply chain. While increased adoption of this trend would impact the business prospects of automobile dealers, ICRA notes that OEMs adopting such models are very few at present, and more importantly, the economic viability of ‘direct-to-customer’ models is still evolving and thus may not have medium-term implications on the automobile dealer industry. 

## **Regulatory Risk** 

The industry has an indirect exposure to the policy changes in the automobile industry, which impacts the demand for the automobiles. Over the past few years, there have been multiple changes in regulatory norms across the automobile subsegments including enhanced safety standards, tightening of emission norms, higher standards of vehicle specifications, restrictions on the age of vehicles by category of powertrains (i.e. diesel or petrol), revision in road tax, and norms relating to vehicle insurance, which led to some level of volatility in demand closer to the switching date. These changes typically result in product price increases, and accordingly, have the potential to impact demand, at least on a temporary basis. Nevertheless, the policy changes have been introduced to improve passenger safety and reduce the environmental footprint, which would result in long-term benefits to the industry.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Scale of Operations** 

An automobile dealer’s scale of operations is one of the key factors in determining its business strength and operating flexibility. The large scale of operations, as measured by revenues, is a reflection of a higher market share and enables the establishment of a stronger franchise. This improves automobile dealer’s purchase efficiency, while enhancing business attractiveness of various stakeholders, including OEMs and investors. The scale of an automobile dealer allows it to spread fixed cost (rentals and employees) over a larger revenue. 

An automobile dealer’s ability to sustain volume and revenue growth above the industry average, especially in comparison to its principal OEM, is considered favourably. Such growth typically reflects an increase in market share and/ or diversification across geographies. Conversely, a reduction in revenues during a period when the industry is growing could be indicative of a weakening business position, which may be on account of declining acceptability for principal OEMs models, unfavourable geographic presence, or increased competition from other automobile dealers in the catchment areas. ICRA attempts to analyse the growth stemming from a rise in volumes and realisations separately. The revenue growth in the spare parts and services business, which is the key to an entity’s profitability, is also considered. 

## **Market Position** 

To assess the market position of an automobile dealer, ICRA considers the market position of the rated entity vis-à-vis its competitors in the addressable segment, factoring in (a) the market position of the OEM with whom the rated entity is affiliated, and (b) the automobile dealer’s share in the OEM’s overall domestic sales. The market position of the principal OEM is generally a good proxy for the demand prospects of the rated entity. A well-established market position of the OEM typically provides stable revenue base and a platform for sustainable earnings and cash flow generation for the affiliated automobile dealer. ICRA notes that the increasing market share of an OEM is a positive for its dealers’ earnings growth, whereas shrinking market share could indicate moderation in demand, resulting in sequentially lower sales and higher discounts, impacting earnings. An automobile dealer with a relatively higher share in an OEM’s overall sales is generally a credit positive as such an entity may get preferential treatment in the allotment of models as well as have an opportunity to get higher financial incentives. Certain OEMs, through their captive arms or tie-ups with financial institutions, provide financial support to their large automobile dealers to tide over short-term exigencies. Effectively, large-scale operations and a strong market position enable an automobile dealer to achieve economies of scale with better cost absorption, while providing a greater ability to offer competitive pricing to its buyers. In line with the rapidly evolving trends in the retail industry, automobile dealers face the challenge of adapting to customer requirements to maintain their position in the business. 

## **Diversification** 

## **Revenue mix** 

The revenue mix refers to the share of revenues from old and new vehicle sales and other segments, such as income from the sale of spare parts and services business, sale of other accessories, incentive payout from financiers and insurance companies. While the revenues of automobile dealers are largely derived from the vehicle retailing business, a substantial proportion of the profits are drawn from the sale of spare parts and the services business. A balanced mix between OEM sales and the spares/ services business provides greater earnings stability and higher operating margins than a standalone retailing business. Also, a sound retailing business is often the basis for achieving a healthy performance in the services business. In case of an economic slowdown and a decline in vehicle sales volume, an automobile dealer with a stronger presence in the spare parts and services segment is likely to withstand pressures on profitability much better than those without an established franchise in allied businesses beyond retailing. The increasing penetration of EVs, especially in select sub-segments, may impact the service and spare parts revenue of dealers over the medium-to-long term, given the lower servicing requirements of EVs.

# **Segmental diversification** 

An automobile dealer with a wide product portfolio catering to multiple automobile segments (CVs, PVs, 2Ws, tractors, etc) is in a better position to withstand the demand volatility arising from decline in automobile volumes in a specific segment. For instance, an automobile dealer catering only to the CV segment, a relatively more cyclical segment compared to other automobile segments, could potentially witness higher volatility in its earnings and cash flows, compared to a diversified automobile dealer. 

Consumer preferences are unpredictable and driven by many exogenous factors, including fuel prices and quality perceptions. In case of a prolonged slowdown in the economy or steady deterioration in the principals’ (respective OEMs) market share, automobile dealers catering to a single segment or single OEM might be adversely affected, given the high operating leverage in the automobile dealer business due to high fixed overheads including rental expenses. On the other hand, automobile dealers with a diversified presence with various OEMs (especially those with a strong market position) are better protected during downturns, thereby reducing the vulnerabilities to an extent. Each individual segment of the Indian automobile industry is characterised by a few OEMs accounting for the major market share in the segment. 

# **Geographical diversification** 

An automobile dealer that has a meaningful presence across multiple regions or geographies is able to withstand competition better and perform more consistently during various demand scenarios, while allaying concerns related to asset concentration risks. The number of sales outlets across regions is one of the measures for assessing geographical diversification. Geographical diversification with respect to showrooms is a positive factor as it better equips an automobile dealer to cater to demand from different regions. An automobile dealer with a diversified geographical presence will be less impacted than a dealer at a single location in the case of unforeseen contingencies including operational shutdowns due to natural calamities, fires, and industrial issues, among others.

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

ICRA analyses the long period past financial performance trends and estimates the future financial performance to assess the financial risk exposure of an entity, i.e., to evaluate the sustainability and adequacy of cash flows against its debt servicing obligations. The financial metrics provide a useful reference not only to assess the performance trends of an entity over a given time horizon, but also to enable a comparison with its peers. The financial risk assessment is not done in isolation but in conjunction with the business and the industry risks that the entity is exposed to. An entity with low exposure to business and industry risks would generally have stable cash flows and thus would have a higher tolerance to operate with a relatively moderate financial risk profile. In contrast, entities that are exposed to high business and industry risks need to maintain a stronger financial risk profile to have adequate cushion to manage cash flow volatility. The various financial metrics assessed by ICRA could be divided into five categories viz., profitability, leverage, coverage, liquidity and cash flows<sup>**<u>[1]</u>**</sup> ~~.~~ Given the uncertainty around how the various credit drivers could evolve in the future, ICRA carries out a sensitivity analysis to estimate the impact of the key variables, such as inventory, revenue growth, and margins, on the financial profile of the entity to evaluate its ability to withstand stress events. 

## **Profitability and Earnings Stability** 

Profitability is a measure of earnings generated by an entity in a given time period in relation to its income or resources deployed. It can be influenced by multiple factors, including those that are firm-specific or are related to the industry, economy or regulations. From a rating perspective, both the level as well as the stability in profitability metrics matter. The contribution margin in an automobile dealer business is akin to a trading business. The automobile dealer business has operating leverage and healthy volumes are critical to absorb the fixed cost overheads. A lean operating setup characterised by low fixed overheads provides an automobile dealer the ability to manage its costs during a down cycle, thereby protecting its profitability. Entities with consistent track record of higher profitability than their peers are likely to show stronger resilience against economic downturns and are more likely to generate relatively higher internal resources for re-investment and debt servicing and also attract fresh capital. 

The two primary measures of profitability for an automobile dealer are—(a) operating profit before interest, depreciation, amoritisation, tax and non-operating income and expense (OPBITDA)<sup>_1_</sup> margin, and (b) return on capital employed (RoCE). Apart from the proportion of variable and fixed costs, other key factors that influence the OPBDITA margins of an automobile dealer are the extent of discounts offered, and the proportion of revenues derived from vehicle servicing and sale of spare parts (higher proportion of the latter signifies better margins, ceteris paribus). Automobile dealers who maintain relatively high OPBDITA margins, while maintaining a lean inventory, are also observed to demonstrate superior RoCE. Overall, the healthy profitability metrics allow an automobile dealer to generate adequate cash flows to support the margin funding required for working capital borrowings as well as to meet the capital expenditure needs associated with the expansion.

# **Leverage and Coverage Indicators** 

Financial leverage is a measure of an entity’s dependence on borrowed funds. Lower the dependence on borrowings, the lower (better) the leverage. When an entity borrows, it is obliged to pay both interest as well as principal to the lenders as per a defined schedule. This increases the fixed cost burden on the borrowing entity and thus heightens the risk of default. Usually, working capital debt constitutes a major share of an automobile dealer’s debt, followed by term loans availed for showroom expansion. Several automobile dealers operate on leased showrooms, and financials are accordingly analysed to factor in the long-term lease obligations. Given the thin operating margins and high working capital intensity in the industry, judicious inventory management policy and calibrated expansion of an automobile dealer is the key to maintaining a prudent leverage structure. 

While high leverage may mean high risk from the credit perspective, it is an often-adopted course by shareholder-oriented managements, as high leverage, in good times, leads to increased returns on equity capital. An entity’s financial leverage could thus be a function of its management’s financial policy and risk tolerance, besides being a point-in-time reflection of an entity’s business and financial choices. An entity with lower leverage has higher financial flexibility to raise incremental capital and is thus better equipped to withstand volatility in cash flow generation in situations of economic downturn, competitive challenges, unexpected costs, changing consumer preferences, or regulatory changes.

# **Working Capital Intensity** 

Automobile dealer operations are working capital intensive, with sizeable funds blocked in inventory holdings. Hence, inventory management plays a vital role in the working capital management of an automobile dealer. High inventory levels increase the holding cost and thus result in a rise in the working capital requirements, while inadequate inventory might lead to market share loss. ICRA compares the working capital cycle of rated entities with their peer groups to gauge the rated entity’s working capital management system as a proxy of their business efficiency. Deterioration in the position of an OEM and subsequent inventory push could result in an inventory pile-up for automobile dealers and might translate into liquidity issues. At the same time, a build-up of inventory could result from the automobile dealer’s exertion to avail discounts/incentives from the OEM and in anticipation of a strong offtake in certain periods of the year (such as festive seasons). Such build-ups would be considered necessary investment. Inventory could also be lower because of supply constraints. While lower inventory would reduce interest costs and support earnings, it would also limit the growth potential. As for receivables, vehicles are generally invoiced after confirmation from financiers or after receipt of some upfront payment from customers. Hence, the overall receivable cycle of automobile dealers is modest with most of the receivables attributable to financiers. 

# **Liquidity** 

Liquidity is the measure of an entity’s ability to meet its short-term cash obligations from various internal or external resources. Internal resources include cash flows from operations, unencumbered cash and cash equivalents on balance sheet and cash inflows expected from the monetisation of physical and financial assets. External resources include undrawn lines of credit or shareholder capital in the form of debt or equity. The short-term obligations include both committed as well as the contingent claims on an entity’s cash, including the debt servicing obligations, working capital requirements, capital expenditure and other investment outlays, dividend and share buyback-related outflows, besides the sudden demand arising from the crystallisation of discrete events such as unfavourable outcome of an ongoing litigation. While assessing liquidity, ICRA assumes that the entity would need to set aside a certain cash amount (as a percentage of operating income) to run its day-to-day operations. This amount is considered unavailable to fund other sundry requirements. The higher the cushion available between the resources available (especially internal resources) and the obligations, better the liquidity profile of an entity. Given the thin operating margins and working capital intensive operations, automobile dealers typically remain highly reliant on working capital borrowings. These requirements are funded by facilities such as inventory funding, cash credit, overdraft, etc., typically from banks. Some dealers also avail facilities from non-banking financial companies (NBFCs). Utilisation levels generally peak before the festive season as dealers stock up vehicles to cater to high demand during the season. 

Liquidity is generally assessed in conjunction with the vulnerability of an entity to timely refinancing/renewal of short-term sources of funding. Depending upon the circumstances, an entity that has a relatively modest liquidity profile, but a strong refinancing ability may not be viewed too unfavourably. ## **Cash Flows** 

It is cash that is required to service the obligations. A cash flow statement represents the sources from which cash is generated and its deployment. Analysed here are the trends in an entity’s funds flow from operations, cash consumed to fund the working capital, the retained cash flows after paying out dividends or carrying out share buybacks, and the free cash flows after meeting debt repayment obligations and capital expenditure needs. The cash flow analysis helps in understanding the external funding requirements that an entity has to meet its maturing obligations. 

# **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Tenure Mismatches, and Risks Relating to Interest Rates and Refinancing** 

Large dependence on short-term borrowings to fund long-term investments or other long-term funding requirements can expose an entity to significant re-financing risks, especially during periods of tight systemic liquidity. ICRA evaluates the extent of such mismatches and the mitigating factors therein. One source of mitigation could be the existence of adequate buffers of liquid assets/committed bank lines to meet short-term obligations. Another source of mitigation could be the entity’s strong financial flexibility to garner fresh funds at a short notice or a potent ability to refinance. Further, ICRA evaluates the extent to which an entity might be impacted by the movement in interest rates. 

## **Financial Flexibility** 

An entity’s financial flexibility (or the lack thereof) is reflected in its ability to access the capital or the money markets at a short notice, attract diverse and marquee investors and enjoy the confidence of banks, financial institutions and intermediaries. A strong financial flexibility allows an entity to raise fresh borrowings or refinance existing debt in quick time and whenever required. Financial flexibility could arise from factors such as an entity's large scale of operations with strong financials, large and unencumbered cash flows (such as rental income), unencumbered assets and the flexibility to borrow against such assets, or strong parentage or linkages with a strong group. 

In contrast, among the various measures of an entity’s depleting financial flexibility, one relates to a high share of pledged promoter shareholding. A sign such as this may imply that the entity might be persuaded to distribute high dividends or support the promoter group through other means to the detriment of its own credit profile. If the promoters fail to repay their loans (availed by pledging of shares) or top-up collateral when required, the lenders could sell the pledged shares. In some cases, this could trigger a change-of-control clause in the rated entity’s bond indentures or loan documents and require it to redeem its debt ahead of schedule, creating a liquidity squeeze, besides affecting fresh capital-raising ability. Financial flexibility could also be impacted in cases of adverse industry developments, weakening business profile, or management and governance concerns, either at the entity or group level. These could translate into sharp decline in market capitalisation or spike in bond yields and consequently constrain an entity’s ability to raise fresh capital or materially increase its cost of capital.

# **Accounting Quality** 

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts, auditors’ comments and other disclosures that are a part of the Annual Report of a rated entity. Any deviation from the accounting standards/practices are assessed, and the financial statements of the entity are adjusted, where feasible, to reflect the impact of such deviations. Significant deviations may be indicative of weak corporate governance practices within the entity. 

# **Contingent Liabilities / Off-Balance Sheet Exposures** 

ICRA’s analyses the likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/off-balance sheet exposures and its impact on the entity’s financial implications while factoring the mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion. 

# **Consolidated Financial Analysis** 

While evaluating the financial risk profiles of companies that have subsidiaries and associate companies, ICRA analyses consolidated/group level financial indicators in terms of capital structure, debt coverage indicators and future funding requirements. 

# **Parentage** 

While the credit rating of an entity is a function of its standalone credit profile, in certain cases, the entity’s credit quality can also be driven by the relationship with its parent or the promoter group (henceforth referred to as the parent). If the parent’s credit profile is relatively stronger than the rated entity, ICRA assesses the ability and the likelihood of the parent extending extraordinary support to the entity. Support here means the financial support from the parent, which is expected to be available to the entity such as loans, equity, extended credit period and advances, in times of credit or liquidity stress on the entity. ‘Support’ does not refer to the ongoing or committed financial contribution from the parent, such as capital infusion for expansion or maintaining a target capital structure. Instead, it denotes exceptional, unforeseen support aimed at preventing default, which is not factored into the standalone rating assessment. It also does not signify operational support in the form of new business opportunities, technology sharing, distribution network sharing and so on, as these aspects are factored in the standalone credit profile assessment itself. It may be noted that promoters in their individual capacity, or private equity firms/other financial investors are generally not treated as parents for assessing the likelihood of extraordinary financial support coming in. If the parent’s credit profile is relatively weaker than the rated entity, the entity’s rating may be lower than what its standalone credit profile assessment would have merited. # **Debt Servicing Track Record** 

The company’s debt servicing track record forms an important rating consideration. Any history of past delays or defaults in meeting the interest and principal repayment obligations reduces the comfort level with respect to the company’s future debt servicing capability and willingness. Nevertheless, the reason behind past defaults are also analysed, which could also be due to adverse demand situations in the underlying segments. The ability to honour its debt obligations during a period of cyclical stress is also factored in.

# **Management Quality Assessment** 

In addition to the industry, business and financial risk analysis, all credit ratings incorporate an assessment of the quality of the rated entity’s management and its financial policies. 

## **Quality of Management and Financial Policies** 

As a part of its process, ICRA undertakes discussions with the rated entity’s management to understand its views on past performance as well as its future plans and strategies, besides the outlook on the industry. Some of the points assessed are: 

- » Experience of the promoter/management in the industry 

- » Commitment of the promoter/management to the rated entity 

- » Risk appetite of the promoter/management and risk mitigation plans / implementation of effective financial controls 

- » Policies on leveraging, managing interest rate and currency risks 

- » Management’s past success in introducing new projects and managing changes in the external environment 

- » Management’s plans on new projects, acquisitions and expansions 

Periodic interactions with the management help ascertain the shifts, if any, in their financial policies. 

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environmental (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly, if nor precisely. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the sub-components of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap **<u>[5]</u>** . 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is likely to play out in the distant future and, hence, these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model.

## **Governance Practices** 

A sound corporate governance structure should clearly delineate the roles and responsibilities of the Board of Directors and the management. The composition of an entity’s Board, its involvement in strategic decision making, and the entity’s compliance with legal and regulatory requirements are factored in during credit assessments. ICRA also seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of an entity’s commitment to following transparent and credible practices as reflected in the presentation of the financial statements, timelines and depth of disclosures, consistency in communication and the openness about sharing information during the rating process. Additionally, factors such as complexity of the corporate group structure, related party transactions, instances of financial support to group entities at the expense of debt holders, and any abrupt resignations of auditors or independent directors are evaluated. 

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the rated entity. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the rated entity’s industry, business and financial risks, its likely cash flows and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis the debt servicing obligations and other funding requirements. ICRA’s rating approach also involves an assessment of the entity’s management quality and governance practices. In addition to these considerations, an entity’s rating may also be influenced by its ownership, the nature of linkages with its parent or group entities, degree of financial flexibility, the corporate legal structure, track record of operations and that of debt servicing, and vulnerability (if any) to discrete event risks.
