The Indian fertiliser industry can broadly be divided into two segments depending on the nutrient composition: (i) nitrogenous fertilisers, primarily urea and (ii) phosphatic and potassic fertilisers (P&K). Out of the total fertiliser volumes sold (70.4 MMT in FY2025), urea contributed around 55%, while the remaining 45% comprised P&K fertilisers, including single super phosphate (SSP). Phosphatic fertilisers have varying levels of nitrogen, phosphorous and potassium, which are used depending on the crop and soil health. These include di-ammonium phosphate (DAP), which is the major phosphatic fertiliser used in India, along with NPK<sup>_1_</sup> and SSP. Potassic fertilisers mainly include muriate of potash (MOP), which is not manufactured in India and is entirely imported. 

The industry has grown over the years, aided by Government policies and demand growth arising from an increasing area under cultivation and changing crop mix. The industry has been heavily regulated for decades by the Government of India (GoI) to ensure the availability of these fertilisers at affordable rates to farmers. The regulations have covered, inter alia, the farm gate prices (FGP), types of fertilisers eligible for subsidy, the distribution pattern and the extent of profitability that can be earned by the manufacturers. Between the two fertiliser segments, the P&K segment was partially decontrolled in 2010, following which players gained the flexibility to make changes in the retail prices of such decontrolled fertilisers as per their cost structure and the demand-supply dynamics. However, the GoI still monitors the reasonableness of the retail prices by auditing the input costs. 

Among the various fertilisers, urea and DAP plants are characterised by high capital intensity, while the NPK complexes and SSP plants are relatively less capital intensive. Given that the capital cost of setting up a urea unit is much higher, it essentially requires higher profitability. While the plants set up under the New Investment Policy-2012 (NIP-2012) continue to garner healthy profitability, pre-NIP2012 plants face under-recoveries on the reimbursement of the fixed costs by the GoI (as there are significant delays in the revision of the fixed costs to be reimbursed by the GoI), which impacts cash generation for such plants.

# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Regulatory risk** 

The profitability of fertiliser companies is significantly influenced by the regulatory prescriptions governing various types of fertilisers. Indian companies sell most of the widely used fertilisers to farmers at subsidised rates, well below the market prices, with the GoI reimbursing the subsidy to the industry. The subsidy is the difference between the normative cost of manufacturing the fertilisers and the farm gate price (FGP). For administrative convenience, the GoI has been routing the subsidy through the fertiliser companies, while farmers pay the subsidised price. The Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) scheme implemented for the fertiliser sector also currently routes the subsidies through the industry. While there were plans to ultimately transition to a subsidy regime wherein farmers receive the subsidy directly instead of the industry, similar to liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), currently there is no defined timeline for achieving this transition by the GoI. Additionally, the distribution and availability of fertilisers are closely controlled by the GoI through monthly supply plans, thus giving entities limited flexibility in exploring distribution to newer geographies. 

The composition of price realisation differs for urea and non-urea fertilisers in terms of the fixed and floating nature of the MRP and the subsidy (see graphic below). Urea farm gate prices are fixed by the GoI and the subsidy payable varies in line with the feedstock and fuel prices. This is to ensure, in principle, that a normative return (post-tax 12% return on equity or RoE) on fixed costs is earned by urea players. Further, in the case of urea, for production beyond the re-assessed capacity (RAC), a uniform per MT incentive is payable, subject to the import parity price (IPP) plus other incidental charges incurred on imported urea.

Thus, the adequacy of budgetary allocation for fertiliser subsidy outgo remains a key monitorable. 

The existing gas-based urea units, apart from the units under the New Investment Policy-2012 (NIP-2012), have been classified into three homogeneous groups based on their energy consumption levels. While the principle of energy savings continues to exist as per New Pricing Scheme-III (NPS-III), the pre-set norms have been tightened over the years, resulting in reduced profitability for urea units unless the unit is able to reduce its energy consumption. ICRA analyses the level of energy savings being earned by urea units. 

**Regulatory risk for new urea plants being set up under New Urea Investment Policy-2012:** The GoI introduced the New Investment Policy-2012 (NIP-2012), which assured a floor and ceiling of post-tax return on equity (RoE) of 12% and 20%, respectively, for setting up brownfield/greenfield urea capacity in the country, for the first eight years of operations (subsequent policy clarity is yet to be seen as none of the plants has crossed the first eight years of operations as yet). These plants are also exposed to offtake risk, given that the GoI removed the assured offtake clause under NIP-2012. As the cost of procurement from these plants can be higher than imported urea in a scenario of elevated gas costs, these plants are exposed to offtake risk. However, since the GoI’s stance is to be self-sufficient in urea, it largely mitigates the risk. As setting up these plants entails sizeable capex, which in turn results in sizeable debt levels and materially large debt repayments and interest servicing in the initial years of operations, the capacity utilisation of such plants remains a key monitorable. Additionally, policy clarity for plants set up under this policy beyond the first eight years of applicability remains a key monitorable. 

# **Agro-climatic risks** 

As the share of irrigated (by dams/canals/wells) area is low in India, most regions are dependent on the monsoons. Even the irrigated areas are indirectly dependent on the monsoons. Thus, fertiliser sales get negatively impacted in areas that witness continued seasons of excess or deficient rainfall, impacting agri-activity in the region. Besides lower sales, fertiliser manufacturers are impacted by higher systemic inventory levels, discounts and a longer credit period to push sales during such years. However, the risk can be mitigated to some extent if manufacturers have a diversified geographic presence across several states, as the probability of a weak monsoon occurring simultaneously across several states remains low to moderate. 

# **Competition** 

In the urea segment, the offtake remains assured till the re-assessed capacity for pre-NIP-2012 plants. While the policy does not guarantee offtake for production beyond RAC for pre-NIP-2012 plants and for plants set up under NIP-2012, the offtake risk is mitigated owing to significant import dependence and the GoI’s stance to ensure self-sufficiency in urea to the maximum extent possible. This, coupled with fixed MRPs (as determined by the DoF), keeps the competitive intensity relatively low for urea players. However, for the non-urea segment, competition is driven by pricing, geographical presence and the distribution network of players.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Scale** 

The scale of operations of a company plays an important role in the evaluation of its business position. A larger scale of operation enables the company to serve a larger catchment area, thereby enabling diversification of its geographical presence along with better absorption of fixed costs. A larger scale also enables the establishment of a wider dealer network and supports brand development across geographies. Large-scale P&K players have relatively better bargaining power in terms of securing raw material availability, though pricing largely remains aligned with global prices. Distribution and marketing territories are also, by and large, decided by the GoI, and distribution freight costs are reimbursed at near-actual levels, albeit with delays at times. Thus, a large scale of operations drives cost and manufacturing efficiencies and supports meaningful diversification across products and geographies, thereby supporting economies of scale and leading to stronger bargaining power with various stakeholders in the value chain. 

## **Cost competitiveness** 

## **<u>Urea players</u>** 

### **Energy efficiency** 

The cost competitiveness of urea players depends on energy consumption relative to normative energy norms. The feedstock cost for all domestic urea players that have gas pipeline connectivity is uniform and is a pass-through to the GoI up to the normative energy norm. Profitability is negatively impacted for urea units with energy consumption higher than the normative energy norm, while units with energy consumption below normative energy result in energy savings. The energy consumption of urea units also determines the competitiveness of production beyond the re-assessed capacity against imported urea. Lower energy consumption results in higher competitiveness against imports. While the natural gas price is a pass-through to the GoI, higher natural gas prices result in higher energy savings for urea players with energy consumption below their normative norms, thus aiding profitability. 

Energy consumption is a function of the vintage of the unit, process technology adopted and the maintenance practices followed. With very few process technology suppliers to choose from, process technology differentiation has mainly been determined by plant vintage. The recent plants have adopted modern processes, while older plants continue to operate using earlier processes. Efficient units that achieve lower energy consumption than the normative parameters gain from the policy, as energy norms are not revised during a particular policy period, leading to certainty of energy savings. 

The GoI has been progressively tightening the normative energy norms for the urea industry over the years. The tightening of energy norms results in lower subsidy outgo for the GoI and improvement in the energy efficiency of the urea industry. However, to meet lower energy norms, urea units have to incur additional capital investments. While energy norms were revised for 11 urea units from April 2018, the norms for the remaining 14 units were revised from October 2020 (was extended till March 2023 and, in a few cases, special dispensation allowed further extensions, which have now expired). Now all urea units are classified under three categories with normative norms of 5.5 Gcal/MT, 6.0 Gcal/MT and 6.5 Gcal/MT. With the revision in energy norms, profitability for some urea players has declined where energy savings after the revision have been lower than earlier levels. Currently, the GoI is in the process of revising the energy norms for urea units further, although the timeline for revision is not yet clear; the revision, as and when it happens, will remain a key event that would impact the cost structure of urea units. 

With the Government assigning top priority status to city gas distribution (CGD) for the allocation of domestic gas, along with declining domestic gas production from nomination fields, the share of domestic gas in the overall consumption mix of the fertiliser sector has declined significantly since FY2014. The shortfall in the availability of domestic gas is being met through higher-cost R-LNG, which has increased the feedstock price for the fertiliser sector. In July 2015, the GoI implemented gas

## **Capacity utilisation** 

Capacity utilisation also plays an important role in evaluating the credit profile of entities. Increase in capacity utilisation visà-vis the re-assessed capacity (RAC) results in higher additional profitability urea units can generate, which is benchmarked to international prices. Additionally, with higher production and sales, the energy savings of the urea unit also increase, and the operations tend to remain more efficient, as urea plants are process plants which can have lower efficiency when operating at part loads. 

# **<u>P&K players</u>** 

In the case of phosphatic and complex fertilisers, the import dependence for raw materials is high because of the lack of sufficient availability in India. The key raw materials for manufacturing complex fertilisers, ammonia, phosphoric acid, rock phosphate, muriate of potash and sulphur, are largely imported. Although some players manufacture phosphoric acid and ammonia to partially meet their requirements, while sulphur is available indigenously from oil refineries, the raw material used to produce these products is also largely imported, leading to indirect import dependence. Of the raw materials/intermediates, phosphoric acid and rock phosphate are in short supply in the global market and hence durable tie-ups with producers in overseas countries could be a source of competitive advantage for the units, although this may not confer any pricing advantage over other importers as the pricing is based on internationally agreed prices. The ability to control the overall cost of production remains a key source of strength for the players. The cost of production is influenced by import prices, exchange rate fluctuations and conversion efficiency. Adequate handling systems and storage facilities, given the high import dependence, also help impart raw material security. The location of the unit can influence both raw material costs and distribution costs of fertilisers. In general, the proximity of units to large consumer markets is an added competitive advantage as the cost of transporting the feedstock is lower relative to that of the finished fertilisers.

While the Department of Fertiliser (DoF) decides the supply of fertilisers as per the supply plan, diversification of the supply area increases as the scale of operations expands. As Indian agriculture is largely dependent on the monsoons, either directly or indirectly, consecutive failures in a particular region can lead to significant agrarian stress and

# **Raw material/feedstock linkages**

As rock phosphate, which is a key raw material for manufacturing phosphoric acid, is more easily available compared to phosphoric acid that has limited international suppliers, it enables an entity to produce phosphoric acid and achieve a cost advantage over imported phosphoric acid.

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

The objective here is to determine an entity’s current financial position, the company’s financial policy and its projected financial risk profile, along with the sensitivity of the financial risk profile to several parameters. Some of the aspects analysed in detail in this context are<sup>_2_</sup> given below: 

## **Adequacy of future cash flows** 

With the rating exercise primarily focused on assessing the future debt servicing capability of a company, it is imperative that projections are drawn up during the rating exercise to evaluate the likely financial position of the company, going forward. The projections are drawn factoring in the expected movement in revenues and operating margins, working capital changes, the upcoming debt obligations, as well as the capex and investment requirements of a company. These financial projections reflect the expected movement in cash flows and the leverage and debt coverage indicators of the company under various scenarios. ICRA also evaluates the funding requirements of the company and the funding options available to it. 

## **Profitability metrics** 

ICRA determines the trends in an entity’s operating profitability, and how they stand in comparison with its peers. The companies’ ability to achieve the normative return for various types of fertilisers is a key rating parameter. Efficient urea companies achieve higher returns by operating the units at more than the normative capacity utilisation, which enables them to recover more than 100% of the fixed cost assumed for computing the RP. Achieving lower energy consumption than the pre-set norms is also another source of gain for the units. For the phosphatic and complex fertiliser units, efficient raw material sourcing, firm policies to hedge currency risk and efficient conversion can aid the profitability because of the relatively high raw material intensity in the business.

## **Leverage and coverage indicators** 

**Leverage indicators:** Financial leverage is a measure of an entity’s dependence on borrowed funds. Lower the dependence on borrowings, the lower (better) the leverage. When an entity borrows, it is obliged to pay both interest as well as principal to the lenders as per a defined schedule. This increases the fixed cost burden on the borrowing entity and in the limiting case, increases the default risk. While high leverage may mean high risk from a credit perspective, it is an often-adopted course by shareholder-oriented managements, given that high leverage, in good times, leads to high returns on equity capital. An entity’s financial leverage could thus be a function of its management’s financial policy and risk tolerance, besides being a point-intime reflection of an entity’s business and financial choices. **Coverage indicators:** Coverage is a measure of an entity’s debt-servicing ability and is calculated as the ratio of profits to the debt-servicing obligations in a given time period. Increase in ratio results in higher cushion availability for an entity to withstand variability in profits while meeting its financial obligations. Coverage is a function of an entity’s profits, leverage and debt characteristics (in terms of cost of debt and repayment schedule). The interest coverage indicator reflects the company’s ability to fund the cost of external borrowings after meeting all operating expenditure requirements. The debt service coverage ratio - - (DSCR) is a measure of an entity’s debt servicing ability and is calculated as the ratio of profits to the debt servicing obligations in a given time period. Entities with higher profitability and lower leverage will generally have better coverage ratios and thereby healthier financial risk profiles. 

Working capital borrowings constitute a larger share of the borrowings for the majority of fertiliser entities and tend to be rolling in nature. As a result, the interest coverage ratio remains a key metric to assess the debt-servicing capability of an entity. For companies with long-term debt on their books (post new greenfield units or brownfield expansions), the ability to meet debt repayments is assessed through the debt service coverage ratio.

# **Liquidity** 

Liquidity is the measure of an entity’s ability to meet its short-term cash obligations from various internal or external resources. Internal resources include cash flows from operations, unencumbered cash and cash equivalents on the balance sheet and cash inflows expected from the monetisation of physical and financial assets. External resources include undrawn lines of credit or equity capital. The short-term obligations include both the committed as well as the contingent claims on an entity’s cash, including the debt servicing obligations, working capital requirements, capital expenditure and other investment outlays, dividend and share buyback-related outflows, besides the sudden demand arising from the crystallisation of discrete events such as unfavourable outcome of an ongoing litigation. The higher the cushion available between the resources available (especially internal resources) and the obligations, better the liquidity profile of an entity. 

Liquidity is generally assessed in conjunction with the vulnerability of an entity to timely refinancing/renewal of short-term sources of funding. Depending on the circumstances, an entity that has a relatively modest liquidity profile, but a strong refinancing ability may not be viewed too unfavourably. ICRA also notes that the liquidity available with an entity may be for a temporary period and hence an entity’s overall policy towards maintaining adequate liquidity (given the trade-off between returns and liquidity) is accorded due importance in the analytical approach. 

# **Other areas analysed:** 

**Cash flow analysis** **_:_** Cash flows reflect the sources from which cash is generated and its deployment for debt servicing as well as other cash outflows. ICRA analyses the drivers of an entity’s funds flow from operations (FFO) after adjusting for working capital changes, the retained cash flows and the free cash flows after meeting the debt repayment obligations and capital expenditure needs. The cash flow analysis also helps in understanding the external funding requirement that an entity has to meet its maturing obligations. 

**Foreign currency-related risks** **_:_** Such risks arise if an entity’s major costs and revenues are denominated in different currencies. Urea players are protected from foreign currency fluctuations due to the pass-through nature of the subsidy regime, although it does impact working capital requirements. However, P&K players are exposed to currency risk as almost the entire raw material is imported and, in situations of steep currency depreciation, the industry may find it difficult to pass on the currency impact to farmers. Foreign currency risk can also arise from unhedged liabilities such as foreign currency debt (both working capital and term debt), especially for companies earning most of their revenues in local currency. ICRA analyses the hedging policy of the concerned entity in the context of the tenure and nature of its contracts with clients (short term/long term, fixed price/variable price). Foreign currency risk for an entity is measured by considering its unhedged net liabilities [= foreign currency receivables – foreign currency payables – foreign currency debt]. ICRA’s analytical focus is on assessing the magnitude of such exposure relative to the entity’s profits.

## **Accounting quality:** 

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts, auditors’ comments and other disclosures that are parts of the Annual Report of a rated entity. Any deviations from the standard accounting practices are evaluated and the financial statements are adjusted, where feasible, to reflect the impact of such deviations. Significant deviations may be indicative of weak corporate governance practices in the entity. 

**Contingent liabilities/Off-balance sheet exposures:** The likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/off-balance sheet exposures and the financial implications of the same are evaluated while factoring the mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion. 

# **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Product Diversity** 

The ability of a company to offer a diverse bouquet of products to farmers provides a distinctive advantage over competitors. Companies can offer unregulated farm inputs like seeds, agrochemicals etc. along with fertilisers which will enable them to realise higher profits. 

## **Parentage/ Group Support** 

While the credit rating of an entity is a function of its standalone credit profile, in certain cases, the entity’s credit quality can also be driven by the relationship with its parent or the promoter group (henceforth referred to as the parent). If the parent’s credit profile is relatively stronger than the rated entity, ICRA assesses the ability and the likelihood of the parent extending extraordinary support to the entity. Support here means the financial support from the parent, which is expected to be available to the entity such as loans, equity, extended credit period and advances, in times of credit or liquidity stress on the entity. ‘Support’ does not mean the ongoing or committed financial support from the parent, such as the capital infusion to fund the rated entity’s expansion plans or support to maintain a targeted capital structure, but the extraordinary support which cannot be foreseen, is intended to avoid a default and thus cannot be factored-in while assessing the notional standalone rating of the entity. It also does not signify operational support in the form of new business opportunities, technology sharing, distribution network sharing and so on as these aspects are factored in the standalone credit profile assessment itself. It may be noted that promoters in their individual capacity, or private equity firms/ other financial investors are generally not treated as parents for assessing the likelihood of extraordinary financial support coming in. If the parent’s credit profile is relatively weaker than the rated entity, the entity’s rating may be lower than what its standalone credit profile assessment would have merited. This is given the possibility that the entity may at some point of time be bound to extend financial support to its weaker parent, possibly to the detriment of its own credit profile<sup>**4**</sup> .

## **Debt Servicing Track Record** 

The debt servicing track record of a company forms an important rating consideration. Any history of past delays or defaults in meeting interest and principal repayment obligations reduces the comfort level with respect to the company’s future debt servicing capability and willingness. Nevertheless, the reasons behind past defaults are also analysed, which could also be due to adverse demand situations in the underlying industry. The company’s ability to honour its debt obligations during periods of cyclical stress is also factored in. 

## **Event Risks** 

ICRA recognises the possibility of events, such as unrelated diversification, mergers and acquisitions, business restructuring, asset sales and spinoffs, equity infusion and refinancing, capital restructuring and litigations, which could have a material impact on the credit profile of a company. Incorporating the impact of such discrete events in the credit rating, from the beginning, is often difficult. Depending on whether and when such events occur, the rating opinion could be different. To take rating decisions in such cases, ICRA applies its analytical judgment based on the rated entity’s track record, the credibility of the management and the experience of having seen similar situations play out in other entities. However, given the nature of such events, it is possible that the rating may undergo a material change later, upon the occurrence of the event.

In addition to the business and financial risk analysis, all debt ratings incorporate an assessment of the quality of an entity’s management and financial policies. 

In addition, the likely cash flow impact on the rated entity from the possible need to support other group entities is of importance, in case the rated entity is among the stronger ones within a group. Usually, a detailed discussion is held with the management of the rated entity to understand its business objectives, plans and strategies, and views on past performance, besides the outlook on the rated entity’s industry. 

Some of the points assessed are: 

- Experience of the promoter/management in the industry 

- Commitment of the promoter/management to the rated entity 

- Risk appetite of the promoter/management and risk mitigation plans / implementation of effective financial controls 

- Management’s plans on new projects, acquisitions and expansions

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

## **Environmental (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking the credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions while taking a forward-looking view on risks and mitigants. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect a rated entity/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the sub-components of E&S risks in a credit analysis as these considerations often tend to overlap. 

The materiality of E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differ widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while E&S risks could be material, their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of an entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today, with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity would have possibly adapted itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is, or intends to, mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or carbon-transition risks such as those arising from changes in regulations or other environmental and social risks, and seeks to understand the mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks. Global efforts towards decarbonisation and focus on the impact of fertiliser use on soil health may lead to the development of new types of fertilisers and lower demand for conventional fertilisers. 

However, the development of new fertilisers and their efficacy testing is expected to take a long time as it has repercussions for food security at a global level. In India, ICRA does not expect any material impact on conventional fertiliser offtake in the near to medium term. Acceptance among farmers will take time due to the generally risk-averse nature of the farming community and the substantial impact that a season of lost/low productivity crop can have on farm incomes. Fertiliser manufacturing, particularly urea, has a significant carbon footprint as natural gas is the key raw material for the synthesis of hydrogen used in the production of ammonia and thereafter urea. With the GoI exploring the possibility of mandating the procurement of green hydrogen by refineries and fertiliser plants, this could lead to an additional cost burden on urea manufacturers. ICRA expects the GoI to provide adequate policy support to the sector if it decides to mandate the sector to meet a part of its hydrogen requirement through the green route. Agro-climatic risk remains a key risk for the sector as a significant portion of the Indian agriculture sector remains dependent on the monsoons, and changing climate patterns leading to lower-than-normal rainfall can impact fertiliser offtake. The spatial and temporal distribution of rainfall also remains a key environmental risk for the sector. 

While the fertiliser sector remains a socially important sector, given its role in ensuring food security, there have been rising concerns related to the excessive usage of fertilisers and its impact on the consumers. Changing consumer preference towards the use of organically-grown products wherein no chemical fertilisers are used could pose a social risk for fertiliser demand. While ICRA does not expect the risk to fructify in the near term to significantly impact fertiliser offtake, innovations in organic farming techniques could lead to higher yields and lower the pricing premium for organically grown products, posing a threat to fertiliser demand in the long term.

# **Summing Up** 

In the current Government-regulated environment for urea, the ability to control costs in line with normative parameters is a key critical factor for fertiliser manufacturers’ profitability and debt-servicing ability. For P&K fertiliser producers, the critical factors on which profitability depends are a healthy scale of operations, a diversified geographical marketing base, ease of access to raw materials and robust risk management policies. While the working capital intensity of the sector has moderated since the clearance of the subsidy backlog by the GoI in FY2020 and thereafter largely timely release of subsidy, it still remains sensitive to the budgetary allocation by the GoI and is a critical factor while evaluating fertiliser entities.
