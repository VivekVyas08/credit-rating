# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Operating environment** 

The operating environment is an important parameter, which could affect an insurer’s growth, underwriting performance and profitability. The life insurance industry in India has seen growth with the increase in penetration and an increase in the population. Life insurance companies provide policyholders with both protection against mortality as well as long term investment opportunities. An increased awareness about life insurance, post the pandemic, augurs well for the industry. Economic development and increase in financial savings typically foster high demand for the long-term savings as well as retirement products offered by life insurers. On the other hand, increased competition leading to pricing pressures, competition from alternate saving avenues or high volatility in interest rates would adversely weigh on the insurers’ growth and profitability. The capital market activity or the prevailing interest rates drives the investment income and they form a part of the evaluation of the operating environment. This apart, the changes in taxation can also temporarily influence the growth in premium collected by the industry. 

## **Regulatory environment** 

ICRA evaluates the current regulatory environment, as well as impact of impending changes in regulations that could impact an insurer’s competitive position or lead to a restructuring of the products/segments within the industry. The life insurance industry has seen budgetary announcements and regulatory changes in the recent past, which had a bearing on the overall growth and segment-wise portfolio of the industry. The Union Budget 2023 announced taxation on returns from life policies with a premium of more than Rs. 5 lakh per annum w.e.f. April 1, 2023. With the taxation, the demand for guaranteed income life insurance products and hence, the individual premium growth was adversely impacted in FY2024. On June 12, 2024, IRDAI released new regulations regarding the surrender value for non-linked savings products which has come into effect from October 01, 2024. The regulator has also revised thresholds pertaining to the commissions and expenses of an insurance company. Further, there are regulations with respect to investments which also drives the composition of the investment book. The solvency requirement and measurement of risk (in solvency calculations) has multipliers, which are pre-defined by the regulator. These regulatory developments can potentially impact ICRA’s assessment and outlook on the growth, profitability, and capitalisation, which are among the key drivers of the credit profile.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Franchise** 

The franchise strength of an insurer determines its capacity to grow while maintaining granularity (share of retail business in overall business) and diversity across product segments, geography and distribution channels, thereby providing stability to revenue and earnings. The franchise strength, therefore, could be evaluated in terms of its scale of operations/market share and its presence across various segments. 

The distribution network is a determinant of its competitive positioning. A well-diversified distribution channel strengthens the business profile and offers opportunities for growth across segments. 

The key aspects of franchise evaluation are discussed below: 

- **Market position** Insurers with a strong market share in the key segments are expected to benefit from better diversification of risk and operating leverage. Apart from the overall market position as measured by the market share in the NBP, ICRA favourably views a higher presence in the granular individual (retail) segment. An established presence in these segments reflects the strength of the distribution franchise, diversification of risk and offers more stability to the overall business. While the initial costs for the retail segments are higher, however, as the scale expands, profitability also improves.  Further, the retail segments are the key drivers for the growth in the life insurance industry. The other nonretail businesses (group fund/ group term) could be large in terms of premium flows, however, given that these could be concentrated and finely priced due to intense competition, the volatility in premium income and profitability could be high. Thus, a strong presence in the retail segments would be important for maintaining/improving the market position. ICRA monitors the growth in the NBP of the insurer compared to the industry average and in relation to its base. 

- **Diversity of product mix:** A well-diversified life insurance product portfolio and accordingly a balanced mix of income make for a healthy operating profile and imparts stability to an insurer’s revenues and profits. The product suite of life insurance companies typically comprises three segments: Savings products, Protection products, and Group fund/pension management products. ## **Quality of Management** 

ICRA’s assessment factors in the management’s long-term strategy, the experience of its key management team, management’s appetite for risk, and its risk management. ICRA’s analysis entails interactions with an insurer’s management, which enables forming an opinion on the management’s stance towards growth. 

The following parameters are considered: 

- **Management depth and breadth:** The presence of qualified and experienced professionals and the track record of a stable top management are positive factors. Business outcomes vis-à-vis the guidance provided by the insurer also become parameters for assessing the management profile. Managements with the ability to identify emerging risks and select the chosen areas of growth are considered favourably and vice versa. 

- **Established policies:** Established policies on factors such as capital cushions vis-à-vis regulatory levels and calibrated growth are some positive attributes. The demonstrated track record of adhering to these policies reflects favourably on the management. 

- **Strategy:** A well-laid out business plan with regular disclosures on strategy and progress on goal achievements are some positive factors. 

- **Conservative reserving:** A prudent and conservative approach in the reserving given the long tail nature of the life insurance business, is a positive attribute. An indicator for the same would be the consistent track record of favourable experiences in the Embedded Value (EV) walk. The various operating assumptions in the EV would include persistency, mortality and operating expenses amongst others. A favourable development would indicate a conservative reserving practise with the actual performance being better compared to estimates. 

- **Quality of disclosures:** While there are various disclosures as part of regulatory requirements, disclosures beyond these through investor presentations, quality of financial reports, earnings calls etc reflects favourably.

Financial performance analysis is one of the key elements for comparing an insurer’s performance over a time period and across its peer group. ICRA conducts a detailed financial analysis of the insurer being rated on the parameters given below.

long-term horizon. The policies remain in force for a long period of time and the ultimate profitability flows in only once all the policy obligations are met. Life insurers report the accounting profitability which does not capture the longer-term profits which the policies will generate over a period. Hence, for a life insurance company the value of new business (VNB) and return on embedded value (RoEV) is a more forward-looking approach to understand the profitability of an insurer. 

**Return on embedded value –** EV is the present value of all the future profits which a life insurer will generate from the existing policies in force along with the networth of the company. The VNB is an important aspect which reflects the profitability of incremental business and drives the growth in the EV. The VNB is nothing but the present value of all the future profits that the business which has been underwritten during a year is expected to generate and is the outcome of the VNB margin and the annualised premium equivalent (APE)<sup>_2_</sup> . The VNB margin is a metric which is a function of the product mix. Higher the VNB margin and growth in APE, the higher is the growth in the VNB and the EV. The VNB margin varies across products with protection having the highest VNB margins followed by non-par, par and linked products. A company which wants to improve its VNB margins will focus on increasing presence in the protection and non-par segments, however, such products are also associated with relatively higher capital consumption, given the sum assured and interest rate risk for these products.

**Unwind** - Unwind refers to the realised value of the profit in the current year from the past businesses written by the company in past years. The value of this profit was earlier captured in the EV at a discounted rate. 

**Operating assumption changes** – These are changes made to the operating assumptions while calculating the present value of future profits. Aggressive operating assumptions while reporting EV will lead to negative experience in future years and hence decline in the EV in future. 

**Operating variance** - The operating experience variance captures the difference between the actual and expected experience typically in terms of mortality, persistency, operating expenses. 

**Economic variance** – Economic variance is a function of the movement in the interest rates and equity markets. It indicates the impact of the movement in interest rates compared to the reference rates used. 

The ROEV is an indication of the return which the company generates on its opening EV. It is computed as the sum of the VNB + Unwind + Operating variance divided by the Opening EV of the insurer. This is a better indicator of the profitability of a life insurer as opposed to the return on equity. 

**Return on equity –** In the absence of disclosures pertaining to EV, the return on equity is a metric used to evaluate the profitability. A life insurer typically takes a longer period to break even. The company starts to generate accounting profitability only once it has reached a certain scale to generate back book surplus (profits from existing policies) which is sufficient to offset the new business strain.

**Persistency –** Persistency ratio is the number of policy holders who continue to pay the renewal premium in the case of regular premium policies. Given the high acquisition cost involved, much of which is upfronted, high persistency is important from the perspective of profitability to recover the initial cost. High persistency ensures a predictable revenue stream for the company and enables better planning for the deployment of investments. Further, high persistency is an indication of a well-informed customer and overall customer satisfaction which in turn is an indicator of sustainable growth. High persistency in the past has also been supported by the high surrender charges which act as a disincentive to surrender policies early. However, the recent guidelines have substantially increased the surrender values and has also enabled the payment of surrender provided one fullyear premium has been received. The insurers have introduced products with persistency linked clawbacks on the distributor commission to ensure minimal impact on persistency post the regulatory guidelines. 

**Operating Efficiency -** ICRA analyses the company’s operating expense ratio  including commissions) compared to the industry and peers. High operating expenses are also a function of the stage of growth of the company. A company which is in its initial stage of growth and is yet to achieve economies of scale is likely to incur higher operating expenses, which in turn also impacts the overall profitability. The scale of renewal premium is typically lower for such companies.

# **Capitalisation** 

The solvency ratio of an insurer is a measure of the capitalisation. The solvency ratio indicates the size of capital of a company relative to the risk it has undertaken. IRDAI has laid down the guidelines for computation of the solvency ratio. It is measured as the Available Solvency Margin (ASM) divided by the Required Solvency Margin (RSM). The RSM is computed segment wise and is a function of the mortality risk and the investment risk. Apart from the networth, the IRDAI has allowed issuance of subordinated debt (sub-debt) instruments upto a specified limit<sup>_3_</sup> which forms part of the ASM. 

While evaluating the insurer’s capital in relation to the risks in its business, ICRA focuses on the following aspects: 

- **Cushion over the regulatory capital requirement:** As per the IRDAI, an insurer is required to maintain a minimum solvency of  . 0x. ICRA assesses the capital cushion available over and above the regulatory requirement and the management’s stance on maintaining the same. A strong capital base supports the growth plans and also provides the necessary cushion against volatility in claim experience. 

- **Adequacy and sustainability of capital:** esides evaluating the insurer’s conformance to the regulatory capital requirements, ICRA assesses the adequacy of the capital in relation to its growth plans and its internal capital generation. ased on this, the insurer’s medium-term capital requirement is estimated. 

- **Ability to raise capital:** The insurer’s capital cushions can be enhanced by internal capital generation or by raising fresh equity capital or subordinated debt. ICRA evaluates the headroom available to raise subordinated debt. For private insurers, the ability to raise capital from the market or the promoters is also factored in. For public insurers, the capital support announced by the Government of India (GoI) for the sector is an important aspect.

# **Mortality risk and reinsurance** 

Mortality risk is one of the key risks which is underwritten by an insurance company. For a life insurer, mortality risk would entail the risk of demise of the policyholders before the expected lifespan or the demise of more policyholders than expected. The estimate of this risk and the reserving done for the same is on the basis of the mortality tables and involves actuarial estimates. The long-tail nature of the business could result in uncertainty regarding the quality of underwriting and hence the level of future claims in relation to the reserves made for mortality risk. A variance in the mortality rates can result into insufficiency of reserves and hence impact the profitability. This was also witnessed during the pandemic when the insurers had a negative variance in mortality thereby resulting into a negative accounting profitability and a decline in the EV. 

The insurers have a reinsurance programme designed to ensure protection against the mortality risks. The insurer cedes a certain portion of its mortality risk to reinsurers by entering into reinsurance contracts. The reinsurance is typically a function of the sum assured. For individual business, the reinsurance is typically on per life basis. Further, the life insurers have reinsurance programmes to protect against catastrophic events. Reinsurance is also used as a capital management strategy by the insurance companies and helps in increasing the capacity of the insurer in the underwriting business. ICRA assesses the insurer’s strategy on reinsurance, the adequacy of the networth against the retained sum assured and effectiveness of reinsurance.

# **Investment risk** 

The investment risk remains high in the case of non-par guaranteed portfolio where the returns are guaranteed by the insurer upfront while the premiums could be received over a period of time. These policies remain vulnerable to reinvestment risk. Any adverse experience in the investments for this segment is to be borne by the shareholders/ investors in hybrid instruments. Insurers do enter into forward rate agreements (FRAs) to hedge the interest rate risk to an extent. This allows the insurer to lock in the current interest rates at which the future premium to be received will be invested. For participating products, the returns are partly linked to investment performance, which are later declared as a bonus if the investment performance is good, and the guaranteed returns are at relatively lesser level in relation to the current interest rates. The linked segment on the other hand is entirely pass-through in nature. A life insurer earns a fund management fees for the linked segment and the profit/losses on the investment book are entirely passed through to the policyholders. Within the investment guidelines framed by IRDAI, individual companies construct their investment portfolio to align the maturity (debt instruments) of these

# **Liquidity** 

In a business-as-usual scenario, the claims payment and operating costs are typically met through the new premium inflows. However, in case of an increase in surrender, the premium inflows may not be sufficient to meet the claims and operating expenses. A higher benefit paid for surrender exposes the insurance company to liquidity risks. Liquidity management for an insurance company is thus to ensure that in case of any unexpected increase in surrender pay-outs or a shortfall in operating cash flows basis the premiums collection, benefits payouts and operating expenses are met through a similar maturity of debt instruments. ICRA assesses the policyholders’ liquidity based on the trends of net claims payments in relation to net premium written as well as share of liquid investments (Government securities) in their investment book. Generally, the operating cash flows of the insurer are expected to be positive after meeting the operating expenses, apart from the claim payments. To assess the liquidity for servicing the subordinated debt, ICRA assesses the debt servicing liability of the company in relation to its shareholders’ investments only.

# **Ownership/Parentage** 

The risk profile of an insurer, which is a part of a large corporate group or has an established promoter, can benefit from its strategic fit with the group or from the experience of the promoter. Moreover, companies backed by strong corporate groups or which have large institutions as strategic investors are expected to have adequate internal controls and processes and are, thus, expected to provide higher comfort. The insurer could also benefit with the promoter (bank or financial institution) sourcing business for the insurer. Over and above the standalone credit considerations, the likelihood of extraordinary support from the parent is factored in while assessing the insurer’s credit profile. This process involves the assessment of the ability and willingness of the parent to extend support in times of distress in addition to the evaluation of the insurer’s own fundamental credit strength. 

If the parent’s/group’s credit profile is weaker than the rated insurer, the insurer’s rating may be lower than what its standalone credit profile assessment would have merited. This is due to the possibility that the insurer may, at some point of time, be bound to extend financial support to its weaker parent, possibly to the detriment of its own credit profile<sup>4</sup> .

# **Assessment of ESG Risks** 

ICRA’s assessment of environmental, social and governance   SG  risks involves a broad range of considerations that pertains to the sustainability of an insurer, focussing on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be sector-related as well as insurer-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the governance risks are largely insurer-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an insurer’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally pull down the rating, but the ratings are usually not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environmental and social risks** 

While undertaking the credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions along with taking a forward-looking view on the risks and mitigants. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly and sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated insurer/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the sub-components of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap.

## **Governance risks** 

A sound corporate governance structure attempts to clarify the distinction of power and responsibilities between the board of directors and the management. The constitution of an insurer’s board and it’s adherence to legal and statutory compliance requirements are considered during the credit assessment. ICRA seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of the insurer’s commitment to following transparent and credible practices from the way its financial statements are reported, the level of disclosures, consistency in communication and openness about sharing information during the credit rating exercise. Besides the corporate group structure (whether simple or complex), the rated insurer’s related-party transactions and instances of supporting group entities at the expense of debtholders are assessed. Specifically, ICRA examines factors such as auditor qualifications, quality of disclosures, notes to accounts for evaluating the adequacy of accounting quality standards, and the internal audit process. 

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the insurer and the instruments being rated. ICRA arrives at this opinion by conducting a detailed evaluation of the insurer’s business and financial risks and using the evaluation to project its future financial performance in various scenarios. While several parameters are used to assess an insurer’s risk profile, the relative importance of each of these qualitative and quantitative parameters can vary across insurer depending on its potential to change the overall risk profile of the insurer concerned.

# **Risk associated with hybrid instruments** 

Hybrid instruments help insurance companies support their capitalisation and add to their solvency margins as they are included while calculating the available assets for solvency margin. These instruments are quite similar to the Upper Tier II instruments which were issued by banks under the  asel II guidelines. These instruments cannot be serviced if the insurer’s solvency margin (approach similar to the capital adequacy ratio of banks) falls below the minimum regulatory requirement. Additionally, the regulator’s approval is required to service the instrument if the company reports a net loss. As per the latest guidelines, prior approval of the regulator is required if solvency is below regulatory requirement. However, given the dependency on the regulatory approval for payment of coupon if the solvency is below the regulatory requirements, solvency and capital buffers maintained continues to the remain key criteria for rating of sub-debt. 

In a nutshell, the major risk associated with the instrument would be the insurance company’s solvency margin declining below the required regulatory level or the company incurring a net loss, which can constrain its ability to pay interest/dividend or redemption of the hybrid instruments. 

# **ICRA’s framework for rating hybrid instrument** 

ICRA assesses the issuer rating for an insurance company, which is the reflection of the insurance company’s ability to pay the policyholders claims and obligations and this becomes the basis for the anchor rating of the hybrid instrument. The insurer’s issuer rating, in addition to the standalone business and financial risk assessment, also takes into account its parentage, its strategic importance to the promoter group, and the ability and willingness of the parent to ensure that all regulatory requirements of the rated insurance company are met in a timely manner at all points in time. The rating of hybrid instruments is typically notched down from the insurer’s issuer rating as the obligation to the policyholders is senior to the obligation to the creditors. The notching down of the rating for the hybrid instrument would depend on the likelihood of these instruments not being serviced, which, in turn, would depend on the insurer’s solvency levels and profitability. 

To arrive at the rating of hybrid instruments – in other words, to determine the extent of notching from the anchor rating – the following parameters are analysed: 

- Company’s policy of keeping a buffer over the minimum required solvency margin and the historical track record of the same 

- Financial strength and flexibility of the promoter to bring in fresh capital and the importance of the subsidiary to the promoter 

- Size of the accumulated earnings or distributable reserves in case of ratings of preference share to ascertain their ability to service a dividend in a year of loss
