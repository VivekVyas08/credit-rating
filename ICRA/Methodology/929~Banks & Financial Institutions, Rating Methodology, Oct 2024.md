# **Overview** 

This methodology document covers ICRA’s approach towards assessing the credit quality of banks (including small finance banks (SFBs), regional rural banks (RRBs) and cooperative banks) and All India Financial Institutions (AIFIs). The initial section discusses the aspects covered to arrive at the standalone credit profile of the issuer. ICRA may notch up the standalone rating of the issuer (say for Government-owned institutions), based on the rating approach to factor in the implicit support from the <u>parent or the Group. This notched-up standalone credit rating will reflect the credit</u> opinion on the senior-most borrowings/debt instruments (like deposits, infrastructure bonds) of the issuer while the ratings for some of the debt instruments (like Tier I or Tier II bonds), eligible for inclusion in capital ratios, could be notched down from the senior rating as per the notching criteria discussed later in the document. 

### **ANALYST CONTACTS** 

ICRA’s credit rating for banks and AIFIs reflects its opinion on the relative exposure to the business and the financial risks faced by the entity while it incorporates the likelihood of the entity receiving extraordinary financial support from the parent (or an affiliate) in case of distress. A broad list of the rating factors that ICRA assesses while analysing banks is covered in this note. While these do not necessarily represent an exhaustive set of the factors considered by ICRA for assigning credit ratings, these are intended to provide an overall perspective to lenders, investors and other market participants on the parameters that are usually considered the most important. # **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Operating environment** 

The operating environment is one of the important parameters, which could affect a bank’s growth, asset quality and earnings. It reflects the prevailing economic conditions including liquidity conditions, interest rate cycle, economic growth prospects, and the outlook for deposits and credit growth. A favourable economic environment is generally characterised by high credit growth with stable funding and benign liquidity conditions, with expectations of less pressure on the asset quality and vice versa. This apart, the legal environment drives the pace of recoveries or losses upon default from the stressed assets. The outlook on the operating environment forms the basis of the assumptions for various operating parameters so as to arrive at a forward-looking credit opinion on the issuer. Besides the above, the structure of the financial market, its stage of development, and avenues for the borrowers to raise funds from other sources (like debt capital markets or overseas borrowing) form an important part of the evaluation of the operating environment for the sector. 

## **Regulatory environment** 

A well-regulated and supervised system is the backbone supporting the credibility and stability of banks. The regulatory environment has a bearing on how banks decide to run their operations in the context of (a) prudential norms relating to capital adequacy<sup>2</sup> , liquidity buffers, provisioning, asset classification, and loan restructuring, (b) policy changes in response to the evolving macro and liquidity conditions, including those relating to policy rates and money supply, (c) social or sectoral growth considerations that shape the norms on directed lending, (d) supervisory actions on individual banks and other financial institutions, (e) prospective regulatory changes driven by financial sector reforms as well as international environment/ learnings, and (f) competitive intensity alterations brought about by the issuance of new licences. 

This apart, the development of capital markets could enable borrowers to access these markets directly, leading to growth and profitability pressure on lenders. Overall, regulatory developments can potentially impact ICRA’s assessment and outlook on the asset quality, capital cushions, profitability, and liquidity of banks, which are among the key drivers of the rating outcome.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Franchise** 

The franchise strength of an entity determines its capacity to grow while maintaining a reasonable cost-to-income ratio and profitability, thereby providing stability to earnings. A strong customer franchise results in a granular asset and liability base, provides opportunities to garner a higher share of granular fee-based income, and results in better cross-selling ability. ICRA evaluates the franchise strength of an entity in terms of its scale of operations/market share and its presence across various financial services segments, which improves customer engagement and retention levels. Increasing digital presence is the way forward for banks, though the physical infrastructure of branches remains a focus area for the sustainable growth of a competitive liability base. For AIFIs, which largely have wholesale assets and liabilities, the asset and liability profile is driven by their lending mandates and the regulatory allocation of deposits from banks against their shortfall in priority sector lending (PSL) targets. Hence, this may not be applicable to AIFIs. 

## **Management, governance and information reporting** 

The quality of the management and governance and the information reporting process followed to cater to the stakeholders’ requirements are evaluated on the following parameters: 

### **Quality of management and governance** 

- **Constitution of board of directors:** A board consisting of qualified and independent members with effective oversight is a positive factor and is typically reflected in the operational and financial performance of the bank. 

- **Management depth and breadth:** The presence of qualified and experienced professionals and the track record of a stable top management are positive factors. Business outcomes vis-à-vis the guidance provided by the bank also become parameters for assessing the management profile. Managements with the ability to identify emerging risks and select the chosen areas of growth are considered favourably and vice versa. 

- **Established policies:** Established policies on factors such as capital cushions vis-à-vis regulatory levels, stressed asset provisioning beyond the regulatory minimum levels, calibrated growth while maintaining liquidity, and the granularity of assets and liabilities are some positive attributes. The demonstrated track record of adhering to these policies reflects favourably on the management. 

- **Strategy:** A well-laid out business plan with regular disclosures on strategy and progress on goal achievements are some positive factors. 

### **Information system and reporting** 

- **Quality of disclosures:** A well-established information system is a prerequisite for adequate disclosures. Some of the positive attributes of information disclosures include consistency and timeliness. When an entity publishes disclosures beyond the minimum regulatory requirements or conducts earnings calls regularly, it reflects well on the commitment to transparency. 

- **Financial reporting and systems:** A well-established information system also supports accuracy in financial reporting on a consistent basis and limits instances of regulatory non-compliance. Frequent instances of regulatory non-compliance or divergence in financial reporting vis-à-vis regulatory audits reflect weakness in the information systems and are negative attributes. 

- **Extent of divergence reported by the bank:** As per the Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) guidelines, banks are required to disclose the divergence between asset classification and provisioning if the additional provisioning requirements assessed by the RBI exceed 10% of the published profit before provisioning or the additional gross non-performing advances (NPAs) identified by the RBI exceed 15% of the published incremental gross NPAs during the reference period. A high level of divergence between asset classification and provisioning on a consistent basis weakens the reliability of the reported financials.

## **Risk management** 

The evaluation of the bank’s risk appetite and management is based on the following parameters: 

- **Credit risk:** A discussion is held with the management on the quality of the fresh exposures, the performance of the vulnerable<sup>3</sup> accounts and movements in the concentration of exposures. High slippage rates or high overdue levels could reflect weak risk management systems. 

- **Market risk:** During a volatile interest rate environment, a discussion is held with the management on the positioning of the investment portfolio as well as the sensitivity of the portfolio to interest rate movements. 

- **Operational risk:** Instances of fraud, the amount involved in such frauds, and corrective actions taken to strengthen the system are discussed. 

- **Extent of regulatory forbearance availed:** Frequent instances of availing regulatory forbearance (such as provisioning of certain expenses over longer periods) are considered unfavourably while assessing a bank.

# **Asset quality** 

A bank’s asset quality reflects its risk appetite, the depth of its franchise and the effectiveness of its management, reporting and risk management. The asset quality can affect the earnings (higher NPAs could dilute the yields and necessitate higher credit provisions) and capital (lower earnings could slow down internal capital generation or, in extreme situations (loss), could weaken the capital or impact the bank’s ability to raise capital from external investors). Asset quality evaluation includes the bank’s loan book as well as its non-statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) investment portfolio. The key aspects of asset quality evaluation are discussed below: 

- **Quality of exposures:** ICRA assesses the quality of the corporate/wholesale loan exposures basis the external ratings of these exposures and the credit rating. For granular loan exposures, ICRA can rely on factors (to the extent data is available) like the distribution of the credit bureau scores or the days past due (dpd) levels of the portfolio or the slippage rates in

Generally, lower RWAs/advances reflects a mix of better-quality advances in the loan book.

# **Diversity in funding and liquidity** 

ICRA analyses the funding profile of the bank in terms of the sources and mix of funds as well as the cost of funds to the bank along the following lines: 

**Deposit mix:** Current and savings accounts (CASA) are generally low-cost deposits. As CASA deposits tend to be sticky, a bank may see an improving CASA deposit ratio in a scenario of a decline in the overall deposit base. Hence, the CASA ratio is seen in conjunction with the absolute growth in the CASA deposit levels vis-à-vis the industry average. 

Other sources of deposits like certificates of deposit (CDs) and bulk deposits from corporate and institutional depositors are typically more volatile than retail and household deposits. A higher portion of retail deposits also lends stability to earnings as the cost of such deposits tends to be less volatile than bulk deposits. Thus, higher retail deposits in the total mix is viewed positively. With the deregulation of interest rates on SAs, some banks have been able to improve their CASA balances by offering higher interest rates on savings deposits. Such deposits may exhibit relatively higher volatility in terms of flow compared to normal SA deposits as these depositors may not be the bank’s core depositors. Accordingly, high CASA balances are seen in relation to the deposit rates offered. 

Banks are also entitled to avail long-term refinance against their eligible loan portfolios from AIFIs. The availability of such loan portfolios in the overall asset mix enhances the funding ability of the institution and adds diversity and stability to the funding. 

Although AIFIs do not raise deposits from the public at large, given their quasi-sovereign status, they are able to raise funds from wholesale markets at competitive rates. While wholesale funding markets may be volatile in terms of pricing and quantum of funds, AIFIs have demonstrated fund-raising abilities even during volatile market conditions as they are typically preferred by investors in such situations. Further, these funds are non-callable and some AIFIs receive funding by way of deposits from other banks as these banks fail to meet their PSL targets. Such deposits are allocated by the Government of India (GoI)/RBI to various AIFIs and form a sizeable part of their overall liabilities. Such funding also imparts a superior funding profile to AIFIs as the same is driven by regulatory allocations at a lower cost. 

**Deposit rate comparison with peers:** A bank could offer higher deposit rates vis-à-vis peers either in a scenario where its liability franchise is not able to deliver a growth matching the growth of its assets or the deposit franchise itself is weak and a higher deposit rate proposition is the only tool to attract depositors. In such scenarios, the quality of the deposits may be less stable and could adversely impact the liquidity of the bank in times of stress. Further, with a higher cost of funds, the bank may be required to take additional exposure to risky assets to generate sufficient return on equity for its shareholders. 

- **Cost of interest-bearing funds:** The cost of interest-bearing funds is determined by the mix of deposits (current, savings and time deposits) and other borrowings of the bank. The tenure of the deposits and the bank’s franchise/market standing influence the interest rate structure for its deposits and borrowings. Other factors include the bank’s reliance on bulk deposits, money market funding (from call money markets and CDs), and the current and expected money market conditions. The cost of funds is compared with the industry averages and a lower cost of funds in relation to the averages is seen positively.

ICRA attempts to capture the liquidity of a bank by analysing the following qualitative and quantitative parameters: 

- **Bank’s SLS:** ICRA evaluates the structural liquidity statement (SLS) of the bank (often across time periods) to analyse the overall mismatches between the maturity profile of its assets and liabilities across various time buckets. Banks with a longer asset maturity profile will require longer-tenure liabilities to reduce the maturity gaps. 

- **LCR**<sup>**6**</sup> **:** Banks are required to maintain a liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) in accordance with the RBI’s guidelines. The LCR is a measure of the short-term resilience of banks to potential liquidity disruptions by ensuring that they have sufficient highquality liquid assets (HQLAs) to survive an acute stress scenario lasting for 30 days. The trends in a bank’s LCR are analysed for comfort on the liquidity position. 

- **NSFR**<sup>7</sup> **:** The net stable funding ratio (NSFR) is a measure of the resilience of banks to potential funding disruptions over the next one year by requiring banks to fund their activities with more stable sources of funding on an ongoing basis. 

- **Credit-to-deposit ratio:** The credit-to-deposit ratio is adjusted to include the net worth in the denominator. An adjusted credit-to-deposit ratio with an increasing trend is a reflection of the credit growth outpacing the deposit growth and the consumption of on-balance sheet liquidity (in the form of excess SLR investments) for credit growth. Hence, a high adjusted credit-to-deposit ratio reflects limited on-balance sheet liquid assets in the form of excess SLR investments, whereas a low credit-to-deposit ratio may be an outcome of the inability to lend because of capital constraints or otherwise.

# **Profitability, earnings stability and prospects** 

A bank’s ability to generate adequate returns is important from the perspective of its shareholders as well as debtholders. Adequate operating profitability helps in absorbing credit costs and other losses emanating from the various risks that a bank is exposed to. The bank’s historical performance is analysed for the stability and quality of earnings. The goal is to assess the level of the bank’s future earnings and the quality/volatility of the earnings by analysing its interest spreads, fee income, operating expenses and credit costs. 

The future profitability of a bank is evaluated by analysing its interest spreads (yields on various assets minus cost of funds) and the likely trajectory of the same in light of the changes in its operating environment, its liquidity position, its status on PSL requirements, the loan mix and its overall strategy. Most banks usually have floating assets and liabilities, thereby reducing the interest rate risk to a large extent. However, an entity with a sizeable share of fixed rate assets or liabilities may also be exposed to interest rate risk, especially in a volatile interest rate environment. 

A bank failing to meet its PSL requirements is required to invest in priority sector lending certificates (PSLCs) issued by other banks or in Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF) deposits at much lower yields. ICRA also assesses the bank’s ability to complement its interest income with fee income. A large fee income allows greater diversification, which can improve the

ICRA also assesses the capital cushion available over and above the regulatory requirement and the management’s stance on maintaining the same. 

- **Adequacy and sustainability of capital:** Besides evaluating the bank’s conformance to the regulatory capital requirements, ICRA assesses the adequacy of the capital in relation to its growth plans, its internal capital generation, its present and prospective asset quality, which may impact the capitalisation, and its risk appetite. Based on this, the bank’s mediumterm capital requirement is estimated. A stress analysis is done at various growth levels and potential asset stress scenarios are evaluated to estimate internal capital generation and capital requirements, if any. 

- **Ability to raise capital:** The bank’s capital cushions can be enhanced by internal capital generation or by raising fresh equity capital or hybrid instruments. ICRA evaluates the bank’s internal capital generating capacity and its ability and flexibility to augment the capital to support growth or withstand stress. For public sector banks (PSBs)/AIFIs, the capital support announced by the GoI for the sector is an important aspect. Also, considering that the GoI’s shareholding is required to be at least 51%, the bank’s ability to meet its capital requirement without any dependence on the GoI is analysed. 

For private sector banks (PVBs), the ability to meet the capital requirements from the markets is analysed. This is typically measured by assessing the extent of capital requirement in relation to the bank’s current market capitalisation. A high level of capital requirement in relation to the market capitalisation can pose a challenge in raising capital. Generally, a low price-tobook value or a weak track record of shareholder returns can weigh on the bank’s capital-raising ability. A bank can also look at other options such as the divestment of non-core investments and reduction of RWAs to meet its capital requirements. 

- Additional Tier 1 (AT1) instruments under Basel III (or Basel III-Tier I) are debt capital instruments with loss-absorption features and comprise the Tier I capital of banks under Basel III regulations. Due to the loss-absorption features of these instruments and the restriction on coupon payments, which are allowed either from the current year’s profits or the reserves created through the appropriation of profits, the bank’s profitability, distributable reserves and capitalisation profile determine the attractiveness of these bonds to investors. Weak performance on these parameters may constrain the bank’s ability to raise AT1 bonds, which means it has to meet the Tier I capital requirements through CET I. Hence, there would be a higher need for raising core capital. 

- **Quality of capital:** A higher percentage of core Tier I capital (more specifically CET I capital) is viewed more favourably, given its greater permanence. ICRA also analyses any other potential sources of flexibility (such as unrealised gains on the investment book, revaluation reserves on fixed assets), which may help boost the capital. 

- **Solvency profile:** This is calculated as the extent of the bank’s net NPAs in relation to its tangible net worth or CET I. It measures the capital coverage for the unprovided portion of the bank’s NPAs. For banks with meaningfully high levels of net non-performing investments (net NPIs) or net security receipts (net SRs) issued by asset reconstruction companies (ARCs), the solvency profile is measured by considering these in conjunction with the net NPAs in relation to the CET I. 

- **Leverage ratio:** The leverage ratio is defined as Total Tier I capital/Total exposure. Total exposure includes on-balance sheet and off-balance sheet exposures adjusted for credit conversion factors. The minimum leverage ratio is 4.0% for domestic systemically important banks (D-SIBs) and 3.5% for other banks as permitted by the RBI. Thus, banks operating at a leverage ratio above the minimum requirement indicates the availability of more capital against exposures. Hence, a higher ratio is better from a credit perspective. 

- **Performance of subsidiaries:** ICRA assesses the risk of financial support, which may need to be extended by the bank to its subsidiaries. The support requirement may be driven by the legal or the publicly perceived moral obligation of a parent to support a subsidiary. While weak-performing subsidiaries may be a drag on a bank’s financial position, well-performing subsidiaries or investments can provide a cushion to a bank’s profitability in events of stress. The capital requirements of the subsidiaries, either for growth or in a stress situation, are factored in while taking a forward-looking view on the entity.

# **Promoter Support and Financial Flexibility** 

Over and above the standalone credit considerations, the likelihood of extraordinary support from the parent is factored in while assessing the entity’s credit profile. This process involves the assessment of the ability and willingness of the parent to extend support in times of distress in addition to the evaluation of the entity’s own fundamental credit strength. 

The Indian banking system consists of PSBs, PVBs, co-operative banks, RRBs, SFBs, payments banks and subsidiaries/branches of foreign banks. While ICRA draws comfort from the sovereign ownership of PSBs, the bank’s ability to raise capital from promoters/other key shareholders, as and when required, is an important credit driver. A PSB with GoI shareholding well in excess of 51% is viewed positively by ICRA as it would have greater flexibility in raising capital by diluting the GoI shareholding. 

For RRBs, there are specified shareholding requirements from the GoI, the state government and the sponsor bank (typically a PSB). Hence, capital raising is dependent on the ability of all the shareholders to put in their requisite share of capital. 

Certain PVBs and SFBs could be required to maintain minimum promoter shareholding for a certain period or dilute the promoter shareholding to the agreed levels, as per the roadmap for receiving approval from the RBI for a banking licence. Hence, the entity’s ability to raise capital, if required, could be driven by these shareholding norms. 

Foreign banks have been operating in India as a subsidiary of a foreign bank/financial institution or as branches of the bank itself. As the branches of foreign banks are an integral part of the entity incorporated overseas, ICRA’s opinion on their ratings is largely driven by the global balance sheet of the foreign bank itself. Funding support from the head office, by way of liquidity or capital to meet the local requirement, and the importance and oversight of the bank on its Indian branches are additional points analysed while rating these banks. For foreign banks that are operating domestically through a subsidiary model, ICRA assesses the standalone credit profile basis this methodology document and may notch up the standalone rating based on the <u>rating approach to factor in the implicit support from the parent or the Group.</u> 

Apart from ownership, the bank’s importance in the domestic financial system has a bearing on the possibility of Government support in times of financial stress. However, such support has remained limited for private institutions in the past. In addition to the balance sheet size, proxies for a bank’s systemic importance could include the share of business in the region of operations, participation in payment systems and the scale of quasi-fiscal responsibilities (such as directed lending) carried out for the Government.

# **Assessment of ESG Risks** 

ICRA’s assessment of environmental, social and governance (ESG) risks involves a broad range of considerations that pertains to the sustainability of a bank with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally pull down the rating, but the ratings are usually not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environmental and social risks** 

While undertaking the credit assessment of banks and financial institutions, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions along with taking a forward-looking view on the risks and mitigants. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly and sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the subcomponents of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is to assess the direct and indirect risks faced by a bank or a financial institution and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. While banks and financial institutions do not face material physical climate risks, they are exposed to environmental risks indirectly through their portfolio of assets. If the entities or businesses, to which banks and financial institutions have exposure, face business disruptions because of physical climate adversities or if such businesses face climate transition risks because of technological, regulatory or customer behaviour changes, the same could translate into credit risks for the rated banks and financial institutions. However, such risk is unlikely to be material because banks and financial institutions typically benefit from portfolio diversification. Further, the lending is usually for short-to-medium-term tenures, which would allow banks and financial institutions to adapt while taking incremental exposures on less environmentally vulnerable businesses. 

With regard to social risks, data security and customer privacy are among the key sources of vulnerability for banks and financial institutions as material lapses could be detrimental to their reputation and could invite regulatory censure. ICRA evaluates the disclosures made by banks and financial institutions outlining the key policies, processes, and investments made to control the occurrence of such instances. Mis-selling and other types of misconduct are other social risks that could result in fines and reputational harm. Customer preference is increasingly shifting towards digital banking, providing an opportunity to reduce operating costs. However, the possibility of subpar execution of the information technology strategy and the consequent inability to adequately meet customer needs could result in costs than benefits. On the positive side, the financial services sector contributes to enhancing financial inclusion. Prudent lending to such underserved segments could create growth opportunities. Overall, societal trends are seen to have a mostly modest impact on the credit quality of banks and financial institutions. 

## **Governance risks** 

A sound corporate governance structure attempts to clarify the distinction of power and responsibilities between the board of directors and the management. The constitution of an entity’s board and the entity’s adherence to legal and statutory compliance requirements are considered during the credit assessment. ICRA seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of the entity’s commitment to following transparent and credible practices from the way its financial statements are reported, the level of disclosures, consistency in communication and openness about sharing information during the credit rating exercise. Besides the corporate group structure (whether simple or complex), the rated entity’s related-party transactions and instances of supporting group entities at the expense of debtholders are assessed. The board of directors of PSBs, in which the GoI has majority ownership, could largely be appointed by the GoI. Therefore, the regulator could have limited control over PSBs compared to PVBs. The multiple objectives of the GoI as the owner of PSBs and the complex principal-agent relationship cannot be wished away where PSBs are concerned.

# **Guidelines for Rating Various Capital Instruments** 

## **Basel II instruments** 

Prior to the RBI’s January 2006 announcement of the guidelines for the use of new instruments for capital raising by banks, apart from internal generation, the only options available to Indian banks to shore up their capital were equity issues and subordinated debt. Detailed <u>guidelines were issued in January 2006</u> for the raising of capital via the following capital instruments: 

- Innovative capital instruments for inclusion in Tier I or innovative perpetual debt instruments (IPDI; referred to as Basel IITier I in this note) 

- Hybrid debt instruments for inclusion in Tier II (referred to as Basel II-Upper Tier II in this note) 

A comparison between the Basel II-Lower Tier II instruments and the new instruments, Basel II-Tier I and Basel II-Upper Tier II, has been presented below. The rating for Basel II-Lower Tier II instruments is equated to the base rating or the rating of the senior-most debt instrument of the bank due to the following reasons: 

- Basel II-Lower Tier II instruments are free from the deferral clauses for debt servicing, i.e. even if the capital adequacy is below the regulatory minimum or losses have been incurred, banks do not face any constraint in servicing the conventional instruments. In India and most other markets, banks are observed to be more willing to avoid defaults (in most cases, systemic support is also extended by sovereign authorities) to prevent reputational risk (or even systemic instability). 

- There is not much difference in the superiority of claims between senior unsecured debt and Basel II-Lower Tier II. Hence, these instruments are rated at par with the issuer rating of the bank. 

- Lower Tier II issuances are of a relatively shorter-term maturity (usually 10 years) in comparison to Basel II-Tier I (perpetual) and Basel II-Upper Tier II (15 years), implying that these are less permanent forms of capital. 

While these instruments have been phased out for scheduled commercial banks required to follow Basel III, they can be issued by banks like SFBs, RRBs, etc, which have not transitioned to Basel III.

The RBI guidelines on Basel III introduce stringent loss-absorption clauses for hybrid instruments so that loss absorption kicks in before the public injection of funds. While both Tier I and Tier II instruments have significant loss-absorption features, Tier I instruments are meant to absorb losses on a going-concern basis, which means the loss-absorption trigger kicks in early. The high loss-absorption features of Tier I are likely to bail out depositors as well as investors in Tier II instruments well ahead of stress. The triggers for the Tier II instruments, which also have loss-absorption features, are meant to be invoked at the point of non-viability (PONV)<sup>12</sup> , and are, therefore, likely to protect depositors and senior lenders on a gone-concern basis.

However, ICRA also notes that there have been precedents of various regulatory relaxations as well as support from the GoI to ensure that the PSBs continue to service their Basel III-Tier I bonds on an ongoing basis. Some of these instances are: 

- Changes in definition of distributable reserves by RBI in February 2017 

- Declaration of inclusion in PCA as a regulatory event and consequently the early exercise of the call option on the Basel III-Tier I bond by weaker PSBs in 2018

The issuer ratings of PSBs factor in an uplift from their standalone credit profile based on ownership. While it could be argued that the Basel III-Tier I bonds should be notched down from the standalone credit profile of the PSBs, the ratings on these Tier I bonds will be notched down from the supported credit ratings of the PSBs, given the multiple precedents of extraordinary support. 

Although larger PVBs have typically maintained better asset quality, superior and stable profitability and excess capital, the probability of a drop in the CET I level to 8% is relatively higher than the likelihood of default on conventional instruments. Considering these riskier features, ICRA would notch down the ratings for the Basel III-Tier I instruments from conventional instruments for both PSBs and PVBs. ICRA’s assessment of the level of notching considers various factors, including: 

- ICRA’s assessment of the asset quality, past trends and the outlook for the core profitability 

- Bank’s level of distributable reserves 

- Bank’s relative ranking on its expected capitalisation levels under stress testing scenarios 

- Bank’s track record and philosophy on maintaining excess capital 

- Bank’s franchise, relative standing in the equity market, its demonstrated ability in attracting fresh equity, and the quality and diversity of its investors 

The probability of reporting losses and the consequent erosion of capital and distributable reserves of banks (both public and private) with relatively higher scores on the above parameters would be much lower, and therefore, the notching of the rating could be correspondingly less. However, banks scoring relatively low on these parameters would have a relatively higher probability of reporting losses and the consequent erosion of capital and distributable reserves. Therefore, the notching of the rating in such cases could be higher. 

**CCB:** According to the Basel III guidelines, banks are required to maintain a CCB of 2.5%, created out of the core equity (by September 30, 2021 in a phased manner<sup>_16_</sup> ), which is above the regulatory minimum CET I requirement of 5.5%. The CCB is required to be maintained across all the regulatory capital levels, i.e. CET I, Tier I and CRAR. There are restrictions on the distribution of capital on some elements (i.e. paying dividend, coupon on Basel III-Tier I bonds, share buybacks or bonus in any form, among others) in case the conservation capital level falls below 2.5%. For instance, if the CCB falls to 2% (or CET I falls to 5.5% + 2%), the bank has to conserve 40% of its earnings<sup>_17_</sup> , and payouts to ‘elements subjected to discretion’ can be made only to the extent of 60% of the earnings.

# **Key Ratios Used for Bank Analysis**<sup>18</sup> 

## **Bank Profile** 

**Total asset base** : Scale of operations **Net worth** : Net worth as reported by the bank (excluding revaluation reserves) **Net income** : Net income as reported by the bank **Age of the bank** : Number of years the bank has been in operation 

## **Capital Adequacy** 

**Net worth as a percentage of assets** : The period-end balance of equity and reserves (excluding revaluation reserves) as a percentage of the period-end balance of total assets **Capital formation rate** : Net income less cash dividends declared as a percentage of net worth at the beginning of the year **CRAR, Tier I, CET I** : Regulatory capitalisation ratios as reported by the bank **Net NPA/Tangible net worth (solvency)** : Total NPAs less period-end balance of provisions for NPAs as a percentage of tangible net worth (or core equity capital) of the bank adjusted for revaluation reserves, accumulated losses and other deferred expenses 

## **Resources** 

**Deposit growth rate** : Increase in deposits as a percentage of previous period-end balance **Composition of deposits** : Mix of deposits like term, retail term, savings and current **Demographic profile of deposits** : Mix of deposits according to branch classification (urban, rural, metropolitan) **Concentration of deposits** : Share of top 20 deposits and large-ticket deposits **Asset Quality NPA as a percentage of credit** : Total NPAs as a percentage of period-end credit **Stressed advances** :   Gross NPA percentage + Standard restructured advances + Standard assets under various forbearance schemes + SR **Gross NPA generation rate** : Fresh NPAs added as a percentage of opening stock of performing assets **Net NPA generation rate** : Fresh NPAs less recoveries, upgrades and write-offs as a percentage of opening stock of performing assets **Provision coverage ratio** : The period-end balance of the provision for credit losses as a percentage of total NPAs **Loan growth** : The annualised change in period-end total loans as a percentage of the previous period-end balance
