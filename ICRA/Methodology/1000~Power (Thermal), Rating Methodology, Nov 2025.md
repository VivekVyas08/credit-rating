This rating methodology describes ICRA’s approach to assess the credit risk of thermal power producers. It aims to help issuers, investors and other market participants understand ICRA’s approach to analysing risks that are likely to affect the rating outcomes for the companies in the thermal power sector. This rating methodology updates and supersedes ICRA's earlier methodology document on this subject, published in November 2023. While this revised version incorporates a few additional modifications and editorial changes, ICRA's overall approach to rating entities in the sector remains materially similar. 

# **Overview** 

The power sector has witnessed large-scale investments in the generation segment, with the installed power generation capacity increasing to 501 GW (gigawatt) as on September 30, 2025, from 425 GW as on September 30, 2023, and doubling from March 2014 levels of 245 GW. Thermal power capacity increased to 245 GW as on September 30, 2025, from 239 GW as on September 30, 2023. Increased generation has reduced the demand supply deficit over the years. The peak power deficit decreased to 0.1% of the peak demand during 7M FY2026, as compared to 4% during FY2023. The increase in capacity was mainly driven by private sector investments in the thermal power segment during the period leading up to March 2017, followed by the renewable energy (RE) segment in the subsequent period. While the installed capacity of thermal power plant accounted for ~52% of the total installed capacity, it contributed to ~75% of the total power generation in the country in FY2025. 

The thermal power segment faced significant challenges during the 2014-2021 period. This was mainly because of the lack of progress in signing long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) by state distribution utilities (discoms), unviable tariffs under PPAs with discoms, owing to escalation in capital cost and higher-thanenvisaged fuel costs and payment delays by discoms. Inadequate fuel supply from domestic sources – mainly for gas-based power plants – and financing challenges in completing the stuck projects were other hurdles, which limited fresh capacity addition. These factors had led to a large stressed-asset portfolio in the thermal power segment. This, along with the policy focus on renewables to meet the climate transition goals, had led to a slowdown in fresh investments in thermal capacities in the past.

While the thermal plant load factor (PLF) was on a declining trend between FY2011 and FY2021, it reversed since FY2022, led by the strong recovery in electricity demand growth. This trend is expected to continue in the near term, led by the healthy demand growth and limited capacity addition in the thermal power segment. Unlike solar or wind, thermal plants provide continuous and stable power and are essential for meeting base load and grid stability. Rapid growth in renewables (especially solar and wind) without adequate energy storage systems has led to grid instability due to their intermittent and variable nature, which can lead to voltage and frequency fluctuations, necessitating the need to increase the thermal capacity. This has also prompted a rethink by the Government on thermal capacity addition, with it looking to encourage investments in new thermal power projects.

The recovery in demand growth has also led to signing of new long- and medium-term PPAs for thermal power projects. On the other hand, the credit profile of central power generation utilities is supported by the presence of long-term PPAs under cost-plus tariff principles, sovereign ownership and strong payment security, enabling regular recovery of payments from discoms.

# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Regulatory Risk** 

The power sector is governed by regulators at the Central as well as the state level, wherein the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) is responsible for specifying the regulatory framework within the terms of the legislation, which acts as a guide to the State Electricity Regulatory Commissions (SERCs). The CERC is responsible for determining the tariff for the Central sector utilities and where inter-state sale and transmission of power are involved. The SERCs are responsible for framing regulations governing the generation, transmission and distribution of electricity at the state level and determining the tariff for state utilities and the tariff payable by the consumers to the distribution utility. 

In case of Central or state-owned generation utilities, where the tariffs are cost-plus based, the risks arise from the timeliness as well as adequacy of tariff determination by the regulators. Also, the revisions in normative tariff principles and their impact on the financial profile of the power generation companies with cost-plus tariffs could be another risk. In case of a change in law and force majeure events impacting power generation projects, including competitively bid-based projects, the timeliness and adequacy of tariff revisions by the regulators is important. 

In addition, the regulatory environment in terms of timeliness and adequacy of tariff determination by the SERCs for state distribution utilities remains significant. Any delays in the tariff determination process (which can be either be attributed to either delays in petition filing by the discom or in issuance of tariff order by SERC) or non-implementation (or roll-back by state government) of either the tariff order or fuel and power purchase cost adjustment framework is a credit concern. This is because the same has an adverse impact on the cash flows of the distribution utilities, which in turn increases the counterparty credit risks for power generation projects. 

Further, there have been certain regulatory interventions in the past by the Government of India to ensure adequate fuel availability through preferential supply of domestic coal and blending of costlier imported coal with cost pass-through arrangements. In such instances, ensuring timely pass-through of any additional cost of fuel to the discoms by the regulators remains key. Also, if any directive is issued to mandatorily run power plants like the order issued by the Central and state governments in May 2022 to run power plants under Section 11 of Electricity Act (to ensure adequate availability of power for the period between May 2022 to June 2025) it is important that the tariffs are timely revised by the Governments to reflect the cost. 

### **Compliance with Emission Norms** 

The Ministry of Environment, Forests and Climate Change (MoEF), Government of India, notified the revised emission norms for thermal power projects on December 7, 2015. While it has been close to ten years since the notification, the overall progress in complying with these norms remains slow, with many of the thermal power plants yet to firm up plans for upgrading the emission control infrastructure (feasibility studies, identifying suppliers and financing mechanism). As per the notification issued by the MoEF in December 2015, the projects were categorised based on location and environmental impact by setting different compliance timelines for each category. Given the challenges faced by the power producers related to regulatory approvals and financing, the timeline has been subsequently extended multiple times. As per the MoEF’s notification in July 2025, the Category A plants (located within 10 kms of National Capital Region and cities with population of more than 10 lakh) are required to comply with the norms by December 31, 2027 (timeline extended from December 31, 2024). The Central Government will decide on a case-by-case basis if the emission norms apply to Category B plants near critically polluted areas or non-attainment cities, and this decision will be made by December 31, 2028, replacing the previous December 31, 2025 deadline. Category C plants, i.e. all others not falling under Category A or B, have been completely exempted from these norms, provided they meet the prescribed stack height criteria. These plants were earlier required to comply to the SO2 norms by December 31, 2026.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

Only the greenfield or expansion projects are exposed to permitting, funding and construction risks. 

## **Permitting Risk** 

Permitting risk refers to the risk pertaining to an entity’s ability to secure all statutory clearances required for constructing and operating a power plant, as well as complying with all the applicable environmental norms. ICRA evaluates issues related to land acquisition, rehabilitation and resettlement and examines the status of various statutory clearances required in accordance with the laws of the land. Typically, these clearances are required from multiple agencies, like the MoEF, state pollution control board, state irrigation department, Ministry of Civil Aviation and so on. As the process of acquiring the various clearances is vulnerable to delays, permitting risk tends to be the major focus area while assigning ratings to greenfield or expansion projects. An established track record of the sponsor in developing power generation projects of similar size and technology could provide comfort while assessing such risks. Within the thermal segment, the permitting risk for gas-based projects is lower compared to coal-based projects, given that gas is a more environment-friendly fuel and the land required for such projects is also relatively smaller. 

## **Funding Risk** 

The entity’s ability to tie up the requisite finances, status of the funding tie-up and capital structure mix are the focus of analysis here. Given the capital-intensive nature of power generation projects and the typical debt-equity ratio of 70:30 or 75:25 adopted by the developers, such projects tend to have a high leverage. The capital cost and capital structure are evaluated in relation to other thermal power projects. The average cost of debt and the foreign exchange component in debt (if any) are also considered. While the sponsor would arrange the equity, the developer would be dependent on banks, financial institutions (FIs) and bond markets for debt funding. Often, while in-principle sanctions by banks/FIs are in place, the final disbursements may be delayed owing to the entity’s inability to fulfil the pre-disbursement conditions specified by the lenders, including obtaining the statutory clearances and tying up fuel supply agreements, among others. In this context, the entity’s ability to comply with such conditions in a timely manner remains critical in alleviating the funding risk. ICRA considers the extent to which the funding is already in place and the likelihood of the remaining funding being available in time, so that the project’s progress is not delayed for lack of funding. The strength of the sponsors is an important risk mitigant, even though project finance is generally ‘non-recourse’ in nature. The strength of the sponsor would also impart financial flexibility in funding cost overruns or other contingencies. 

## **Construction Risk** 

Construction risks refer to the risks associated with the physical construction of the power plant as well as stabilisation of its operating parameters. ICRA also evaluates the construction risks associated with the implementation of the associated auxiliary infrastructure, including the power evacuation line for connectivity with the designated grid sub-station of the transmission utility, the water intake system and railway siding for coal transportation. Delays in the construction of either the power plant or the associated auxiliary infrastructure can lead to time and cost overruns. Fixed-price, fixed-time contracts, with adequate clauses for liquidated damages (LD) are usually the mitigating factors against the construction risk. However,

# **Operating Risk** 

The operating risks for an entity engaged in thermal power generation arise from the uncertainty over fuel supply, water availability, inadequate transmission capacity and the possibility of plant performance being lower than the normative parameters, as specified in the PPA. In terms of physical operations, the power project would need to at least attain the PPAstipulated norms for plant availability, heat rate and auxiliary consumption to avoid any under-recovery in revenues. These risks get mitigated to the extent that they are borne by the O&M contractor (through relevant LD clauses). Further, ICRA evaluates the impact of revision in environmental norms on thermal power projects and their ability to comply with these norms in a timely manner. In view of the rising share of RE in the electricity generation mix in India and the ‘must run’ status accorded to the RE plants, the coal-based power generation projects are likely to face frequent back-down requests and stoppages, especially in states with high RE capacity. This could impact the operating efficiency of thermal plants, resulting in suboptimal station heat rate and auxiliary consumption. Against this backdrop, ICRA favourably considers coal-based plants that make the relevant investments to achieve flexible operating capabilitiessuch as faster ramp-up and ramp-down rates, improved part-load efficiency, among others—since these measures enable better integration with variable renewable energy generation. 

The ability of a thermal power project to meet the stipulated availability norms would depend on the availability of adequate fuel supply and water supply. Also, non-availability of transmission open access would hinder supply to the project’s customers. 

<u>Fuel availability and pricing risk: An assessment of fuel supply risks involves the evaluation of the sources of supply, distance</u> from the source, reserve availability, contractual obligation of the seller and the price of supply. ICRA evaluates the adequacy of the fuel supply contracted against the fuel required to declare plant availability at a normative level. For projects that have won captive coal blocks through competitive bidding, ICRA focusses on the ability of the entity to ensure that the actual coal mining volumes are in line with their requirements and the actual cost of mining (sum of operating and capital costs) is within the quoted bid price for the mine won in the auction. Inadequate supply from the contracted sources or captive mines exposes the projects to volume and pricing risk in the open market (e-auction by Coal India Limited and imported coal).

# **Asset diversification** 

Thermal power projects are typically large, single-location plants, which heightens asset concentration risk and exposes operations to site-specific challenges such as fuel supply disruptions, water availability, transportation bottlenecks, and local regulatory or environmental issues. Force majeure events—like natural disasters or regional unrest—can further impact generation and cash flows. In case of projects are spread across multiple locations in different states, ICRA draws comfort from the geographical diversity of the portfolio as it provides cushion to the cash flows in case of any stress in any of the assets. Other mitigants also include comprehensive insurance coverage for assets and business interruption, force majeure relief under long-term PPAs (such as timeline extensions or penalty waivers), and diversified fuel supply arrangements, which collectively provide some cushion against operational and financial stress.

# **Demand and Tariff Risks** 

<u>PPA mix: While evaluating the demand risk, ICRA analyses the PPAs tied up by the project (long-, medium- and short-term</u> PPAs) and the nature of the PPAs. Long-term PPAs can be cost-plus based or competitively bid-based, while medium- and short-term PPAs can either be competitively bid-based or based on mutually agreed terms in case of bulk industrial consumers. The IPPs may also sell at spot tariff rates on the power exchange. The demand risk is normally mitigated by the presence of long-term PPAs with availability-linked capacity charge payments. In case of medium-term PPAs, ICRA evaluates the tenure and the tariff structure (either single-part or two-part tariff structure) of the PPA and the ability of the company to renew the PPA. Projects without long-term PPAs remain exposed to volume and price risks in the short-term market. 

<u>Cost competitiveness: Apart from the availability of PPA, the cost competitiveness of the tariff offered remains an important</u> component of risk assessment for a power generation project. As per the distribution tariff regulatory framework, power procurement by distribution utilities is approved by the SERCs based on the merit order dispatch principles. The projected variable cost of generation for a power plant and its likely position in the merit order dispatch list of the utilities reflects the cost competitiveness of the project, wherein the power plants with the highest variable costs are given least priority. A generating entity with a competitive cost structure can explore other avenues for sale of power, including sales to commercial and industrial customers. However, this exposes the entity to the risk of variations in open-access regulations and tariff. Further, there is a proposal to move to a market-based economic dispatch, with discoms procuring power from the lowestcost producers through centralisation of power scheduling at an all-India level. This could benefit the power-generating companies having competitive cost of generation and without long-term PPAs currently. 

- <u>Extent of fuel cost pass through: ICRA also analyses the fuel price pass-through conditions in the PPA. Unlike cost-plus based</u> projects, where the fuel cost is a pass-through, subject to achieving the normative operating parameters, the extent of the pass-through of costs in a competitively bid-based PPA varies depending on the bid structure. Projects with competitively bidbased PPAs, where the fuel cost is largely a pass-through based on the quoted heat rate, are relatively less risky. On the other hand, projects with energy charges quoted in PPA as non-escalable or partially escalable remain exposed to the risk of underrecovery in fuel cost. 

Further, ICRA evaluates the impact of increasing the cost of compliance towards environmental norms on the competitiveness of thermal power projects, especially in the context of the revised emission standards notified by the MoEF, Government of India, for thermal power projects. ICRA assesses the ability of the projects with long-term PPAs to pass on the increased cost to the off-takers under the change in law.

## **Counterparty Credit Risk** 

In assessing counterparty credit risk, ICRA evaluates the financial position of the counterparty, track record of payments for operational projects and the strength of the payment security mechanism (PSM). Most long-term PPAs with state-owned distribution utilities provide for a two-tier PSM, consisting of a letter of credit (LC) and/or an escrow arrangement. While the structure of the PSM provides comfort, the track record of implementation of such a mechanism is evaluated for operational projects. ICRA also assesses the availability of the right for third-party sale of power for the project, in case of delays exceeding a certain limit. For projects where the PPA is signed with an intermediary procurer (central nodal agency or a trader), which in turn signs the PSA with the ultimate off-takers which are state-owned distribution utilities, ICRA examines key clauses under the PSA related to tariff payment (whether it is back to back) and payment security available such as LCs. 

For operational projects, ICRA focusses on the timelines in cash collections from the off-takers and the extent of compliance of PSM in line with the terms of the PPA. ICRA favourably views the realisation of payments within the PPA-stipulated timelines from the off-takers. For under-construction projects, ICRA analyses the track record of payments by the off-taking utility to other IPPs to understand how the payment flow might be to the rated under-construction project when it begins operations. 

The credit quality of the state-owned distribution utility is linked with the intrinsic credit quality of the state government, the distribution utility’s financial position can be determined from its trends in cost coverage ratio, adequacy of subsidy release by the relevant state governments, the extent of cost-reflective tariffs and the ability to improve the efficiency levels and keep actual costs in line with regulatory targets. If the entity is dependent on a limited number of counterparties, the rating of the entity would be linked to the counterparties’ credit profile, unless there are mitigating factors such as strong liquidity buffer and presence of a strong parent/promoter group which is expected to provide support to the entity, in case the need arises.

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

## **Adequacy of Future Cash Flows** 

The prime objective of the rating exercise is to assess the adequacy of the issuer’s debt-servicing capability. So, ICRA draws up projections on the likely financial position of the issuer under various scenarios, in addition to reviewing the past financials. Future cash flows are projected based on the PPAs tied up, tariff structure, fuel cost, PLF, O&M cost, interest cost, debt repayment schedule, working capital requirement and other funding requirements related to expansion (if any). These cash flows are then used to determine the company’s future debt-servicing capability. 

The various financial metrics assessed by ICRA could be divided into three categories - profitability, leverage and coverage and liquidity. This document provides a summary of why ICRA considers these ratios to be important. For a more detailed description, readers may refer to the note titled, ‘Approach for Financial Ratio Analysis’, published on ICRA’s website.

# **Profitability** 

The profitability of a power project is critically dependent upon the nature of the PPA, tariff structure under the PPA as well as fuel availability and pricing. In case of a cost-plus based PPA, the profitability depends on the entity’s ability to declare the plant availability and maintain the actual costs within the normative benchmarks. Further, an entity with a cost-plus based PPA can achieve higher than the allowed level of returns if its actual operating and financial performance (related to plant efficiency, working capital requirement and cost of funding) is better than the normative benchmarks. In case of a competitively bid-based PPA, the actual returns for an entity would depend on its ability to keep the actual operating and cost parameters within the tariff bid levels. For projects exposed to the short-term power market, the entity’s profitability remains exposed to volatility in volume and tariff in the short-term market as well as the volatility in the fuel price level. Tariff structures are evaluated to assess whether they are sufficient to maintain project profitability and meet debt obligations under minimum performance conditions. For plants with a significant portion of uncontracted capacity, the analysis also considers their historical performance in the merchant market, both in terms of sales volume and pricing and examines the long-term viability of these trends when projecting for future years. Given the finite life of the power generation assets, the rating methodology focusses on the internal rate of return (IRR) indicators. ICRA assesses the project IRR in relation to the company’s weighted average cost of capital. 

# **Leverage and Coverage Indicators** 

The power generation projects are generally funded with heavy dependence on external debt leading to high leverage level. Nonetheless, availability of debt with a long maturity profile and competitive interest rate can partially offset the risk associated with a high financial leverage. ICRA’s focus of analysis here is debt coverage indicators like cumulative debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and minimum DSCR over the debt repayment tenure. 

The financial assessment of IPPs is primarily driven by debt coverage metrics like cumulative DSCR and minimum DSCR as well as leverage (total debt to operating profits)) ratio. ICRA evaluates multiple scenarios to assess the DSCR over the debt repayment period, with the key sensitive variables being project cost overrun, plant availability, plant efficiency (heat rate and auxiliary consumption) and cost of debt. Further, in case of projects with competitively bid-based PPAs, the additional sensitivity factors include the actual cost of fuel, fuel price escalation in the PPA and exchange rate (in case of projects with foreign currency debt or projects using imported coal). In case of projects exposed to the short-term market, the additional sensitivity variables are tariff and PLF. 

Typically, the developers infuse the required promoter contribution for a project SPV through a mix of equity and unsecured debt. As per the terms of the loan agreement, the promoter debt generally remains subordinated to the project debt from lenders and the payments on the promoter debt are allowed by lenders, subject to testing of financial covenants and restricted payment conditions like maintenance of the required reserves, security creation, etc. In such instances with clear cash flow subordination of promoter loan to the project debt, the coverage metrics for the project are assessed on an external project debt basis.

## **Liquidity** 

Liquidity is the measure of an entity’s ability to meet its short-term cash obligations from various internal or external resources. For a thermal power project, internal resources include cash flows from sale of power, debt service reserve account (DSRA) and free cash balances. External resources include undrawn lines of credit or equity capital. The short-term obligations include operating costs, debt-servicing obligations, working capital requirements and capital expenditure (if any). Higher the cushion available between the resources available (especially internal resources) and the obligations, the better is the liquidity profile of an entity. Liquidity is generally assessed in conjunction with the vulnerability of an entity to timely refinancing/renewing short-term sources of funding. Depending upon the circumstances, an entity that has a relatively modest liquidity profile, but a strong refinancing ability may be viewed favourably. ICRA also notes that the liquidity available with an entity may be for a temporary period , and hence, an entity’s overall policy towards maintaining adequate liquidity in the form of a DSRA, undrawn working capital lines and free cash balances is accorded due importance in the analytical approach. ICRA evaluates the transaction documents to ascertain the availability of the DSRA prior to a default to ensure timely debt servicing. If the DSRA is available for use only post default or is contingent on meeting certain conditions, ICRA does not consider the DSRA for liquidity assessment.

## **Financial Flexibility** 

An entity’s financial flexibility (or the lack thereof) is reflected in its ability to access the capital or the money markets at a short notice, attract diverse and marquee investors and enjoy the confidence of banks, FIs and intermediaries. Strong financial flexibility allows an entity to raise fresh borrowings or refinance existing ones in quick time and whenever required. For a thermal power project, financial flexibility could arise from factors such as the tail period<sup>_1_</sup> available in the PPA and/or strong parentage or linkages with a strong group. For operational projects, ICRA also examines the refinancing ability by assessing the expected cash flows from the remaining tenure of the project life. ICRA considers ratios such as project life coverage ratio (PLCR) to evaluate the financial flexibility.

# **Tenure Mismatches and Risks Relating to Interest Rates and Refinancing** 

Given the capital-intensive nature of thermal power projects, which are largely debt-funded, a long debt maturity profile with debt-servicing obligations well spread out over the debt tenure aids to maintain a comfortable liquidity and financial profile. Dependence on short-term borrowings to fund long-term investments can expose a company to significant refinancing risks, especially during periods of tight systemic liquidity. ICRA evaluates the extent of such mismatches and the mitigating factors therein. One source of mitigation could be the existence of adequate buffers of liquid assets/committed bank lines to meet short-term obligations. Another source of mitigation could be the entity’s strong financial flexibility to be able to garner fresh funds at a short notice or a potent ability to refinance. ICRA also evaluates the impact of movement in interest rates on the debt coverage indicators of the issuer. 

# **Foreign Currency-related Risks** 

The foreign currency risk can arise from unhedged foreign currency liabilities pertaining to funding capital expenditure and/or working capital. The focus here is on assessing the hedging policy of the issuer concerned in the context of the tenure and the nature of its PPA with off-takers. The foreign currency risk also arises for projects using imported coal, without having the variable charges component in a long-term PPA in foreign currency (US dollar). In such a case, ICRA evaluates the sensitivity of the profitability and the debt coverage indicators to exchange rate movements. Hedging, if not for the entire tenure of debt, then at least for near-term foreign exchange repayment commitments, is viewed favourably. 

# **Structural Features Relating to the Debt Instruments/Facilities** 

ICRA reviews the loan documents (such as sanction letters, facility agreements, escrow agreements) to evaluate the structural features associated with the loans/borrowing programme. These features provide additional protection to the lenders/bondholders through the presence of an escrow mechanism and the ring-fencing of cash flows. The assessment involves a review of the cash flow waterfall mechanism, which spells out the payment priority towards statutory dues, debt servicing, operating expenses, creation of reserve funds for debt servicing, besides the stipulation of covenants, including minimum coverage ratios that must be met before payouts to subordinate debt holders (mainly sponsor debt) or declaration of dividends. This apart, restriction on the ability of the project company to take on additional debt and/or capital expansion without the approval of the lenders/bondholders and implementation of cash sweep mechanism to use surplus cash flows for debt prepayment by the lenders are viewed favourably by ICRA. 

Additionally, ICRA assesses the management’s stance on growth, capital expenditure and acquisitions, particularly where an aggressive approach could heighten event risk from a large-scale expansions or inorganic initiatives, given the potential to weaken the financial risk profile. The evaluation also considers whether the management prioritises shareholder returns over creditor interests, as reflected in relatively high dividend payouts. A willingness to operate with elevated financial leverage further underscores a risk-tolerant financial strategy, which could accentuate vulnerability during periods of business or economic stress.

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts and auditors’ comments that are part of the annual report of the rated entity. Any deviation from the accounting standards is noted and the financial statements of the entity are adjusted to reflect the impact of such deviations. Significant deviations may be indicative of weak corporate governance practices in the entity.

## **Contingent Liabilities/Off-balance Sheet Exposures** 

For this, the likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/off-balance sheet exposures and the financial implications of the same are evaluated, while factoring the mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion. 

## **Force Majeure Risk** 

Like any infrastructure project, thermal power projects are also vulnerable to force majeure events. While this is an event risk, the presence of force majeure clauses in the PPAs limit the company’s liability arising from non-performance or underperformance. ICRA examines if, and the extent to which, the force majeure risks are mitigated through insurance contracts or by specific provisions in the PPA that cover such eventualities. The strength of these mitigants influences the overall financial flexibility of an entity. 

## **Event Risks** 

ICRA recognises the possibility of events such as mergers and acquisitions, asset sales and litigations, which could have a material impact on the credit profile of an entity. It is often difficult to incorporate the impact of such discrete events in the credit rating from the beginning. Depending on whether and when such events occur, the rating opinion could be substantially different. To take rating decisions in such cases, ICRA applies its analytical judgment based on the rated entity’s track record, the credibility of the management and the experience of having seen similar situations play out in other entities. However, given the nature of such events, it is possible that the rating may undergo a material change later upon the occurrence of the event. 

## **Parentage** 

Despite the non-recourse nature of the debt availed by the issuers in the thermal power sector, the financial strength of the parent/sponsor is a credit factor, given that apart from contributing the equity capital and/or subordinated debt, a stronger sponsor has greater wherewithal to meet any funding shortfalls faced by the SPV, while ensuring timely financial closure of the project. Further, during the pre-commerical operation date (COD) stage, most of the projects take recourse from the sponsor for cost overrun support. 

If the parent’s or promoter group’s credit profile is relatively stronger than the rated entity, ICRA assesses the ability and the likelihood of the parent extending extraordinary support to the entity. Support here means financial support from the parent expected to be available to the entity in the form of loans, equity, extended credit period, advances, etc. in times of credit or liquidity stress on the entity. ‘Support’ does not mean the operational support or the ongoing/committed financial support from the parent, such as the capital infusion to fund the rated entity’s expansion plans or support to maintain a targeted capital structure, but the extraordinary support which cannot be foreseen, is intended to avoid a default and thus cannot be factored in while assessing the notional standalone rating of the entity. It may be noted that promoters in their individual capacity or private equity firms/other financial investors are generally not treated as parents for assessing the likelihood of extraordinary financial support coming in. 

While the weak financial health of the sponsor constrains its ability to support the project SPVs in times of need, the presence of a financially strong sponsor need not necessarily mean assured support to fund SPVs in case of any funding shortfall. In the absence of explicit support (guarantees/shortfall undertakings), the sponsors’ willingness to support the operational projects is determined by the long-term project attractiveness, which is a function of the strength of the project cash flows and duration of the tail period. Also, ICRA considers the past track record of the sponsor in extending timely support to any of its other SPVs and gauge the intent of the sponsor to extend extraordinary support to the rated SPV based on interactions with the sponsor.

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the governance risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environment (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/ transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the subcomponents of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, but their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or carbon transition risks such as those arising from changes in regulations or other environmental and social risks; and seeks to understand the various mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks. 

The environmental risks for coal-based power producers are significant because of their use of fossil fuels. Coal-based power plants are one of the leading emitters of pollutants and cause significant damage to the environment. They are also one of the largest industrial users of water. Further, coal mining has an adverse impact on the environment, especially on the flora and fauna in the coal-mining region. This, in turn, leads to focus on increasing the share of renewables in the generation mix. Nonetheless, the contribution of coal-based power generation is expected to remain significant in the next few years, considering the need for consistent, non-intermittent base load generation. The ability of the power producers to comply with the emission norms prescribed by the Government remains important to avoid any disruption in operations or penalties for

# **Governance Practices** 

A sound corporate governance structure should clearly delineate the roles and responsibilities of the Board of Directors and the management. The composition of an entity’s Board, its involvement in strategic decision-making and the entity’s compliance with the legal and regulatory requirements are factored in during credit assessments. ICRA also seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of the entity’s commitment to follow transparent and credible practices, as reflected in the presentation of the financial statements, timeliness and depth of disclosures, consistency in communication and the openness about sharing information during the rating process. Additionally, factors such as the complexity of the corporate group structure, related-party transactions, instances of financial support to group entities at the expense of debt holders, and any abrupt resignations of auditors or independent directors are evaluated.

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the company’s business and financial risks, likely cash flows over the life of the instrument being rated and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis its debt-servicing obligations. 

As this note highlights, for thermal power projects, project risks assume importance during the implementation phase, while post COD, it is the adequacy of cash flow generation vis-à-vis the debt-servicing commitments that primarily influence the rating. As revenues and profitability for a power project remain sensitive to the structure of the PPA and the extent of passthrough of fuel cost in tariff, various scenarios are drawn up to assess the impact of the key variables on the debt-servicing metrics. While key sensitive variables vary depending on the PPA structure, the most commonly used ones are project cost, tariff, plant availability, station heat rate and cost of debt. ICRA’s rating approach also involves an assessment of the entity’s management quality and governance practices. An entity’s credit rating may also be influenced by its ownership, the nature of linkages with its parent or group entities, degree of financial flexibility, the corporate legal structure, track record of operations and that of debt servicing, and vulnerability (if any) to discrete event risks. Further, ICRA draws comfort from strong debt coverage ratios and the presence of structural features like an adequately funded DSRA, restrictive covenants on additional debt and adequate dividend lock-up/restricted payment triggers.
