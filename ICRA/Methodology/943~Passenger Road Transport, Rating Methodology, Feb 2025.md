For this rating methodology, we define the passenger road transport entities (RTEs) as those providing road transport services to passengers and generating revenues predominantly from passenger activities. In some instances, non-transport revenues that are generated along with passenger services, such as when an entity has developed commercial lease rental opportunities by exploiting space in its bus depots or through advertisements, are also included in the calculation of overall revenues. Entities covered in the methodology also include public road transport corporations (RTCs) incorporated under the Road Transport Corporation Act, 1950. However, entities which provide services on behalf of the RTEs, state or the Central Government authorities, as part of the latter’s outsourcing endeavour, are not covered under this methodology. 

# **Overview** 

The RTEs play a critical role of providing mobility to the passengers within the city, state and the country. The business is highly capital intensive, because of the need to invest in the regular replacement of ageing fleets to run efficiently and sustain cash flows. Maintaining high load occupancies, optimal vehicle utilisation and controlling costs are critical as a large part of the cost structure consists of fixed costs for fuel and labour. Moreover, the passenger road transport sector is subject to intense competition, with a steady influx of new entrants and competing alternate means like the railways, metro rails, cab aggregators and waterways. The competition gives rise to intense pricing pressure and hence an entity’s ability to pass on the increase in operating costs is critical to sustain profitability. The business models vary, ranging from largely concentrated networks (such as regional point-topoint players) to the RTEs that have a diversified footprint in the region. The RTEs, while performing an important societal function, are also governed by a regulatory framework that differs from state to state. The regulatory bodies directly or indirectly control important factors that drive performance, including the licence fee to operate in a particular route and limitations on ticket prices. 

For analytical convenience, the risk factors analysed by ICRA are classified under four segments viz., Business Risk Assessment, Financial Risk Assessment, and select other elements of credit risk assessment. In addition to these considerations, an entity’s credit rating may also be influenced by its management quality, and the relevant environmental, social and governance risks.

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

### **Scale and Market Position** 

ICRA views scale as an important indicator of an RTE’s revenue-generating capability and its ability to withstand declines in traffic collections or cost increases. Size is also a key factor for determining the pricing power of an RTE. Moreover, a large revenue base can provide economies of scale leading to better absorption of fixed costs and improve cost efficiencies and help negotiate better terms for fleet purchases. It also helps generate higher cash flows for capital reinvestment and debt reduction. Such investments result in improved levels of service, in terms of geographies covered and the quality of the fleet operated, which are the keys to maintaining or growing the share in the road transportation market. Larger RTEs also have greater financial flexibility allowing them to reduce their cost of capital. Scale is measured based on the total reported revenues and the number of vehicles held.

|---|---|---|---|

The market position is reflective of the entity’s brand strength and track record and helps capture a higher volume share in the road transport market. It also acts as a strong barrier for new entrants in the region, particularly because of the considerable outlay required towards bus additions. The markets in which an entity operates, the frequency of its schedules, and its pricing have a large degree of influence on its market position. ICRA measures the market position by comparing the passenger traffic collections of all the players in the region, to the extent that the data is available for analysis. 

### **Diversification** 

RTEs also differentiate themselves through diversity in routes and frequency of services. The network strength considers the diversity of the RTE’s operating locations. Those with a balanced revenue contribution from several cities / towns are likely to better withstand the pressures on traffic collections on account of competition. A diversified network base tends to balance revenues across various loss-making and break-even routes vis-à-vis profitable routes, Also, operating across a diverse network spread across many regions lessens the impact of any labour strikes, natural disasters, competitive action of regional players and other operational event risks that could affect operations. ICRA measures network strength through the number of routes and the corresponding geographical diversity of the RTE’s operating footprint.

However, presence in this segment, even at relatively lower profitability, helps the RTEs in better absorbing the fixed costs and providing other scale benefits. 

## **Operational Efficiency and Cost Effectiveness** 

In addition to economies of scale, the operating profitability of the RTEs primarily depends on their level of operating efficiencies and cost competitiveness (discussed in the next section). ICRA looks at various operational parameters that reflect an RTE’s ability to improve its operational efficiency and generate stable earnings. These parameters also provide insights into the management’s business strategy and efficiency of the entity’s operations. Key variables analysed by ICRA to assess efficiency include effective utilisation of the fleet/vehicles (average number of buses on road / average number of buses held), passenger load factor (defined as passenger kilometres/ seat kilometres), average mileage derived, staff ratio (number of staff divided by total number of vehicles held), revenue per vehicle and fleet age to the extent of data availability.

Improving profitability is a challenge for the industry, given the high fixed costs and limited pricing flexibility, owing to intense competition among market players, apart from competition from alternative avenues like railways and taxi aggregators. Hence, efficient vehicle utilisation, ability to increase fares, and savings on other costs are typically the primary levers that an entity utilises to contain pressure on earnings. 

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

To assess the financial risk profile of an entity, ICRA analyses its performance trends over a period in the past as well as that projected for the future. It also factors in the sensitivity of performance to various scenarios. ICRA also compares the operational and financial metrics of the entity with its peers. ICRA benchmarks the financial ratios of the entity against its rating medians to assess its relative standing across the rated universe and entities in the sector. 

The key financial ratios discussed in this note have been divided into three categories viz., leverage, coverage and liquidity. Leverage ratios measure the indebtedness of an entity, while coverage ratios measure its debt-servicing ability. Liquidity ratios measure the buffer that an entity has with respect to its obligations, which can be utilised in case of any temporary cash flow mismatch. 

The following section provides only a summary of why ICRA considers the leverage, coverage and liquidity ratios to be important. For a more detailed description, readers may refer to the note titled, _<u>Approach for Financial Ratio Analysis,</u>_ published on ICRA’s website.

# **Profitability** 

ICRA uses operating profit before depreciation, interest, tax and amortisation (OPBITDA) as the primary profitability metric, as the same is capital structure agnostic and is also reflective of the efficiency of operations. The road transportation sector is generally characterised by low margins as road transportation services are considered a public service in most parts of the country. 

Fuel and labour costs are largely fixed in nature, as labour costs are difficult to reduce and the fuel costs being market-driven, depend on the distance plied. An important factor is the vulnerability to fuel costs, where the capability of an RTE to pass on increased fuel prices may have a significant impact on its profitability and cash flow generation. The ability to pass on higher fuel prices primarily depends on the RTE’s market strength and the pricing action taken by the competitors. While pricing flexibility for private sector operators are determined by market forces, pricing actions of the public transport corporations are subject to approval from the respective state governments. 

The key areas with potential for cost reduction include procurement (based on bargaining power enjoyed with original equipment manufacturers) and other operating costs like maintenance (consumables depending on the age and quality of the fleet). ICRA undertakes cost structure analysis on a per kilometre (km) basis for each key cost item to measure the competitiveness of an RTE. In terms of assessing the profitability, ICRA looks at the operating profit or loss per km. The type of service offered, entity-specific operational parameters, and market-wide conditions are the key variables that affect profitability. ICRA looks at the profitability of the routes/schedules operated by the RTE, where the schedules are classified into two categories. These are 1) profitable schedules where earnings (revenue) per kilometre (EPKM) exceed the cost of operations and 2) loss-making schedules (EPKM < cost of operations), or the break-even category of schedules (EPKM=cost). ICRA measures the distribution of schedules across these categories under each product offering. Further, it also looks at the overall load occupancy factor to assess the sustainability of revenues over a period.

# **Leverage and Coverage** 

The financial policy of an RTE, with respect to managing its balance sheet, is an important rating driver. This is because it provides insight into the management’s philosophy regarding the entity’s capital structure and the financial risk under which it is willing to operate. The RTEs that pursue an aggressive financial policy, including heavy reliance on debt financing, are likely to be more vulnerable to downturns than those that employ a lower degree of financial leverage in their business. The RTEs have significant re-investment requirements for the expansion and replacement of fleet. The debt profile of such entities is typically skewed towards the long term on account of the capital needed for funding fleet procurement and establishing depot infrastructure, with the cash and carry nature of the business limiting the working capital requirements. # **Capital Structure** 

An entity’s leverage is typically measured through its total debt to operating profits. A low debt-to-OPBIDTA multiple is a credit positive, as this reiterates the entity’s ability to fund growth opportunities and improve its competitive position without being overly reliant on external sources. In addition, other qualitative factors are considered such as the debt profile, whether it has been supported by the state government through repayment grants or explicit guarantees (especially applicable for stateowned road transport corporations). The non-debt liabilities such as pending dues towards staff salary, power bills, contractor/vendor payments, contingent liabilities, etc. are also analysed based on the best available information.

Coverage is a measure of an entity’s debt-servicing ability and is calculated as the ratio of profits (or cash flows) to the debtservicing obligations in a period. An entity’s coverage is measured through its interest coverage and debt-service coverage ratio. The interest coverage indicator reflects the entity’s ability to service the cost of external borrowings after meeting all operating expenses and its flexibility in absorbing any decline in operational earnings arising from various business risks, without impairing its ability to meet interest payments. This is an important rating consideration as a weak OPBIDTA-to-interest multiple indicates the entity’s inability to generate adequate operating profits to meet its interest and may signal a default risk. The debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) indicates the entity’s ability to service its interest and repayment from accruals generated by the business. Further, given the thin operating margins in the industry, aggressive debt-funded expansion to pursue strong revenue growth may result in inadequate coverage indicators. Hence, a judicious management policy for expansion and related funding is the key to maintaining a prudent leverage structure and associated coverage metrics.

ICRA places considerable emphasis on measuring the adequacy of cash flows (after meeting operating and investment needs) to meet debt repayment obligations in a timely manner. The ratings factor in the existence of an adequate buffer in the form of liquid assets to meet any short-term obligations and the extent to which the entity could be impacted by interest rate movements on borrowed funds. Healthy operating cash flows enable the RTEs to undertake critical investments, without unduly stressing the balance sheet. The sector remains highly dependent on the banking system to meet its funding requirements, with limited access to capital markets. # **State government’s credit quality** 

ICRA also evaluates the credit quality of the state government concerned while assigning credit rating to a Government-owned RTE. The credit quality of the state government is assessed based on _‘Rating Methodology for State Governments’_ available on ICRA’s website. Apart from the legislative and administrative relationship between the two, an RTE regularly receives various transfers from the state government, the extent and frequency of which is directly linked to the financial position of the state government. Considering the strong operational and financial linkages between the RTE and the state government, the credit quality of the latter becomes  an important criterion in influencing ICRA’s ratings for the RTEs. 

# **Support from the parent (in case of private RTEs)** 

Apart from the standalone credit considerations, the likelihood of extraordinary support from the parent to an entity is also an important consideration for arriving at the final rating. This process involves an assessment of the parent’s ability and willingness to extend support to the entity (and vice versa), determining the strategic importance of the entity to the parent, in addition to evaluating an entity’s own fundamental credit strength. Further, the support from the parent could also be explicitly in the form of a guarantee. The strength and comfort of parent support is analysed as per _‘Rating Approach – Implicit Support from Parent or Group’_ and _‘Rating Approach – Explicit Third-Party Support_ ’—methodologies that are available on ICRA’s website.

# **Management Quality** 

All debt ratings necessarily incorporate an assessment of the quality of the rated entity’s management. Experienced management teams are considered positive factors. Thus, the comfort level of the management, evident from discussions and past actions, becomes a key rating consideration in such cases. Thus, ICRA gives considerable importance to qualitative aspects, resulting from management meetings. A detailed discussion is held with the management to understand their business strategies, growth plans as well as risk appetite which may have an impact on the future performance of the entity, besides the outlook on the industry. Periodic interactions with the management also help ICRA evaluate the management’s tendency to deviate from its business and financial policies in times of stress. The management’s ability to meet their committed performance targets and ensure stability in operations is an important input for analysis. 

In case of Government-owned RTEs, its ability to implement adequate tariff revisions on a regular basis becomes critical as inadequate tariff would result in losses and increase dependence on the respective state government. ICRA also evaluates the RTEs’ track record of introducing e-governance initiatives such as electronic ticketing and payment and online systems for routes and bus tracking, etc., which helps in improving the overall efficiency. ICRA also evaluates the timeliness and accuracy of financial reporting, operating statements and budget documents and considers the sufficiency of staff at key positions and adequate capacity of the staff in critical functions as strong positives.

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of a rated RTE with the focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. The impact of the E&S risks on an RTE’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally pull down the ratings, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable.

## **Environmental (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking the credit assessment of the RTEs, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigants. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated RTE/debt instrument. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly, if not precisely. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the sub - components of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the RTE. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future and, hence, these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today — with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated RTE by then would possibly be able to adapt. While evaluating E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an RTE faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. 

### **Environmental considerations** 

The RTEs are heavy users of high-speed diesel (HSD) fuel and are thus exposed to the carbon transition risk. The entities would have to incur investment for upgrading fleets with buses having lower emissions, as the relevant regulations evolve. Further, the RTE operations could partly be impacted by natural calamities such as earthquakes, cyclones, floods, etc. In case of state government-owned entities, any major investment in new fleet/technology arising out of low emission requirements and significant financial loss or damage to the infrastructure due to natural calamities are generally funded by the respective state governments. 

### **Social considerations** 

Passenger road transport across the states is largely considered as a social obligation by the respective state governments. The RTEs are highly exposed to the risk of inadequate tariff revision since state governments are reluctant to pass on any major financial burden on the passengers. Further, the operations of the RTEs could also be disrupted by employee strikes because of a host of reasons, including demand for higher wages. The state government-owned RTEs get regular support in the form of financial assistance to keep the operations running. 

## **Governance Risks** 

A sound corporate governance structure attempts to make clear the distinction of power and responsibilities between the board of directors and the management. The constitution of an entity’s board and the board of directors’ participation in strategy formulation, besides the entity’s adherence to legal and statutory compliance requirements, are factored in during credit assessments. ICRA seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of an entity’s commitment to following transparent and credible practices by the way its financial statements are reported, its level of disclosures, consistency in communication and the openness about sharing information during the credit rating exercise. Besides, the corporate group structure (whether simple or complex), the rated entity’s related-party transactions and instances of supporting group entities at the expense of debt holders are assessed. 

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. The rating methodology broadly highlights ICRA’s approach in assessing the business and financial risks of the RTEs in the passenger road transport sector. As the note highlights, for passenger RTEs, special attention is paid to the rated entity’s market position and business profile, operating efficiency, profitability, the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis debt servicing obligations and the overall approach towards maintaining/improving the credit policy through prudent financial policies.
