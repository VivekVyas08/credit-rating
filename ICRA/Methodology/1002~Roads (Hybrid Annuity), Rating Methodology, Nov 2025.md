# **Overview** 

Over the past two decades, several road projects have been executed through public-private partnerships (PPP) under the build-operate-transfer (BOT) model, through BOT-Toll and BOT-Annuity, and more recently through BOT-Hybrid Annuity Mode (HAM)<sup>1</sup> . This document provides an overview of ICRA’s methodology for rating the BOT-HAM road projects. These road projects are implemented through projectspecific special purpose vehicles (SPVs), in which the debt is raised to fund a part of the project cost. The main source of revenue for these SPVs is the annuity (including interest) and O&M payments from the project owner<sup>2</sup> , which it uses to meet the contractual obligations, including O&M expenses (routine and major maintenance (MM)), statutory dues and debt servicing.

With the authority funding part (40% in case of NHAI<sup>_5_</sup> HAM, the share could be different for state HAMs) of the BPC (as determined by the developer) under HAM, developers stand to benefit as their equity requirements are lower when compared to other modes of BOT projects. Lower equity requirements, provision for mobilisation advances which are recovered later from the grant payments (authority funding), higher RoW availability, inflation-linked adjustments for BPC<sup>_6_</sup> , termination payments during the construction period and de-linking of the construction and operations period are the major positives of HAM. 

HAM road projects undergo a significant change in their risk profile once they go past the commercial operation date (COD). While execution risks dominate in the pre-COD period, the primary risk in the post-COD period pertains to counterparty credit risk (timeliness of payments) and O&M risk, as the quantum of annuity is linked to maintaining the project stretch as per the service-level requirements<sup>_7_</sup> stated in the concession agreement (CA). For a project with a demonstrated track record, the associated O&M costs are relatively more reliable compared to a project which has not achieved COD as there is no history of O&M on the stretch.

# **Credit Risk Assessment Framework** 

This rating methodology explains ICRA’s approach to assessing the business and financial risk profiles of BOT-HAM road projects. It aims to help issuers, investors and other interested market participants understand ICRA’s approach to analysing the quantitative and qualitative risks that are likely to affect rating outcomes in this sector. The list of rating drivers covered in this methodology is not exhaustive by itself but provides an overall perspective on the rating considerations that are usually considered the most important. For analytical convenience, the key factors are grouped under the following broad heads— Business Risk Assessment, Financial Risk Assessment & Sponsor Risk Assessment.

# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Industry risk** 

The entities operating in the HAM segment are exposed to the risk of increasing competitive intensity, resulting in aggressive bidding, which in turn impacts profitability metrics. Further, the entities’ cash flows and returns are exposed to inflation risk as O&M receipts, though linked to inflation index (70% WPI and 30% CPI), may not be adequate to compensate for the actual increase in O&M/periodic maintenance expenses. 

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Execution risks** 

### • **Permitting risk** 

A key component of the completion risk is the permitting risk, which refers to a project’s ability to acquire all statutory clearances prior to the commencement of construction activity. Typically, for a road project this would include securing the RoW, rehabilitation and resettlement, clearances from the Ministry of Environment and Forests (MoEF<sup>_9_</sup> ) and the Railways and clearances for shifting all utilities that lie in the project area. The project owner usually assumes the permitting risk and also undertakes to indemnify the project SPV against losses arising out of any disputes with respect to the acquisition of land. The permitting risks are usually low where the project involves upgradation of an existing operating stretch, however, such risks are significant where projects involve construction of entirely new stretches (including bypasses), where a considerable amount of land needs to be acquired.

## • **Cost and time overruns** 

- **Cost overrun:** The project cost is dependent on several variables, which include the nature of the surface (flexible versus rigid) the number of lanes, and complexity of the project (e.g. the terrain of the project stretch and the number of structures (bridges, culverts, rail over bridges)), which need to be constructed. The benchmarking of the project cost on a per-lane-km basis is attempted to be done with other road assets of a similar type. In addition, ICRA looks at the variation between the authority’s base cost and the BPC as per the developer to assess the likelihood of a cost overrun in case of aggressive bidding. ICRA tries to evaluate the vulnerability of the project to cost and time overruns, and the SPV’s financial flexibility to fund these overruns. This assumes significance, given that in such BOT projects, cost escalations need to be funded by the sponsor, which could affect the returns to the sponsor. 

- **Time overrun:** Road construction projects often face challenges related to the mobilisation of labour, equipment and key raw material availability (aggregate, sand, etc.). Further, factors such as difficult terrain, availability of construction equipment, weather and labour problems also contribute to construction delays, with such time overruns in effect translating to cost escalations. 

Some of these risks are partially mitigated through fixed-price, fixed-time EPC contracts with strong counterparties with liquidated damage provisions for non-performance. In such cases, operational capabilities (including past track record) of the EPC contractor, and its ability to meet the contractual commitments acts as a mitigant. 

Handing over 80% of the RoW by the appointed date is the authority’s obligation. This is a condition precedent for HAM projects, failing which an appointed date cannot be announced. The permitting risk remains in case the 80% land provided is not contiguous. If the authority is unable to provide the remaining RoW within 180 days from the appointed date, it is removed from the scope of work under the provision of ‘change of scope’ as per CA. If the appointed date is not announced before the first anniversary of the CA date, the HAM awards are eligible to be terminated. However, with the recent changes announced by the ministry, RoW of 90% is required for providing the appointed date for HAM projects. Further, with forest clearance from MoEF to be cleared prior to the awarding of letter of acceptance (LoA), this is expected to largely streamline the execution process and reduce the time gap between LoA and appointed date, going forward. 

Moreover, the construction and the operations period are de-linked<sup>_10_</sup> . Thereby, the number of semi-annuities remains fixed<sup>_11_</sup> depending on the operations period, unlike the earlier BOT-Annuity projects where any increase in the construction period reduces the operations period as the concession period is fixed. The permitting risks are relatively lower for BOT-HAM projects when compared to other modes of BOT due to the aforesaid reasons. 

# **Funding risks** 

By virtue of lower permitting risks and higher termination payments, the ability of BOT-HAM projects to attract capital is relatively superior compared with conventional BOT-annuity projects. 

## • **Equity mobilisation risk** 

The ability of the sponsors to infuse equity (promoter’s contribution) in a timely manner is assessed. The financial strength of the sponsor is a key credit determinant during the pre-COD stage, given that apart from contributing the promoter’s share in the form of equity capital and/or subordinated debt, the sponsor is also directly or indirectly responsible for ensuring the financial closure of these projects. 

## • **Timeliness of grant from authority during construction period** 

The authority grant during the construction period is paid in five instalments for HAM 1.0 (40% of BPC in case of NHAI HAM) – which has been revised to ten instalments in HAM 2.0 (remained unchanged in HAM 3.0) –  based upon milestone completion

As per the CA, the O&M payments are also adjusted for inflation and are paid on a semi-annual basis along with the annuity payments. While payments from the NHAI have been largely timely, a few state authorities have previously delayed the annuity payments. Therefore, assessment of the credit risk profile of the counterparty authority remains critical. For authorities with limited track record of annuity payments, ICRA tries to evaluate the budgetary allocations/funding sources for annuity payments and credit profile of the authority for assessing the timeliness of annuity payments. 

The credit profile of the project is also influenced by the credit profile of the counterparty/authority. In certain circumstances, where there are certain mitigating factors, like structural features including significant liquidity reserves, debt service reserve, forward-looking tests on restricted payments, etc., the credit rating could be higher than the rating of the counterparty. 

While the credit quality of the state-owned authorities is closely linked with that of the state government, ICRA analyses the project awarding authority’s financial position in terms of track record of annuity payments and the adequacy of the budgetary allocations by the relevant state governments to assess the timeliness of the annuity payments. In such cases, the rating of the entity would be linked to the counterparties’ credit profile, unless there are mitigating factors such as strong liquidity buffer and presence of a strong parent/promoter group which is expected to provide support to the SPV, in case the need arises. 

# **Operating risk** 

The project not conforming to the required performance parameters over the period of the CA amounts to operating risk. Timely and adequate O&M activity is crucial for a project’s cash flow-generating ability to remain healthy. Otherwise, it could lead to material weakening, thereby disrupting the revenue-generating capacity (which is linked to lane availability). This risk, if not properly addressed, could manifest in the form of reduced revenues, increased operating costs or increased MM expenditure at a later date. 

<mark>Although the CA does not mention lane availability, in line with the conventional BOT-Annuity projects, the concessionaire is required to adhere to the service-level requirements stated in the CA.  Slippages on maintaining adequate road quality could result in penalties/deduction from annuity payments. T</mark> herefore, the concessionaire’s ability to fairly assess O&M expenses and lay down a proper maintenance schedule is important to avoid any annuity deductions on account of inadequate maintenance of the project stretch. 

O&M expenses for road projects are primarily of two types: 

- Major maintenance (MM), which involves the relaying of the bituminous-concrete (in the case of a flexible pavement) or replacement of the concrete slabs (top layer, in the case of a rigid pavement), is undertaken to maintain the project stretch as per the maintenance requirements stipulated in the CA. 

- Routine maintenance, which involves repairing of cracks, replacement of safety girders along the highway, clearance of debris following accidents, ensuring functionality of signposts, maintenance of a security set-up and such other activities. 

ICRA considers the track record and experience of the O&M contractor and the mitigants available (such as liquidated damages) in case of any underperformance. The other aspects which could impact O&M risks in BOT road projects are: 

- **The mechanism by which the scheduled O&M expenses would be funded:** O&M outflows are likely to peak during the year in which MM activity falls due, when the relaying of the top surface is carried out. ICRA, therefore, evaluates whether the cash flow waterfall provides for the MM reserve that would get built up gradually, either from the annuity payments, additional debt drawdown or through sponsor fund infusion, so that adequate funds are available for these peak outflows. 

- **Inflation sensitivity of the O&M estimates:** The O&M estimates would be highly sensitive to inflation and would, therefore, be difficult to budget over the long operational period. Most of these projects are likely to enter fixed-cost O&M contracts, possibly with the sponsors themselves. However, if actual expenses exceed the budgeted costs, the incentive for the O&M contractor to maintain the road in accordance with the terms could diminish. 

ICRA’s assumptions on expenses towards O&M (including MM) are based on empirical costs observed in other similar projects. Additionally, the assumptions attempt to have adequate in-built buffers to account for the expected rise in expenses (owing to inflation).

# **Inherent profitability of the projects** 

Given the highly capital-intensive nature of the road projects and low operational expenses, the emphasis is on the internal rate of return (IRR) and NPV instead of the conventional profitability indicators. Aggressive bidding could eventually lead to suboptimal IRR. The sponsors may not have the economic incentive for supporting such projects, in which case there is a possibility of invocation of concessionaire event of default clause or lenders exercising their substitution rights. 

# **Event risks** 

Given the limited ability to negotiate increased cost/revenue disruptions, the adverse effect of any event risk is more pronounced for the SPVs when compared to corporate entities. Upon occurrence of events like force majeure, changes in law and concessionaire/authority event of default, the nature of the event and its impact on the cash flows is assessed. This is largely a post-facto assessment as factoring in event risks at the outset may not be entirely possible.

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

## **Stability of cash flows** 

The stability of cash flows is higher for HAM road projects when compared with toll road projects. The quantum of annuity payments from the authority critically hinges on the quality of the O&M activity – poor maintenance would result in lower lane availability (than contractually stipulated) and thereby in annuity deductions. Also, the O&M payments from the authority are adjusted for inflation as per the PIM, whereas the actual increase in prices of key raw materials consumed in O&M activity could be higher than the PIM. 

Here emphasis is laid on the predictability of cash flows and the operator’s ability to exercise better control over operating costs. Assets whose operations are stabilised, i.e. with demonstrated operational track record, are positioned favourably from a cash flow predictability perspective. The effectiveness of the O&M activity which is in line with the CA for such assets is relatively well established. On the expense front, the ability of the concessionaire to manage operations and maintenance costs within the budgeted levels remains critical. 

The key sensitivity scenarios include increase in O&M expenses, incremental debt and interest rate risk. 

## **Liquidity and debt service coverage metrics** 

HAM road assets are usually funded with a considerable proportion of external debt, although in many cases grants from the project owners serve to keep leverage at moderate levels. Unlike toll road projects, which start generating cash flows from the COD; for annuity projects, the annuities commence after 180 days from the COD. Therefore, projects with adequate funding – sufficient to meet the O&M expenses for the first six months and interest payments (if applicable) are viewed favourably. In some cases, such funding is part of the project cost. Further, given that the inflows are variable (linked to bank rate or MLCR), non-convertible debentures (NCD)/debt instruments with floating rate/coupon mitigates interest rate risk to an extent. 

<mark>Typically, the sponsor infuses its required contribution for a project SPV through a mix of equity and unsecured debt. As per the terms of the loan agreement, such sponsor debt remains subordinated to the project debt from lenders and the payments on the sponsor debt are allowed by lenders, subject to meeting the restricted payment conditions like maintaining the stipulated financial covenants and the required reserves, etc. Also, in many instances, the subordinated debt from sponsors does not have a right to call an event of default, leading to either acceleration of the subordinate debt payment or initiation of bankruptcy proceedings. In such instances, the coverage metrics for the project SPV are assessed on the external project debt, given the presence of an escrow/cash flow ring-fencing structure monitored by the lenders/trustee.</mark> 

The minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and cumulative DSCR are the key coverage ratios which are analysed. The DSCR measures the cushion between the debt-servicing obligation and the cash flows available for debt servicing (CFADS) in any given period (the annuities are typically semi-annual but may be quarterly/yearly to match with debt repayment frequency). Further, ICRA also assesses the funding mix of the projects, with higher proportion of capital infusion in the form equity compared to other quasi-equity/hybrid instruments (perpetual debt/optionally convertible debentures/unsecured loans) is viewed favourably. In addition, unencumbered cash balances and the debt service reserve account (DSRA) provide liquidity support and help the entity tide over any delays or deductions in annuities from the authority. ICRA also evaluates the gap available between the scheduled date of annuity receipt and the scheduled date of repayment. The ratios which are typically used by ICRA to analyse an entity’s coverage are:

## **Interest rate and forex risks** 

The interest component accounts for a sizeable portion of the annuity payments for the HAM projects, amounting to 45-50% of overall inflows during the operation period, thereby exposing the SPV to interest rate risk. Although the interest rates would move in tandem with bank rates for floating rate debt, the entities are exposed to transmission delays between bank rate and the lending rate. Therefore, the extent to which an issuer would be impacted by movements in interest rates is also evaluated. However, from HAM 2.0 onwards, the computation of interest on annuities has been shifted to MCLR from bank rate. Although this provides a natural hedge between the annuity inflows and interest costs (for debt linked with MCLR), due to different reset frequency, the interest rates risk remain, albeit mitigated.

# **Structural features** 

In most cases, the BOT-HAM road projects are funded through the SPVs, without recourse to the sponsors for debt servicing. Annuity payments from the authority are the only source of revenue available to the project SPV. Thus, apart from assessing the counterparty credit risk and O&M risk for the project, ICRA also evaluates certain structural aspects of these projects. The creation of DSRA either in the form of cash deposits or a Guarantee (DSRA Guarantee) to cover a few quarters (in line with frequency of annuity payments) of debt-servicing obligations (P+I) provides additional comfort. In case of DSRA Guarantee, ICRA also evaluates the risks associated with renewal of such guarantees. Similarly, the creation of a MM reserve (MMR)/capital expenditure (capex) reserve for undertaking the scheduled MM activities also supports the ratings. In this context, the MMR built from operating cash flows over the period of the frequency of MM is viewed favourably when compared to the built-up reserves in years when MM activity is falling due. The presence of the cash-trap mechanism or restricted payment clause is also viewed favourably and supports the ratings. 

Presence of the escrow mechanism and ring-fencing of cash flows to prevent any leakage of funds, cash sweeps well ahead of the debt maturity are some of the structural considerations. In circumstances where the project level debt holder’s position is weakened by the existence of a weak sponsor or higher leverage of the sponsor/holding company, if adequate cash flow ringfencing is not in place and/or if there are no/weak dividend lock-up triggers, the rating could be lower, ceteris paribus. 

<mark>ICRA evaluates the transaction documents to ascertain the availability of DSRA prior to default to ensure timely debt servicing.</mark> 

Other forms of credit support include senior-subordinate debt tranching, trapping surplus cash flows on activation of triggers, etc. Ratings on tranched debt can be distinguished only where there are no cross-default clauses. This is clearly established through specific documented mitigants under the project’s transaction documents and legal framework to establish the fact that the default on one tranche will not result in a payment default on other senior tranches. 

ICRA also reviews the minimum DSCR requirements that must be met prior to payment of dividends, and creation of minimum liquidity buffers, for meeting revenue shortfalls due to disruptions in operations. In addition, guarantees from sponsor for O&M and MM cost overruns are also assessed favourably. 

ICRA also draws comfort from the presence of a trustee, who would effectively control the project cash flows and regularly monitor the various reserves to be created as per the common loan agreement/debenture agreement on behalf of the debt investors. 

# **Amortisation structure, refinancing risk and financial flexibility** 

While in most cases, the debt structure is fully amortising, there could be situations where some portion of the debt involves a bullet repayment and hence is exposed to refinancing risk. ICRA’s assessment of refinancing risk includes but is not limited to determining (a) the quantum and certainty of residual cash flows at the time of refinancing vis-a-vis the amount to be refinanced, (b) resilience of the project’s debt coverage metrics to refinancing risk under different scenarios of refinancing costs (including ability to withstand a significant step-up in coupon at the time of refinancing), (c) the available liquidity with the SPV in the event of financial market disruptions and (d) in case of presence of a call option, a sufficient refinancing window. 

Entities with a long tail period or a high project life coverage ratio (PLCR) are better equipped to raise additional debt or refinance the existing debt with elongated tenor, if needed. However, there is a risk of promoters leveraging projects with long tail periods to support other group projects, and this could be an additional source of credit risk.

## **Covenant package** 

ICRA draws comfort from the restrictive debt covenants, which include prohibitions/tests on additional indebtedness or liens, restrictions on the acquisition and sale of assets, limitations on mergers and consolidations, limitations on investments (permitted investments), change of control/ownership, especially if the sponsors are important to the project. # **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Accounting quality** 

Here, the accounting policies, notes to accounts, and auditor’s comments are reviewed. Any deviations from the standard accounting practices are evaluated and the financial statements of the issuer are adjusted where feasible to reflect the impact of such deviations. Significant deviations may be indicative of weak corporate governance practices in the entity. 

## **Contingent liabilities/Off-balance sheet exposures** 

In this case, the likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/off-balance sheet exposures and the financial implications of the same are evaluated while factoring mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion. 

# **Sponsor Risk Assessment** 

All debt ratings necessarily incorporate an assessment of the quality of the issuer’s management, as well as the strengths/weaknesses arising from the issuer’s being a part of a “group”. Usually, a detailed discussion is held with the sponsor of the issuer to understand its business objectives, plans and strategies and views on past performance. Some of the other points assessed are: 

- a. Experience of the sponsor in the line of business concerned 

- b. Commitment of the sponsor to the line of business concerned 

- c. Track record of sponsor in adhering to its committed financial policies 

- d. Policies of the sponsor on leveraging, rationale for debt raising, balance between leverage and returns, interest risks and currency risks 

- e. The ability and willingness of the sponsor to support the issuer through measures such as fund infusion, if required 

Despite the non-recourse nature of the SPV, the financial strength of the sponsor is a key credit determinant during the preCOD stage, given that apart from contributing the promoter’s share in the form of equity capital and/or subordinated debt, the sponsor is also directly or indirectly responsible for ensuring the financial closure of these projects. Further, during the preCOD stage, most of the HAM projects involve recourse to the sponsor for cost overrun support and shortfall in debt servicing in the event of termination. Post the project completion, while many SPVs enter into O&M contracts with the sponsors, the financial linkage is relatively lower during the post-COD period.

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environmental (E) and social (S) risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/ transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly segregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to segregate the sub-components of E&S risks fully or precisely in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, but their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or carbon transition risks such as those arising from changes in regulations or other environmental and social risks (including resettlement and rehabilitation risks); and seeks to understand the various mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks. Notwithstanding the above, as an example, it is possible that even if an entity A has a higher carbon footprint than entity B, it does not materially affect ICRA’s credit opinion on entity A. This is because ICRA’s credit opinion on an entity considers a wide gamut of credit-relevant factors and the E&S factors are only one among those. 

The road sector is critical to economic growth and is closely linked to the transport sector, which is one of the major contributors to the greenhouse gas emissions. Alternative modes like rail and inland waterways are relatively less polluting, however, the travel time and the last-mile connectivity remain challenges for these modes. Road developers remain exposed to natural disasters (such as cyclones, floods), which could interrupt construction activity or result in an increase in maintenance costs in case of any damage to carriageway, post the COD. However, the availability of insurance for most projects mitigates the adverse effects of these circumstances. Given the large execution targets set by the Government for the road sector, there is a large requirement of land for development of such projects which may also require deforestation (depending on the project location) and, therefore, pose environmental risks. ## **Governance practices** 

A sound corporate governance structure should clearly delineate the roles and responsibilities of the Board of Directors and the management. The composition of an entity’s Board and the entity’s compliance with the legal and regulatory requirements are factored in during credit assessments. ICRA also seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of the entity’s commitment to follow transparent and credible practices, as reflected in the presentation of the financial statements, timeliness and depth of disclosures, consistency in communication and the openness about sharing information during the rating process. # **Summing Up** 

ICRA‘s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the issuer‘s business and financial risks, its likely cash flows over the life of the instrument being rated and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis its debt-servicing obligations and other funding requirements. 

The compliance from the authority on RoW and other prerequisite approvals has improved for HAM projects, resulting in lower execution risks. The features of HAM, like automatic de-scoping, upfront 80% RoW availability (90% for upcoming projects), forest clearance prior to award of LoA (for upcoming projects based on ministry circular dated May 06, 2025), deemed termination clauses and low equity mobilisation risk result in lower execution risks. Given the lower execution risks, HAM road projects qualify for relatively higher ratings even during the construction phase, when compared to the conventional BOT projects. 

Post achieving COD, the revenue and operating risks assume greater significance. ICRA has a more favourable opinion on projects which have been conservatively funded, awarded by a strong counterparty, promoted by strong sponsors which have demonstrated satisfactory O&M track record. ICRA also draws comfort from strong DSCR ratios with a long tail period (to facilitate refinancing if required). The presence of structural features like adequately funded DSRA, contingency and O&M reserves with restrictive covenants on additional debt are credit positives.
