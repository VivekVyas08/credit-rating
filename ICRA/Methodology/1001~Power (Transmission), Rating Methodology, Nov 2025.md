This rating methodology describes ICRA’s approach to assessing the credit risk of power transmission companies. It aims to help issuers, investors and other market participants understand ICRA’s approach to analysing risks that are likely to affect the rating outcomes of the companies in the power transmission sector. This rating methodology updates and supersedes ICRA's earlier methodology document on this subject, published in November 2023. While this revised version incorporates a few additional clarifications and editorial changes, ICRA's overall approach to rating entities in the sector remains materially similar. 

# **Overview** 

The power transmission infrastructure enables the transfer of electricity from the power generation stations to the demand centres through high-voltage transmission lines. The power transmission sector in India is dominated by the Central and state utilities, with Power Grid Corporation of India Limited (PGCIL) (a Government of India (GoI) enterprise) managing the inter-regional and inter-state network, while the state transmission utilities manage the intra-state network. The inter-state transmission projects are governed as per the regulations approved by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC), while the intra-state transmission projects are governed by regulations notified by the State Electricity Regulatory Commission(s) (SERCs). The sector has witnessed large scale investments since 2007 with the installed transmission line network increasing at a CAGR of 5.2% between March 2007, and March 2025 and the sub-station capacity increasing at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 9.6% during this period. This has enabled the interregional transmission capacity to increase from 14 GW as of March 2007 to 119 GW as of March 2025. Nonetheless, there continues to be constraints in certain pockets and there is a need for strengthening the transmission network amid the growing share of renewable energy (RE) in the electricity generation mix, necessitating the transfer of power from renewable-rich regions to demand centres. The National Electricity Plan (NEP), notified by the GoI, proposes to increase the transmission line length from 494,374 circuit kilo metres (ckm) as of March 2025 to 648,190 ckm by March 2032 and the substation capacity from 1,338 GVA as of March 2025 to 2,345 GVA by March 2032, thereby increasing the inter-regional transmission capacity to 168 GW by March 2032. This expansion aims to integrate over 600 GW of RE capacity by 2032. 

The National Tariff Policy notified by the Ministry of Power, GoI, in January 2006 opened the power transmission segment for private sector participation through the tariff-based competitive bidding route. The Central Government has notified PFC Consulting Limited and REC Power Development and Consultancy Limited as the nodal agencies or the bid process coordinators for awarding independent power transmission projects (IPTPs) through competitive bidding. The projects are being awarded on a build, own, operate and maintain (BOOM) basis (new projects are being awarded through the build, own, operate and transfer (BOOT) basis) with annual transmission charges (comprising non-escalable and escalable components) as the bidding parameter.

The bid process coordinator sets up a special purpose vehicle (SPV) for developing the designated transmission project, which is transferred to the winning developer. In case of inter-state transmission projects, the winning bidder signs a long-term (typically for 35-years) transmission service agreement (TSA) with the Central Transmission Utility (CTU)/ long-term transmission customers 

The CTU function has been carved out from PGCIL to its 100% subsidiary, the Central Transmission Utility of India Limited. The CTU is responsible for billing and collecting the transmission charges from all transmission system customers and for distributing the pooled amount to the transmission licensees in proportion to their share of transmission charges in the pool. Also, state-owned distribution utilities have limited alternatives in terms of transmission infrastructure, and transmission, unlike generation, constitutes a small proportion of their payables, thereby ensuring payment of transmission charges. Therefore, the counter-party credit risks for inter-state transmission projects are low due to diversification in the credit risk of state-owned distribution utilities on an all-India basis and associated pooling benefit. 

During the under-construction phase, the risks remain high for the transmission projects owing to the challenges in securing right of way and statutory approvals, mainly related to forest clearance (wherever applicable). However, once the project is operational, the revenue profile is expected to remain stable, considering the availability-linked payments and long tenure of the TSAs. Moreover, the collections are timely for the inter-state projects supported by the pooling mechanism. On the other hand, the intra-state transmission projects remain exposed to the credit profile of the respective state distribution utilities.

# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

### **Regulatory Risk** 

The power sector is governed by regulators at the Central as well as the state level, wherein the CERC is responsible for specifying the regulatory framework within the terms of the legislation, which acts as a guide to the SERCs. The CERC is also

responsible for determining the tariff for the Central sector utilities and where inter-state sale and transmission of power are involved. The SERCs are responsible for framing regulations governing the generation, transmission and distribution of electricity at the state level, and determining the tariff for state utilities and the tariff payable by the consumers to the distribution utility. 

In case of a Central or state-owned transmission utility, where the tariffs are cost-plus based, the risks arise from the timeliness as well as adequacy of the tariff determined by the regulators. Also, the revisions to normative tariff principles and their impact on the financial profile of the power transmission companies with cost plus tariff could be another risk. In case of a change in law and force majeure events impacting power transmission projects, the timeliness and adequacy of tariff revisions by the regulators are important. In addition, the regulatory environment in terms of timeliness and adequacy of tariff determination by the SERCs for state distribution utilities remains significant. Any delays in the tariff determination process (which can be attributed to either delays in petition filing by the discom or delays in issuance of tariff order by the SERC) or nonimplementation (or roll-back by the state government) of either the tariff order or fuel and power purchase cost adjustment framework is a credit concern. This is because the same has an adverse impact on the cash flows of the distribution utilities, which in turn increases the counter-party credit risks for transmission projects. While the risk of delays in issuance of tariff orders by the regulators remains similar for state transmission and distribution utilities, the impact of such delays is less prominent for transmission utilities compared to distribution utilities as there are no major uncontrollable costs like power purchase cost for transmission utilities. 

In case of transmission projects, the transmission service provider (TSP) signs a long-term (typically for 35-years) TSA with customers. However, the transmission licence granted by CERC/SERC is for 25 years, as per the Electricity Act, 2003 and relevant regulations. Hence, the TSP must apply for license renewal before the expiry, thereby exposing it to approval risk to continue operations beyond 25 years as per TSA tenure. 

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

Only the greenfield or expansion projects are exposed to permitting, funding and construction risk. 

## **Permitting Risk** 

Permitting risk refers to the ability of the entity to secure the right of way (RoW) and all statutory clearances required for constructing and operating a power transmission project, as well as compliance with the applicable norms. While assessing permitting risk for the projects under implementation, ICRA evaluates the progress on land acquisition/ RoW and examines the status of various clearances in accordance with the laws of the land. These clearances are generally required from the CERC/ SERC, the Ministry of Environment, Forests and Climate Change (MoEF), the GoI (in case of use of forest land), the Power and Telecommunications Coordination Committee (PTCC), the National/ State Highway Authority (for highway crossings), the Railway Authority (for railway line crossings), and so on. Since the process of acquiring various clearances is prone to delays, this remains one of the key focus areas while assigning ratings to greenfield or expansion projects. Once the company receives a transmission licence from the regulator and approvals under Sections 68 and 164 of the Electricity Act, the company has the right to acquire the RoW for the transmission lines, which mitigates this risk to some extent. In case of a Central or state-owned power transmission utility, the permitting risk is limited to the expansion projects under its portfolio. 

## **Funding Risk** 

The entity’s ability to tie up the requisite finances, status of the funding tie-up and capital structure mix are the focus of the analysis here. Given the capital-intensive nature of the power transmission projects and the typical debt-equity ratio of 70:30 or 75:25 adopted by the developers, such projects tend to have a high leverage. While equity would be arranged by the sponsor, the developer would be dependent on banks, financial institutions (FIs) and bond markets for debt funding. ICRA evaluates the extent of the actual funding tie-up and the likelihood of the balance funding being available in time, so that

Even though project finance is generally ‘non-recourse’ in nature, the strength of the sponsors is an important risk mitigant as it imparts financial flexibility in terms of funding cost overruns or to meet other contingencies. In case of a state-owned transmission utility, any funding support available from the state government in the form of equity, grants or soft loans with long maturity to partially-fund the capital expenditure (capex) schemes are viewed favourably, as it reduces the external debt burden on the utilities. 

# **Construction Risk** 

Construction risks refer to risks associated with the physical construction of a transmission project, which includes the erection of transmission lines and the associated sub-station (if any). This risk is dependent on the location of the project, wherein transmission lines passing through forests and/ or hilly terrain are exposed to higher construction risks, as opposed to lines passing through plain areas. Delays in construction would lead to time and cost overruns. Fixed-price, fixed-time contracts, with adequate clauses for liquidated damages (LD) and the strength of the counterparty to pay such LDs are usually the mitigating factors against construction risk (essentially, the risk gets transferred to the Engineering, Procurement & Construction (EPC) contractor). ICRA evaluates the status of project construction vis-à-vis the scheduled commercial operation date (COD) while assessing construction risks. Further, ICRA carries out a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of the delay in commissioning on the project cost (including due to increase in interest during construction), projected cash flows and debt servicing ability. The LD clauses, which are a part of the contract with the EPC contractor, are also evaluated to assess their adequacy for the loss of profits and/ or LD payable by the entity under the TSA. Further, in case of transmission projects linked to a particular generation project, the impact of delays in commissioning of the generation station on the cash flows of the transmission project is evaluated.

# **Operating Risk** 

The operating efficiency of the IPTPs is linked with the entity’s ability to maintain the transmission line availability as specified in the TSA. In case of a Central or state-owned transmission utility, the target transmission system availability is approved by the CERC or the SERC, as applicable. If the actual line availability is lower than the target availability as per the TSA or as approved by CERC/ SERC (typically 98%), the transmission charges are reduced on a pro-rata basis. Further, availability below a certain level (typically 95%) attracts penalty. However, the project would be eligible for incentives if the actual availability is higher than the target availability. The operating risk is higher for transmission projects located in hilly terrains and/ or in locations prone to extreme weather conditions, which could lead to greater wear and tear and in turn to higher operation and maintenance (O&M) costs. While evaluating operating risks, ICRA assesses the track record of the line availability in relation to the normative availability and takes comfort from projects having a satisfactory operating track record of at least 12 months. Further, most operational transmission assets have a demonstrated history of line availability and any impact on the line availability tends to be temporary, reflecting low operating risk for such assets. ICRA also evaluates the experience of the O&M contractor and the mitigating factors available in the O&M contract such as LD clauses for any shortfall in line availability below

# **Demand and Tariff Risk** 

The demand risk for power transmission projects is low, given the presence of a long-term TSA with availability-linked payment of transmission charges and natural monopoly of the projects. Further, ICRA evaluates the nature of tariff structure to assess the tariff risk for the project. The transmission charges are paid on a monthly basis, as per the bid quoted by the winning developer. This provides a stable and certain revenue stream for the projects over the period of the TSA. ICRA also factors in the tariff adjustment, if the same is allowed by the CERC or the SERC under change in law or any other uncontrollable factors (i.e., beyond the company’s control) with a bearing on either the capital or operating cost. In case of projects under the regulated tariff mechanism, mainly applicable for Central / state transmission utilities, the tariffs are cost-plus based, which is approved as per the tariff regulations notified by the CERC or the SERC. For such utilities, the timelines in issuance of the tariff order along with the true-up orders for previous years and any unrecovered revenue gaps are evaluated. In case of a transmission project, which is ready for commissioning and is not being able to commence operations for reasons not attributable to the project company, the transmission system is deemed to be commissioned as per the timelines agreed in the TSA and would be eligible to receive the monthly transmission charges.

# **Counterparty Credit Risk and Receivable Days** 

In assessing the counterparty credit quality and receivable days, ICRA evaluates the collection mechanism, the credit profile of the counterparty, the payment security mechanism and the track record of payments from the transmission system customers. 

**Framework for inter-state projects:** Under the framework introduced by the CERC in 2010, the CTU is responsible for collecting the transmission charges from all the designated customers of inter-state transmission services and this amount is pooled together to make payments to various transmission licensees as per the respective TSAs. Under this mechanism, the transmission projects are not exposed to any single counter party and the impact of any shortfall in collections from any customer is collectively borne by all the transmission licensees, in proportion to their revenue share in the pool. As a result, the counter-party credit risks for inter-state transmission projects under the pooling mechanism are low due to the diversification in the credit risk of state-owned distribution utilities on an all-India basis and associated pooling benefit, against the intra-state transmission projects that are mainly exposed to the counter-party credit risk of state-owned distribution utilities in their respective states. Moreover, the projects have the benefit of the payment security mechanism available in the form of the letter of credit provided by the transmission system customers to the CTU along with the levers available with the

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

## **Adequacy of Future Cash Flows** 

Since the prime objective of the rating exercise is to assess the adequacy of the rated entity’s debt servicing capability, ICRA draws up projections on the likely financial position of the issuer under various scenarios, in addition to reviewing the past financials. The future cash flows are projected after considering the tariff as per the TSA, the O&M cost as per the contractual terms, interest cost, debt repayment schedule and working capital requirements. These cash flows are then used to determine the company’s future debt-servicing capability. 

The various financial metrics assessed by ICRA could be divided into three categories - profitability, leverage, coverage and liquidity. This document provides a summary of why ICRA considers these ratios to be important. For a more detailed description, readers may refer to the note titled, ‘Approach for Financial Ratio Analysis’, published on ICRA’s website. 

## **Profitability** 

The profitability for a power transmission project is linked to the transmission line availability and operating expenses, primarily related to the O&M of the transmission project. Given the finite life of transmission projects, the rating focusses on internal rate of return indicators (IRR). ICRA assesses the project’s IRR in relation to the company’s weighted average cost of capital. In case of projects based on competitively bid tariff without any cost pass through, returns depend upon the project developers’ ability to maintain the capital costs and operating parameters within the budgeted levels. For projects, with costplus based tariff, the actual returns depend on the company’s ability to maintain the capital costs and operating parameters within the normative levels. 

## **Leverage and Coverage Indicators** 

The power transmission projects are generally funded with a heavy dependence on external debt leading to high leverage level. However, the stability in revenues and profitability for these assets leads to lower volatility in debt servicing indicators, despite the higher leverage. As a result, the transmission projects can withstand relatively lower debt coverage metrics when compared with, e.g. renewable power projects, which are exposed to variability in generation. Also, debt with a long maturity profile and competitive interest rate can partially offset the risk associated with a high financial leverage. While ICRA looks at leverage (total debt to operating profits) in its analysis, the focus is on debt coverage indicators like cumulative debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and minimum DSCR over the debt repayment tenure. ICRA evaluates multiples scenarios to assess the DSCR over the debt repayment period with the key sensitive variables, which include the project cost overrun, line availability and cost of debt. However, for transmission utilities which operate as a going concern corporate entity with new projects regularly being undertaken on the same balance sheet, rather than a transmission project with a finite life, the financial assessment is driven by a mix of leverage (total debt to operating profits) and coverage (DSCR) metrics. 

Typically, the developers infuse the required promoter contribution for a project SPV developing an IPTP through a mix of equity and unsecured debt. As per the terms of the loan agreement, such promoter debt generally remains subordinated to the project debt from lenders and the payments on the promoter debt are allowed by lenders, subject to meeting the stipulated restricted payment conditions. Also, the subordinate debt holders do not have a right to call an event of default leading to either acceleration of the debt payment or initiation of bankruptcy proceedings. In such instances, with presence of an escrow/ cash flow waterfall mechanism monitored by the lenders/ trustee, the coverage metrics for the project SPV are assessed on the external project debt.

## **Liquidity** 

Liquidity is the measure of an entity’s ability to meet its short-term cash obligations from various internal or external resources. For a power transmission project or power transmission utility, internal resources include cash flows as per the agreed tariff under the TSA based on the project’s line availability, debt service reserve account (DSRA) and free cash balances. External resources include undrawn lines of credit or equity capital. The short-term obligations include operating costs, debt servicing obligations, working capital requirements and capex (if any). The higher the cushion available between the resources available (especially internal resources) and the obligations, the better is the entity’s liquidity profile. ICRA also notes that the liquidity available with an entity may be for a limited period and hence an entity’s overall policy towards maintaining adequate liquidity in the form of a DSRA, undrawn working capital lines and free cash balances is accorded due importance in the analytical approach. ICRA evaluates the transaction documents to ascertain the availability of the DSRA prior to a default, to ensure timely debt servicing. If the DSRA is available for use only post default or is contingent on meeting certain conditions, ICRA does not consider the DSRA for the liquidity assessment. 

For an IPTP, surplus cash flows post meeting the financial covenants stipulated by the lenders, and after maintaining liquidity reserves and as per the cash flow waterfall mechanism are assumed to be paid out to the sponsor and/or used for debt prepayment under cash sweep mechanism of the loan agreement. 

# **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Financial Flexibility** 

An entity’s financial flexibility (or the lack thereof) is reflected in its ability to access the capital or the money markets at short notice, attract diverse and marquee investors and enjoy the confidence of banks, FIs and intermediaries. A strong financial flexibility allows an entity to raise fresh borrowings or refinance existing ones in quick time and whenever required. For a power transmission project, financial flexibility could arise from factors such as the tail period<sup>_1_</sup> available in the TSA, and/ or strong parentage or linkages with a strong group. In case of entities having bullet debt repayment, ICRA examines the refinancing ability of such projects by assessing the expected cash flows from the balance tenure of the project life. Entities having bullet debt repayment towards the end of the licence period would be exposed to approval risk to continue operations

A sign such as this may imply that the entity might be persuaded to distribute high dividends or support the promoter group through other means to the detriment of its own credit profile. These could translate into a sharp decline in market capitalisation or spike in bond yields and consequently constrain an entity’s ability to raise fresh capital or materially increase its cost of capital. 

# **Tenure Mismatches and Risks Relating to Interest Rates and Refinancing** 

Given the capital-intensive nature of power transmission projects, which are largely debt-funded, and their long TSA tenure with steady revenues, a long debt maturity profile with debt servicing obligations well spread out over the debt tenure aids to maintain a comfortable liquidity and financial profile. Dependence on short-term borrowings to fund long-term investments can expose a company to significant refinancing risks, especially during periods of tight systemic liquidity. ICRA evaluates the extent of such mismatches and the mitigating factors therein. One source of mitigation could be the existence of adequate buffers of liquid assets/ committed bank lines to meet short-term obligations. Another source of mitigation could be the entity’s strong financial flexibility to be able to garner fresh funds at a short notice or a potent ability to refinance. ICRA also evaluates the impact of movement in interest rates on the debt coverage indicators of the entity. 

# **Foreign Currency-related Risks** 

The foreign currency risk can arise from unhedged foreign currency liabilities pertaining to funding of capex and/or working capital. ICRA’s analytical focus is on assessing the magnitude of such exposure, relative to the entity’s profits. The hedging policy of the issuer concerned is assessed in the context of the company’s entire cash flows being denominated in domestic currency. Firm hedging, if not for the entire tenure of debt, then at least for the near-term foreign exchange repayment commitments, is viewed favourably. 

# **Structural Features Relating to the Debt Instruments/Facilities** 

ICRA reviews the debt transaction structure to evaluate features available to provide additional protection to the lenders/bondholders through presence of the escrow mechanism and the ring fencing of cash flows. The assessment involves a review of the cash flow waterfall mechanism which spells out the payment priority towards statutory dues, debt servicing, operating expenses, creation of reserve funds for debt servicing, besides the stipulation of covenants including minimum coverage ratios that must be met before payouts to subordinate debt holders (mainly sponsor debt) or declaration of dividends. This apart, restriction on the ability of the project company to take on additional debt and/or capital expansion without the approval of the lenders/bondholders and implementation of cash sweep mechanism to use surplus cash flows for debt prepayment by the lenders are viewed favourably by ICRA.

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts and auditors’ comments that are part of the annual report of the rated entity. Any deviation from the standard accounting standards are evaluated and the financial statements of the entity are

# **Contingent Liabilities/Off-balance Sheet Exposures** 

For this, the likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/off-balance sheet exposures and the financial implications of the same are evaluated while factoring the mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion. 

# **Force Majeure Risk** 

Like any infrastructure project, power transmission projects are also vulnerable to force majeure events. While this is an event risk, the presence of force majeure clauses in the TSAs limits the company’s liability arising from non-performance or underperformance. ICRA examines if, and the extent to which, the force majeure risks are mitigated through insurance contracts or by specific provisions in the TSA that cover such eventualities, like deemed availability clause. The strength of these mitigating factors influences the overall financial flexibility of an entity. 

# **Event Risks** 

ICRA recognises the possibility of events such as unrelated diversification, mergers and acquisitions, business restructuring, asset sales and spin-offs, litigations, equity infusion and refinancing, which could have a material impact on the credit profile of an entity. It is often difficult to incorporate the impact of such discrete events in the credit rating from the beginning. Depending on whether and when such events occur, the rating opinion could be substantially different. To take rating decisions in such cases, ICRA applies its analytical judgement based on the rated entity’s track record, the credibility of the management and the experience of having seen similar situations play out in other entities. However, given the nature of such events, it is possible that the rating may undergo a material change later, upon the occurrence of the event. 

# **Parentage** 

Despite the non-recourse nature of the debt availed by the issuers for IPTPs, the financial strength of the parent/sponsor is a credit factor, given that apart from contributing the equity capital and/or subordinated debt, a stronger sponsor has greater wherewithal to meet any funding shortfalls faced by the SPV, while also ensuring timely financial closure of the project. Further, during the pre-COD stage, many projects tend to take recourse to the sponsor for cost overrun support. 

If the parent’s or promoter group credit profile is relatively stronger than the rated entity, ICRA assesses the ability and the likelihood of the parent extending extraordinary support to the entity. Support here means financial support from the parent expected to be available to the entity in the form of loans, equity, extended credit period, advances, etc. in times of credit or liquidity stress on the entity. ‘Support’ does not mean ongoing or committed financial support from the parent, such as the capital infusion to fund the rated entity’s expansion plans or support to maintain a targeted capital structure, but the extraordinary support which cannot be foreseen, is intended to avoid a default and thus cannot be factored-in while assessing the notional standalone rating of the entity.  It may be noted that promoters in their individual capacity, or private equity firms/other financial investors are generally not treated as parents for assessing the likelihood of extraordinary financial support coming in. 

While the weak financial health of the sponsor constrains its ability to support the project entity in a time of need, presence of a financially strong sponsor need not necessarily mean assured support to fund the entity in case of any funding shortfall. In the absence of explicit support (guarantees/shortfall undertakings), the sponsors’ willingness to support the operational projects is determined by the long-term project attractiveness, which is a function of the strength of the project cash flows and duration of the tail period. Also, ICRA considers the track record of the sponsor in extending timely support to any of its other projects and gauge the intent of the sponsor to extend extraordinary support to the rated entity based on the interactions with the sponsor.

# **Management Quality Assessment** 

In addition to the industry, business and financial risk analysis, all credit ratings incorporate an assessment of the quality of the rated entity’s management and the financial policies. 

## **Quality of Management and Financial Policies** 

As a part of its process, ICRA undertakes discussions with the rated entity’s management to understand its views on past performance as well as its future plans and strategies, besides the outlook on the industry. Some of the points assessed are: 

- Experience of the promoter/management in the line of business concerned 

- Commitment of the promoter/management to the rated entity 

- Risk appetite of the promoter/management and risk mitigation plans 

- Policies on leveraging, managing interest rates and currency risks 

- Management’s past success in introducing new projects and managing changes in the external environment 

- • Management’s plans on new projects, acquisitions, expansion, etc. 

Periodic interactions with the management help in ascertaining the shifts, if any, in the issuer’s financial policies. 

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environment (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate all relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly disaggregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to fully or precisely disaggregate the subcomponents of E&S risks in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, but their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence, these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or carbon transition risks such as those arising from changes in regulations or other environmental and social risks and seeks to understand the various mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks.

Power transmission companies are exposed to the risk of natural disasters and extreme weather conditions, which could damage the equipment and disrupt operations. Nonetheless, the entities avail insurance against such risks, including against loss of profits due to business interruption and the TSAs include a provision for deemed availability in case of certain force majeure events. This clause enables the entity to claim the transmission charges under the TSA. As power transmission projects are agnostic to the underlying source of power and given the presence of long tenured TSAs with availability-linked payments, these projects are unlikely to face any carbon transition risk. 

Also, there have been challenges in completing land acquisition for transmission projects, and social risks manifest, when there are disagreements over the compensation between the developers and the landowners. Further, projects passing through forests face delays in receiving statutory approvals, owing to concerns over impact of such projects on the flora and fauna. This apart, the discoms face social risks in the form of resistance against tariff hikes from the political establishment and the public, leading to deferment or roll-back of the tariff hikes, thereby adversely impacting the profitability and cash flows of the discoms. This manifests as a risk for power transmission companies in the form of heightened counterparty credit risk, especially for intra-state transmission projects. 

## **Governance Practices** 

A sound corporate governance structure should clearly delineate the roles and responsibilities of the Board of Directors and the management. The composition of an entity’s Board, its involvement in strategic decision making and the entity’s compliance with the legal and regulatory requirements are factored in during credit assessments. ICRA also seeks to gain a qualitative understanding of the entity’s commitment to follow transparent and credible practices, as reflected in the presentation of the financial statements, timeliness and depth of disclosures, consistency in communication and the openness about sharing information during the rating process. Additionally, factors such as the complexity of the corporate group structure, related-party transactions, instances of financial support to group entities at the expense of debt holders, and any abrupt resignations of auditors or independent directors are evaluated. 

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the company’s business and financial risks, likely cash flows over the life of the instrument being rated and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis its debt-servicing obligations. 

As this note highlights, for power transmission projects, project risks assume importance during the implementation phase, while post COD, it is the adequacy of cash flow generation vis-à-vis the debt servicing commitments that primarily influence the rating. The certainty of future cash flows is relatively high for power transmission projects, given that the revenues are linked to line availability. Nonetheless, ICRA evaluates the impact of key variables such as capital cost (for under-construction projects), line availability and cost of debt on the debt-servicing metrics. Further, ICRA considers the exposure to the counterparty credit risks and associated mitigating factors like pooling mechanism for inter-state projects. ICRA’s rating approach also involves an assessment of the entity’s management quality and governance practices. In addition to these considerations, an entity’s credit rating may also be influenced by its ownership, the nature of linkages with its parent or group entities, degree of financial flexibility, the corporate legal structure, track record of operations and that of debt servicing, and vulnerability (if any) to discrete event risks. In case of a Central or state-owned transmission utility, the credit quality generally remains linked with that of the concerned Central / state governments, given the significant linkages and the fact that the power sector remains strategically important to the Central and the state governments.
