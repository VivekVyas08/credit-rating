# **Overview** 

Over the past two decades, several road projects have been executed through the Public Private Partnership (PPP) mode under the Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) model, through BOT-Toll, BOT-Annuity and BOT-Hybrid Annuity Mode (HAM)<sup>1</sup> . This document provides an overview of ICRA’s methodology for rating BOT-Toll roads. These road projects are generally implemented through project-specific special purpose vehicles (SPVs), in which the debt is raised to fund a part of the project cost. The bidding parameter for BOT-Toll road projects is either the lowest grant (viability gap funding) or the highest premium (or negative grant, payable to the authority). The main source of revenues for these SPVs is the toll revenue, which is used to meet the contractual obligations, including operational and maintenance (O&M) expenses (routine and major maintenance), payments to authority (in case of negative grant or revenue share) and debt servicing obligations. 

_-_ _<u>Key risk factors and rating considerations for BOT Toll road projects</u>_ 

From a credit perspective, many of the challenges involved in developing road projects are similar to those faced by other infrastructure projects. 

The BOT-Toll road projects are exposed to execution risks (delays in securing right of way (RoW), MOEF<sup>_2_</sup> clearances, engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contractor’s execution capability and track record, etc), funding risks if the debt and equity are not fully tied up, interest rate risks, operation and maintenance risks and most importantly traffic risks (as toll collections is the main source of revenue). For an under-construction road project, execution and funding risks are prominent and can lead to cost and time overruns as well as loss of toll revenues in case of delayed project completion. Although acquisition of the RoW is the responsibility of the project owner (generally the National Highway Authority of India (NHAI) or the state road development authority), delays in timely handing over of the required RoW, post the start of construction, can result in time and cost overruns in project implementation. The likelihood of an under-construction project taking recourse to the sponsor for financial support is generally higher than in the case of an operational project.

Toll road projects undergo significant change in their risk profile once they go past the commercial operations date (COD). While execution and funding risks dominate the pre-COD period, the primary risk in the post-COD period is that of the revenues (a market risk) which depends on the toll-able traffic on the project road, the sensitivity to toll rates and the commuters’ willingness to pay the toll, along with the O&M risk. Generally, for a project stretch with a toll collection track record of two to three years, the assumptions of toll-able traffic are relatively more reliable compared to a project where there is no history of toll collection on the stretch. In the latter case, assumptions for toll collections are generally based on traffic studies and remain a key rating sensitivity. 

A toll road project’s rating may see a significant change post the COD as the project implementation risks abate and the uncertainty associated with the project’s toll collections is reduced with the commencement of toll collection and track record of toll-able traffic on the project stretch.

# **Credit Risk Assessment Framework** 

This rating methodology explains ICRA’s approach to assessing the business and financial risk profiles of BOT (Toll) road projects. It aims to help issuers, investors and other interested market participants understand ICRA’s approach to analysing the quantitative and qualitative risks that are likely to affect rating outcomes in this sector. The list of rating drivers covered in this methodology is not exhaustive by itself but provides an overall perspective on the rating considerations that are usually considered the most important. For analytical convenience, the key factors are grouped under the following broad heads— Business Risk Assessment, Financial Risk Assessment and Sponsor Risk Assessment, among others.

# **Industry Risk Assessment** 

## **Industry Risk** 

### **Competitive Intensity** 

The competitive intensity is limited for operational projects as once the project is awarded, there exists a natural monopoly till the concession period. However, at the time of the bidding for the projects, the competition for award of projects varies from project to project and is typically a function of the attractiveness of the route over the concession period. Relative to other sectors, the level of competition intensity has not been among the central risk factors for this sector. Nevertheless, with the emergence of new expressways and alternative routes, and alternate means of transportation (e.g., dedicated freight corridor or coastal shipping), the risk of diversion of traffic exists. However, the provision for extension in concession period in case of shortfall/decline in traffic as compared to base case estimates during the traffic testing provides comfort to an extent. 

### **Economic Cyclicality** 

BOT toll road projects remain inherently exposed to economic cyclicality, as traffic volumes closely track the overall macroeconomic conditions. These assets are also influenced by the pace of industrial, commercial, and demographic activity in the surrounding region, which drives freight and passenger traffic movement. Consequently, any slowdown in regional or nationwide economic activity can materially impact the traffic and toll collections, thereby impacting the overall project cash flows. 

### **Regulatory Risk** 

Regulatory intervention manifests in multiple forms, including developing new road networks, toll exemption for certain vehicle categories, or increase in load-carrying capacity of trucks, etc. 

Development of new road networks or other modes of transport (such as rail or waterways) may impact the existing network by directly competing with some of the existing BOT (Toll) road projects. In cases where toll exemption to certain vehicle categories is given, the compensation mechanism from the respective road authorities / project owner could have a direct bearing on the coverage metrics.

In most cases, state governments being party to the State Support Agreement are expected to support and facilitate land acquisition, cooperate with the authority and the concessionaires in the implementation and operation of the projects – viz. not to levy local taxes, provide police assistance when required for traffic regulation, remove trespassers, and not construct competing roads/additional tollways. Regulatory changes such as revision in axle load norms for commercial vehicles also have an impact on traffic growth, as witnessed in the past. 

Nevertheless, with implementation on FASTag and proactive measures by the authority to address some of the concerns of stakeholders including premium deferment, changes to model concession agreement for upcoming projects, faster approvals for refinancing and change in ownership and enhanced dispute resolution mechanism, the overall impact of any adverse regulatory intervention has been limited for the sector with strong policy support. 

# **Business Risk Assessment** 

## **Execution Risks** 

- **Permitting risk** 

A key component of the execution risk is the permitting risk, which refers to a project’s ability to acquire requisite statutory clearances prior to the commencement of construction activity. The project owner (i.e., authority) usually assumes the permitting risk and undertakes to indemnify the project SPV against losses arising out of any disputes with respect to the acquisition of land. The permitting risks are usually low where the project involves upgradation of an existing operating stretch, however, such risks are significant where projects involve construction of entirely new stretches (including bypasses), where considerable amount of land needs to be acquired. Wherever available, the lenders’ independent engineer (LIE) report or the correspondence between the concessionaire, the independent engineer and the authority are relied upon to assess the status of the various approvals. 

- **Cost and time overruns** 

   - **Cost overrun:** The project cost is dependent on several variables, which include the nature of the surface (flexible versus rigid), the number of lanes, the terrain of the project stretch, and the number of structures (bridges, culverts, rail over bridges), which need to be constructed. A substantial variation between the authority’s base cost and the bid submitted by the developer or between L1 and L2 bids, may reflect aggressive bidding, which could lead to cost overruns. 

   - **Time overrun:** Road construction projects often face challenges related to the right-of-way acquisition, mobilisation of labour, equipment, and key raw material availability (aggregate, sand, etc). Further, factors such as difficult terrain, weather and labour problems contribute to construction delays, with such time overruns in effect translating into cost escalations. 

Some of these risks could be partially mitigated through fixed-price, fixed-time EPC contracts with an established and financially sound construction company having track record in executing similar nature projects. 

## **Funding Risks** 

- **Equity mobilisation risk** 

The ability of the sponsors to infuse equity (promoter’s contribution) in a timely manner remains crucial for timely completion of the project. The financial strength of the sponsor is a key credit determinant during the pre-COD stage, given that apart from contributing the promoter’s share in the form of equity capital and/or subordinated debt, the sponsor is directly or indirectly responsible for ensuring the financial closure of these projects as well as fund cost overrun, if any.

The grant payments from authority during the construction period are linked to the physical progress of the project. Substantial delay in the release of grant could also results in time overruns. Therefore, the timeliness of grant payments from the authority remains important for timely completion of project. In addition, the project cost generally have provision for the interest during construction (IDC) and contingency in case of some cost/time overrun. 

# **Revenue risk** 

Post completion of construction activity, toll road projects are primarily exposed to the risks associated with the build-up of traffic<sup>_3_</sup> volumes and the users’ willingness to pay tolls as per the prescribed rates. The traffic risks associated with such stretches are primarily dependent on the following factors: 

- The economic utility of the stretch; in this context, the projects which serve a captive demand, such as stretches which connects major cities, ports or city bypasses which relieve congestion levels, could be said to bear relatively lower traffic risks. Further, stretches which are a part of important corridors including Golden Quadrilateral, East West/North South corridors and routes connecting key economic centres including Delhi-Mumbai/Bangalore-Chennai are exposed to relatively lower traffic risk. 

- The growth prospects of the service area, play a critical role in determining the longer term traffic potential of the asset. Strong industrial, commercial, and residential expansion in the catchment area of the asset typically supports higher traffic volumes and higher toll collections. Conversely, subdued regional development can constrain traffic growth expectations and weaken the project’s cash flow stability. 

- The availability of alternative freeways and other competing modes of transport (such as railways), which could lead to traffic diversion 

- The composition of traffic, along the stretch and the sensitivity of various user segments towards payment of tolls—while commercial traffic is more remunerative when compared to passenger traffic, it remains exposed to economic cycles. 

In case of an existing asset with a long track record of toll collection, the traffic movement may be well established, and historical traffic data helps in gauging the trends and seasonality of traffic. However, for stretches with limited track record of toll collections, forecasting future traffic volumes and measuring the market risks associated with toll road projects is challenging, given the absence of reliable and sufficiently long historical traffic data in most parts of the country. In such cases, ICRA, therefore, relies on traffic studies conducted by independent agencies to assess the future traffic potential for these projects and are suitably sensitised.  Further, in some specific cases, particularly state highways, alternative sources of revenue could also be significant – such as those projects which have land development rights along the corridor under management – could also provide interim cashflow support to improve project viability. 

# **Operating risk** 

The road is required to be maintained as per the service level requirements stated in the concession agreement. Noncompliance with some of the performance parameters may result in financial penalties and/or impinge on the concessionaire’s right to collect tolls. Further, in the event of poor maintenance, attractiveness of the road may diminish making commuters shift to alternative stretches, if any. Therefore, the concessionaire’s ability to fairly assess operating expenses and lay down a proper maintenance schedule is important. 

Operations and maintenance (O&M) expenses for road projects are primarily of two types: 

- Major maintenance (MM), which involves the relaying of the bituminous-concrete (in the case of a flexible pavement) or replacement of the concrete slabs (top layer, in the case of a rigid pavement), is undertaken to maintain the project stretch as per the maintenance requirements stipulated in the CA. 

- Routine operations and maintenance involve salaries of staff, repair/maintenance and such other activities.

Track record of the O&M contractor, which is typically the sponsor, in operating and maintaining toll assets provides comfort and mitigates operating risk to an extent. ICRA’s assumptions on expenses towards O&M (including major maintenance) are based on empirical costs observed in other similar projects. Additionally, the assumptions attempt to have adequate in-built buffers to account for the potential rise in expenses owing to inflation. Hence, maintenance of adequate reserves for O&M and MM expenses remain critical. 

# **Inherent profitability of the projects** 

Given the finite life of the road projects, highly capital-intensive nature of the road projects and modest operational expenses, the emphasis is on the internal rate of return and net present value (IRR and NPV) instead of the conventional profitability indicators. Aggressive bidding could lead to sub-optimal IRR eventually. The sponsors may not have the economic incentive for supporting projects with weak IRR, especially in cases where there is a possibility of invocation of concessionaire event of default clause or in cases where lenders exercise their substitution rights.

# **Financial Risk Assessment** 

## **Stability of cash flows** 

Two toll road projects with similar set of debt coverage metrics could be rated differently depending on the stability of cash flows – the one with relatively more stable cash flows could be rated higher<sup>_4_</sup> when compared to the one with high variability in cash flows. Here emphasis is laid on the stability of cash flows and ability of the operator to exercise better control over operating costs. Projects whose operations are stabilised i.e. with at least two to three years of operational track record are better from a cash flow predictability perspective. The users’ willingness to pay and base traffic will be established during this period. On the expenses front, the concessionaire’s ability to manage operations and maintenance costs within budgeted levels remains critical. 

For both the under-construction project as well as operational projects, the strength of the cash flows is tested under various sensitivity scenarios for ICRA projections. The key sensitivity scenarios include time and cost overruns during the construction phase and variability in traffic volumes, toll rates and O&M expenses post completion. 

## **Liquidity and debt service coverage metrics** 

Toll road assets are usually funded with a considerable proportion of external debt, although in some cases grants from the project owners serve to keep leverage at moderate levels. <mark>Typically, the sponsor infuses their required contribution for a project SPV through a mix of equity, hybrid instruments (like preference shares, perpetual debt or compulsory convertible debentures) or unsecured debt. As per the terms of the loan agreement, such sponsor debt typically remains subordinated to the project debt from lenders and the payments on the sponsor debt is allowed by lenders, subject to meeting the restricted payment conditions like maintaining the stipulated financial covenants, and the required reserves etc. Also, in several instances, the subordinated debt from sponsors does not have a right to call an event of default including acceleration of the subordinate debt payment or initiation of bankruptcy proceedings. In such instances, the coverage metrics for the project SPV are assessed on the external project debt, given the presence of an escrow/ cash flow ringfencing structure monitored by the lenders/ trustee.</mark> 

The minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and cumulative DSCR are the key coverage ratios which are analysed. The DSCR measures the cushion between the debt-servicing obligation and the cash flows available for debt-servicing (CFADS) in any given period. In addition, unencumbered cash balances and the debt service reserve account (DSRA), along with other reserves stipulated by the lenders provide liquidity support and help the entity tide over any delays or penalties from the authority. The ratios which are typically used by ICRA to analyse an entity’s coverage are:

Further, ICRA assesses the funding mix of the projects, where a higher proportion of capital infusion in the form equity compared to other quasi-equity/hybrid instruments (perpetual debt/optionally convertible debentures/unsecured loans) is viewed favourably as part of financial risk assessment. 

## **Interest rate and forex risks** 

Given the considerable reliance of road projects on external debt, the interest rate movement will have a direct bearing on the coverage metrics. The foreign currency risk can arise from unhedged liabilities, especially for entities with unhedged foreign currency borrowings, which could pertain to part-funding of the project cost. As there is limited scope for natural hedge, the focus here is on the hedging policy of the issuer to mitigate such risk for net foreign currency exposure. 

# **Debt Structure** 

Ceteris paribus, the tenure of the term debt is a key driver for the debt coverage as entities with longer tenure debt and similar levels of leverage will be more comfortably placed as compared to companies with shorter tenure debt. Also, a ballooning repayment structure is viewed favourably as the burden on cash flows is lower during the initial ramp up period of operations. The credit profile remains exposed to the terms and conditions associated with the infrastructure debt instruments being rated. 

While the financial risk assessment considers the capacity of the asset to generate adequate cash flows and the stability of those cash flows, the following debt structure analysis considers each tranched debt separately, considering the payment waterfall ranking and the structural features, amortisation structure and the associated refinance risks, and the covenant package. The credit analysis thus considers the various terms and conditions associated with the debt instruments being rated.

# **Amortisation structure, refinancing risk, and financial flexibility** 

While in most cases, the debt structure is fully amortising, there could be situations where some portion of the debt involves a bullet repayment and hence is exposed to refinancing risk.

That said, there is a risk of promoters leveraging projects with a high tail period to support other group projects and this could be an additional source of credit risk.

# **Other Elements of Credit Risk Assessment** 

## **Accounting quality** 

ICRA reviews the accounting policies, notes to accounts, auditors’ comments and other disclosures that are parts of the Annual Report of a rated entity. Any deviations from the standard accounting practices are evaluated and the financial statements are adjusted, where feasible, to reflect the impact of such deviations. Significant deviations may be indicative of weak corporate governance practices in the entity. 

## **Contingent liabilities/ Off-balance sheet exposures** 

ICRA, on a best effort basis, analyses the likelihood of devolvement of contingent liabilities/ off-balance sheet exposures and its impact on the entity’s financial implications while factoring the mitigants such as a strong liquidity cushion. 

## **Event risks** 

Given the limited ability to negotiate increased cost/revenue disruptions, the adverse effect of any event risk is more pronounced for the SPVs when compared to corporate entities. Upon occurrence of events like force majeure, changes in law and concessionaire/ authority event of default, the nature of the event and its impact on the cash flows is assessed. This is largely a post-facto assessment as factoring-in event risks at the outset may not be entirely possible. 

# **Sponsor Risk Assessment** 

All debt ratings necessarily incorporate an assessment of the quality of the issuer’s management, as well as the strengths/weaknesses arising from the issuer’s being a part of a group. Usually, a detailed discussion is held with the sponsor of the issuer to understand its business objectives, plans and strategies, and views on past performance. Some of the other points assessed are: 

- a. Experience of the sponsor in the line of business concerned 

- b. Track record of sponsor in adhering to its committed financial policies 

- c. Policies of the sponsor on leveraging, rationale for debt raising, balance between leverage and returns, interest risks and currency risks 

- d. The ability and willingness of the sponsor to support the issuer through measures such as fund infusion, if required 

Despite the non-recourse nature of the SPV, the financial strength of the sponsor is a key credit determinant during the preCOD stage, given that apart from contributing the promoter’s share in the form of equity capital and/or subordinated debt, the sponsor is also directly or indirectly responsible for ensuring the financial closure of these projects. Further, during the preCOD stage, most of the BOT (Toll) projects involve recourse to the sponsor for cost overrun support and shortfall in debt servicing in the event of termination. Post the project completion, while many SPVs enter into O&M contracts with the sponsors, the financial linkage is relatively lower during the post-COD period. 

While the weak financial health of the sponsor constrains its ability to support the project SPVs in times of need, presence of a financially strong sponsor need not necessarily mean assured support to fund SPVs in case of any funding shortfall. In the absence of explicit support (guarantees/ shortfall undertakings), the sponsors’ willingness to support the operational projects is determined by their skin in the game, which is a function of the strength of the project cash flows and their reputation sensitivity to default. Also, ICRA considers the past track record of the sponsor in extending timely support to any of its other SPVs and gauges the intent of the sponsor to extend extraordinary support to the rated SPV based on the interactions with the sponsor.

# **Assessment of Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Risks** 

The assessment of ESG risks by ICRA involves a broad range of considerations that pertain to the sustainability of an entity with focus on aspects that can have a material impact on its credit quality. While the E&S risks tend to be both sector-related as well as entity-specific and could be driven by external factors such as regulations or demographic changes, the G risks are largely entity-driven. The impact of the E&S risks on an entity’s credit profile tends to be asymmetric. If the ESG risks are material but unmitigated, these generally translate into pulling down the rating, but generally the ratings are not pushed up even when the ESG context is favourable. 

## **Environment (E) and Social (S) Risks** 

As this methodology highlights, while undertaking credit assessment of entities, ICRA seeks to incorporate relevant credit considerations into its rating decisions, while taking a forward-looking view on the risks and the mitigating elements. The relevant credit considerations include (sometimes overtly, sometimes covertly) the E&S factors that could affect the rated entity/ transaction. While ICRA’s analytical approach does not explicitly segregate these risks to assess their impact on the rating, these risks are often assessed broadly. Further, it is not always feasible to segregate the sub-components of E&S risks fully or precisely in credit analysis since these considerations often tend to overlap. 

That said, the materiality of the E&S risks and the time horizon over which they are expected to crystallise differs widely across sectors and entities. In some cases, while the E&S risks could be material, but their effect on the credit profile may be muted because of other fundamental strengths of the entity. In other cases, the adverse impact of the E&S risks is expected to play out in the distant future, and hence these considerations do not necessarily weigh on the rating today—with the expectation that when these risks manifest in the distant future, the rated entity by then would possibly adapt itself by realigning its business model. 

While evaluating the E&S risks, ICRA’s objective is only to assess the direct and indirect risks that an entity faces and how it already is or is intending to mitigate the impact of such risks on its credit profile. As an example, ICRA only assesses whether an entity is exposed to physical climate risks or other environmental and social risks (including resettlement and rehabilitation risks) and seeks to understand the various mitigation and adaptation approaches that the entity is implementing to mollify these risks. Notwithstanding the above, as an example, it is possible that even if an entity A has a higher carbon footprint than entity B, it does not materially affect ICRA’s credit opinion on entity A. This is because ICRA’s credit opinion on an entity considers a wide gamut of credit-relevant factors and the E&S factors are only one among those. 

The road sector is critical to economic growth and is closely linked to the transport sector, which is one of the major contributors to the greenhouse gas emissions. Alternative modes like rail and inland waterways are relatively less polluting. However, the travel time and the last-mile connectivity remain a challenge for these modes. Road developers remain exposed to natural disasters (such as cyclones, floods), which could interrupt construction activity or result in an increase in maintenance costs in case of any damage to carriageway, post the COD. Nevertheless, the availability of insurance for most projects mitigates the adverse effects of these circumstances. Given the large execution targets set by the Government for the road sector, there have been challenges in completing land acquisition, and social risks manifest when there are disagreements over-compensation between the developers and the landowners. Thus, the road developers remain vulnerable to E&S risks arising from the aforementioned factors.

# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s credit ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with the instrument being rated. This opinion is arrived at following a detailed evaluation of the issuer’s business and financial risks, its likely cash flows over the life of the instrument being rated and the adequacy of such cash flows vis-à-vis its debt servicing obligations and other funding requirements. 

For BOT (Toll) road project SPVs, execution risks assume importance during the implementation phase, while post COD, the revenue and operating risks assume greater significance. ICRA has a more favourable opinion on projects, which have been conservatively funded, promoted by strong sponsors, which have demonstrated robust traffic flow characteristics. ICRA draws comfort from the allocation of O&M-related risks to an experienced and creditworthy O&M contractor, with requisite experience in operations and maintenance of road stretches. ICRA draws comfort from the healthy DSCR and PLCR ratios. The presence of structural features like cash flow waterfall with cash trap/sweep mechanism, contingency and O&M reserves with restrictive covenants on additional debt are credit positives.

# **Exhibit 2: Salient features of a typical NHAI concession agreement for a toll road project** 

- The CA entitles the concessionaire to design, build, finance, construct, operate and maintain the project facility during the concession period as well as to levy and collect toll fees from vehicles for using the project or any part thereof 

- The CA usually stipulates that the tolls would be levied at rates notified by a government agency and also defines the rates for annual escalation in toll rates. The government can also propose exemptions or subsidies for certain vehicle categories. While the traffic risks are to be largely borne by the concessionaire, some concession agreements make it obligatory for the government to direct vehicle movement in a particular manner or prevent the construction of alternative roads, which to an extent reduces the traffic risks. 

- In most cases, the political risks are assumed by the project owner. Further, some of the concession agreements also have provisions whereby the project owner undertakes to indemnify the project company from a shortfall in toll collections due to a political force majeure, through an advance/ revenue shortfall loans. 

- • The concession agreement usually allocates the risks associated with securing various regulatory approvals, and acquisition of land to the project owner. Most concession agreements provide for an independent consultant or supervisor to monitor the progress and the quality of construction during the project period. 

- • It also provides for the appointment of an O&M contractor to operate and maintain the highway during the concession period with ‘events of default’ being clearly specified. Some agreements also attempt to ensure the availability of adequate funds for maintenance through the creation of a ‘reserve fund’. 

- • Force majeure clauses are an integral part of any concession agreement with the types of such force majeure and the consequent sharing of such risks among the government, sponsors and the lenders being clearly defined. 

- • The concession agreement enumerates various events, whose occurrence could potentially trigger termination of the concession agreement by NHAI leading to termination payment.
