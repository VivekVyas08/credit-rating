This rating methodology updates and supersedes ICRA's earlier methodology on this subject, published in May 2024. While this revised version incorporates a few additional clarifications and editorial changes, ICRA's overall approach to rating entities in the sector remains materially similar. 

The objective of this rating methodology is to highlight the approach used to evaluate the expected loss (EL) profiles of infrastructure projects across sectors such as roads, power and airports, including the infrastructure assets which are financed through Municipal Bonds. It aims to help issuers, investors and other interested market participants understand ICRA’s approach to analyse the quantitative and qualitative risk characteristics that are likely to affect the rating outcomes. This methodology does not include an exhaustive treatment of all the factors that are reflected in the ratings but enables the reader to understand the rating considerations that are usually the most important.

India’s infrastructure sector is poised for substantial expansion, necessitating significant long-term capital to bridge the large investment gap. However, funding continues to be skewed towards bank and non-banking financial companies (NBFC) borrowings, with relatively limited participation from institutional investors and corporate bond markets. This is largely due to moderate credit ratings of infrastructure projects particularly under-construction projects, driven by inherent risks such as execution delays (during implementation phase), demand uncertainty, market risk, shorter debt tenure compared to the overall economic life of the project and risks pertaining to counterparties, which constrain their ability to access a broader investor base. The conventional credit rating framework, based on a probability of default (PD) approach, adopts a conservative stance wherein even a “single rupee, single day” delay is classified as default, without adequately reflecting eventual recovery prospects. 

In this context, the EL rating framework, when used alongside traditional PD ratings, provides a more nuanced assessment by incorporating both PD and loss given default (LGD) for the lenders and investors. This facilitates better risk differentiation and pricing of debt instruments, enabling issuers to access a wider pool of investors, including those constrained by rating thresholds. Over time, such an approach can support more efficient capital allocation while attracting wide variety of investors, enabling the issuers to raise capital at competitive rates and deeper bond market participation, thereby aiding the sector’s large-scale financing requirements 

The credit rating of infrastructure entities is constrained owing to reasons highlighted above and these risks results in <mark>lower credit ratings for entities in the infrastructure sector, at least in the initial phase, on the conventional 20-point rating scale. P</mark> ost completion and stabilisation, these projects generate a steady stream of long-term cash flows. Notwithstanding the risks associated with the operational phase, these projects often possess a nearly monopolistic market position, fair degree of inelastic demand and low technological obsolescence risk.

Further, public private partnership (PPP) infrastructure projects have additional features such as availability of termination payment mechanisms, escrow arrangements, step-in rights for lenders and underlying asset value with long economic life to safeguard the ultimate loss to lenders. As such, despite temporary cash flow disruptions, recovery outcomes are generally stronger than implied by PD-based ratings alone. 

Due to the above-mentioned characteristics, the ultimate loss, post default, is expected to be lower for infrastructure assets when compared to a corporate with a similar PD. Given that the conventional rating scale (based on the PD approach) focusses on timely servicing of financial obligations, it is not able to differentiate the entities based on the ultimate loss to be borne by the investors, and thus there was a need for a rating scale based on the EL approach. To overcome these limitations and provide broader information on the associated risks to the prospective investors, a new credit rating system (based on EL) for infrastructure projects was devised by the Department of Economic Affairs (Ministry of Finance, Government of India) and the domestic credit rating agencies in 2017. 

This EL-based rating framework is a combination of the PD and the LGD. This rating framework aims to provide a measure of risk in an infrastructure project and ranks various projects based on their EL. Moreover, in its disaggregated form, this rating system can offer an opinion on both the PD (for the investors seeking timely repayment) as well as the prospects of recovery of their principal and interest, post default, and thereby provide incremental information to the investors/lenders regarding the EL 

# **ICRA’s Expected Loss Assessment Framework** 

The EL rating follows a two-pronged approach: determining the PD and assessing the LGD. The EL for a project is the product of PD and LGD over the residual life of the project. While the PD aspect focusses on the default risks, the LGD looks at the loss in the event of default. Hence EL reflects the expected credit loss associated with the project. 

## **Steps for EL Evaluation** 

For estimating EL, ICRA first evaluates the PD and LGD at various time intervals during the residual life of the project. The methodology used for arriving at PD and LGD are explained in the next sections. The PD and LGD are then used to arrive at EL for each time period. The ELs for various time periods are then used to arrive at the final EL value for the project. ICRA has mapped various possible EL values into a seven-point rating scale. Each rating on this scale corresponds to a range of EL values, the details of which are summarised in the section on Rating Scale. For example, the [ICRA] EL 1 rating (which is the highest rating on the scale) indicates that EL value for the project is less than or equal to 1.25%.

Based on historical data on default rates for each rating category, ICRA has estimated a yearly PD for each rating. The conventional rating arrived above is mapped with this data to estimate the PD associated with the project for various time intervals. 

# **Evaluating LGD** 

The LGD is defined as loss post default, which is expressed as a percentage of the defaulted amount (principal and accrued interest). The LGD gives the estimates of actual loss, which the lender/investor will incur if the issuer defaults. It is often expressed as (1 – Recovery Rate) where Recovery Rate (RR) is the amount recovered post default as the percentage of total outstanding amount at the time of default. 

**Loss Given Default = 1 –**<sup>**Amount Recovered post default**</sup> **= 1 – Recovery Rate Amount under default** 

ICRA considers various scenarios (mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive) which can result in default and assesses the expected recoveries for each of the time intervals during the residual life of the project. These scenarios can be related to decline in revenue/cash flows which can be structural or cyclical, termination/abandoning of project, etc. Each of the default scenarios is assigned a probability of occurrence based on historical data/estimates, or judgement. Subsequently, for each of the default scenarios, the recovery amount is estimated based on the expected cash flows, waterfall mechanism for utilisation of funds, and security including termination payment, residual asset value, etc. 

The following aspects of infrastructure projects are generally considered while evaluating the recovery: 

- » Adequacy of termination payments (PPP projects) in conjunction with the credit quality of the concessioning authority, or residual value of the asset in general 

- » Step-in/Substitution rights available with lenders (would be helpful in a scenario where sponsor group is in stress) 

- » Adequacy of the insurance cover 

In terms of termination payments, ICRA notes that the receipt of the termination payments might take longer than anticipated. Accordingly, certain scenarios are drawn on the timelines within which the termination payments are received, and this feeds into the recovery estimation. 

ICRA’s approach involves assessing the LGDs for scenarios in which the outcome is dependent on certain variables (e.g. plant load factor (PLF), coal price in case of a thermal power plant). Subsequently, the weighted average LGD for each time interval is estimated with the weights being the likelihood of each scenario. 

# **Evaluating EL** 

Post evaluation of PD and LGD, ICRA evaluates the EL for each time interval. It is evaluated as a product of the PD, the LGD and the Exposure at Default (EAD), which is the amount of debt outstanding at the time of default as a percentage of the total debt. Subsequently, the incremental EL at each time interval is discounted to the present value (using average cost of debt) and added to arrive at the final EL estimates. 



## _r = average cost of debt; t = project residual life_ 

The final EL value is mapped to the EL rating scale to arrive at the final rating.

# **Rating Scale** 

The rating scale for the EL ratings is a seven-point scale from [ICRA] EL 1 to [CRA] EL 7, with [ICRA] EL 1 being the highest rating with the lowest EL and [ICRA]EL 7 being the lowest rating with the highest EL. The broad definition of the rating scale is provided in the table below: 

|**Rating**|**Indicative EL**|**Definition**|
|---|---|---|
|**[ICRA]EL 1**|**Up to 1.25%**|**Lowest expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 1’ are considered to have the lowest expected<br>loss over the life of the instrument|
|**[ICRA]EL 2**|**1.25% to 3.5%**|**Very low expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 2’ are considered to have very low expected<br>loss over the life of the instrument|
|**[ICRA]EL 3**|**3.5% to 7.5%**|**Low expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 3’ are considered to have low expected loss over<br>the life of the instrument|
|**[ICRA]EL 4**|**7.5% to 15%**|**Moderate expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 4’ are considered to have moderate<br>expected loss over the life of the instrument|
|**[ICRA]EL 5**|**15% to 25%**|**High expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 5’ are considered to have high expected loss over<br>the life of the instrument|
|**[ICRA]EL 6**|**25% to 35%**|**Very high expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 6’ are considered to have very high expected<br>loss over the life of the instrument|
|**[ICRA]EL 7**|**Over 35%**|**Highest expected loss**– Instruments rated ‘EL 7’ are considered to have the highest expected<br>loss over the life of the instrument|



# **Summing Up** 

ICRA’s EL ratings are a symbolic representation of its opinion on the expected credit loss associated with the instrument being rated. While the PD aspect of the scale focusses on the default risks or timeliness of debt servicing, the LGD part assesses the loss or recovery prospects post default and factors in the overall project viability over its life cycle (or over the concession period), the structure of debt and various protective aspects of infrastructure assets such as the strength of concession, termination payment adequacy, timelines of receipt of termination payment, etc. In effect, the EL ratings provide an opinion on the overall recovery prospects of the investors’/ lenders’ debt exposure over the tenure of the instrument for a given infrastructure asset.
