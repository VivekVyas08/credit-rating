# **Overview** 

This rating methodology updates and supersedes ICRA’s earlier methodology document on this subject, published in November 2020. While this revised version incorporates a few modifications, ICRA’s overall approach to rating Pooled Loan/ Bond Issuance structures remains materially similar. 

# **What is a Pooled Loan Issuance Structure?** 

Under a pooled loan issuance (PLI) structure, a lender (bank or non-banking financial company or NBFC) provides loans to multiple borrower entities (typically NBFCs, microfinance institutions (MFIs) or corporates). Similarly, under a pooled bond issuance (PBI), an investor subscribes to bonds issued by multiple entities. The loans provided to (or bonds issued by) different borrower entities are pooled together and credit enhanced, typically by a common partial guarantee provided by a guarantor in favour of the lenders/investors. For the sake of convenience, we would be referring to the PLI/PBI structures as PLI structures for the rest of this note unless explicitly mentioned. 

# **Analytical Framework for Assessing PLI Structures** 

The credit ratings assigned by ICRA address the timely payment of the due principal and interest on the debt being rated. A single-day-single-rupee delay in the payment of the amounts due is treated as a default. Thus, a particular debt instrument of an issuer can be rated higher than its standalone credit rating if there are some structural features or some form of explicit support associated with that instrument, which ensure(s) that the investor in this instrument is more likely — relative to the issuer’s other debt holders — to receive all payments in full and in a timely manner, as per the loan’s payment schedule. 

A common form of explicit support for a loan or debt instrument has been an unconditional and irrevocable guarantee covering the entire dues through the full tenure of the instrument from an entity that is rated higher than the issuer of the said instrument. The guarantee, coupled with a timely invocation and payment mechanism, ensures that the probability of default (PD) of the instrument is the same as that implied by the credit rating of the guarantor. This is a classic case of credit substitution, i.e., the credit rating of the instrument is equated to the credit rating of the guarantor.

However, in recent times, structures have emerged wherein loans given to different borrowers by a single lender are pooled together and credit enhanced by a common partial guarantee provided by a guarantor. The guarantee covers a percentage of the initial principal outstanding of the total pooled debt (i.e., the aggregate amount of guarantee is lower than the aggregate pooled debt amount. The guarantee is designed as a first-loss default guarantee (i.e., the guarantor agrees to pay the entire shortfall pertaining to one or a few instalments for the borrowers in the PLI structure rather than a limited or pro-rata proportion of the instalment). To the extent that the credit enhancement in the structure is adequate to fully meet some of the instalments for the borrowers in the PLI structure, the PD on those instalments can be reduced. When an entity defaults on its debt servicing obligation, it could be owing to a temporary liquidity stress or a serious and enduring solvency issue. If the default is a temporary one, the entity could get back to health and resume regular debt servicing over a short period of time. If the guarantee is sufficient to meet that entity’s obligations under the PLI structure for that period, it would be able to protect the holders of that particular debt from default even though the other lenders to that company faced a default. Thus, owing to the credit enhancement through a common partial guarantee, the debt instruments in the structure can be rated higher than the standalone credit rating of the individual borrowers in the structure.

# **Key Elements of the Transaction Structure** 

The key elements of a PLI structure have been summarised below: 

## **Partial Guarantee** 

- **First-loss nature of common partial guarantee:** An improvement in the credit rating can happen only if there is a reduction in the probability of default (PD) of the debt being rated. Therefore, the common partial guarantee should be designed as a first-loss default guarantee i.e., any shortfall in debt servicing obligations by the borrowers in the structure should be met from the common partial guarantee as long as the net cumulative utilisation is lower than the stipulated quantum of the guarantee. If the common partial guarantee is to be utilised on a pro-rata basis in the event of a shortfall in the cashflows to meet the debt servicing obligations (for example, if the guarantee is 20% of the pooled loan size, and on each

event of shortfall, the guarantor pays 20% of the shortfall amount), it would result in a default at each instance of shortfall. Therefore, such a guarantee would not enhance the credit quality of the structure. 

- **Common partial guarantee to be unconditional, irrevocable and legally enforceable:** The common partial guarantee must be unconditional, irrevocable and legally enforceable. Further, post invocation, any payments to the guarantor should be subordinated to payments on the rated debt, at least till there is no payment default on the rated debt. Similarly, non-payment to the guarantor should not constitute an event of default for the rated debt. 

- **Non-amortising/amortising guarantee:** The common partial guarantee can be non-amortising (i.e., the guarantee remains fixed in absolute terms during the tenure of the debt, thereby increasing in percentage terms as the pooled debt amortises), amortising (i.e., the guarantee amortises in line with the amortisation schedule of the pooled debt being rated) or somewhere in between (i.e., the guarantee builds up in percentage terms initially with pooled debt amortisation and then stays at the same level in percentage terms thereafter). Comfort drawn from a non-amortising guarantee would be higher compared to an amortising guarantee if all other factors remain the same. This is because in a non-amortising guarantee, the absolute level of guarantee remains the same over the life of the instrument and thus the debt coverage by the guarantee increases as the debt amortises. On the contrary, in an amortising guarantee, the absolute level of the guarantee comes down as the debt amortises, and this could result in reduction in debt coverage by the guarantee. While an amortising guarantee may cover several instalments at the time of the pooled debt issuance, it may cover only a fraction of an instalment towards the end of the tenure of the rated pooled debt. In the subsequent period, if the borrower reimburses the defaulted amount, the guaranteed amount will be restored to the prior amount. While assessing these structures, ICRA considers the number of future debt installments covered by the guarantee. Transactions with higher number of installments covered are considered more favorable. 

- **Guarantee invocation and payment mechanism:** The invocation of the common partial guarantee and subsequent payment by the guarantor should happen in a timely manner such that there is no default on the rated instruments. In these structures, the borrowers/issuers have to fund a designated account prior to the due date of payment to the lender/investor. The trustee checks the adequacy of funds and invokes the guarantee in the event of any shortfall. The guarantor is obligated to fund the designated account on or before the due date to ensure timely payment to the investor/lender on the due date. 

- **Cross-default and acceleration with the other debt of issuer:** An improvement in the credit rating through a common partial guarantee can be constrained if the rated debt backed by the common partial guarantee has an acceleration clause owing to a cross default by the issuer on any other debt / proposed debt on its balance sheet. In such a scenario, any default on other debt (rated lower than the rating of the common partial guarantee-backed debt) by a borrower would trigger an acceleration of the common partial guarantee-backed debt, leading to the faster utilisation of the common partial guarantee. Even if the partial guarantee fully covers the accelerated debt of a particular borrower, the remaining borrowers in the structure would have lower residual guarantee available for credit enhancement. 

- **Cashflow waterfall:** The priority of payments or the cash flow waterfall is an important element (for instance, the treatment of cashflows in  the  transaction,  mechanism  for the reinstatement  of  the  guarantee, etc).  An important factor in this regard is that the guarantor’s claim on the cashflows should be subordinate to that of the senior  debt  lender. In  other  words,  in  the  event  that a  part  of  the  guarantee  has  been utilised,  in  the  subsequent period, the payment to the guarantor should only be made if there is any excess left after meeting the senior debt dues during that period.

- **Number of borrowers and diversification between borrower entities in the PLI structure:** The number of borrowers and the degree of correlation between various borrowers in the PLI structure have an impact on the benefit arising from the structure. The higher the number of borrowers, the lower the credit risk associated with the PLI structure (the loan exposure should also be well distributed among the borrowers). This is because in the event of default by one of the borrowers in the structure, the PD of the other borrowers in the structure with a similar business model could also be high. The credit enhancement required in the structure is thus higher in case of (a) fewer number of borrowers in the structure, (b) concentrated loan exposure on the relatively lower rated borrowers,and (c) high correlation (by asset class and geographical presence in case of NBFCs and MFIs) between the borrowing entities. 

- **Credit quality of borrowing entities and guarantor:** ICRA analyses the credit profile of the borrowing entities in the PLI structure as well as the guarantor, following the applicable sector methodology, to determine the individual entities’ probability of default (as represented by the credit rating).The weighted average rating of the borrowers in a PLI structure is one of the key inputs to determine the notching up that is possible based on the credit enhancement available in the transaction. The higher the average rating, the lower would be the credit enhancement required to achieve the same final rating. The individual standalone rating of the borrowing entities would also have a bearing on the final rating of the transaction under PLI structure. The final rating of the PLI would, however, be capped at the rating of the guarantor. 

- **Environmental and Social risks:** While PLIs do not face material physical climate risks, they are exposed to environmental risks indirectly through their business of the borrowing entities. If the entities or businesses on whom PLIs have an exposure face business disruption because of physical climate adversities, or if such businesses face climate transition risks because of technological, regulatory, or customer behaviour changes, it could translate into credit risks for the PLIs. However, the risk tends to be mitigated via adequate portfolio diversification. Further, the exposure is typically over a short-to-medium time horizon that could allow the PLIs to adapt by utilizing the guarantee in the interim period. Likewise, the exposure of PLIs to social risks depends on the nature of the sectors and/ or entities and their relative concentration. 

# **ICRA’s Approach for Rating Instruments in PLI Structure** 

ICRA’s framework for rating instruments in the PLI structure backed by a common partial guarantee involves the steps illustrated below: 

## **1. Assessing the credit quality of borrowers and guarantor** 

ICRA assesses the credit quality of each borrower entity in the PLI structure and the guarantor to arrive at a standalone credit rating for each of them. ICRA’s approach for assessing the standalone credit quality of an entity depends on the industry in which it operates. ICRA follows the rating methodology applicable for the respective industry as available on ICRA’s website. 

## **2. Assessing the partial guarantee** 

PLI structures have a common partial guarantee provided by a guarantor. The guarantee should be unconditional, irrevocable and legally enforceable. The guarantee should also have a defined invocation and payment mechanism. Other parameters considered while assessing the guarantee are explained in the preceding section. 

## **3. PD estimation through modelling** 

ICRA’s cashflow modelling for estimating the PD in the PLI structure is based on the following inputs: 

- Composition of PLI structure: This considers the borrower-wise break-up of debt in the PLI structure. 

- Scheduled payouts/repayments to investor/lender: The scheduled principal and interest payouts to the investor/lender by each borrowing entity in the PLI structure is taken into consideration. 

- Common partial guarantee amount: This is the proportion of the total principal amount outstanding of the pooled loans covered by the partial guarantee. 

- Correlation between entities: The correlation between the borrowing entities in the PLI structure, based on business diversification (in terms of asset class and geographical presence in case of NBFCs, MFIs), is taken into consideration. The higher the correlation between the entities, the higher is the probability of the entities defaulting at the same time, thereby resulting in a higher common guarantee amount with everything else remaining the same. 

- PD of each borrower: Each borrower in the PLI structure has a PD driven by its standalone credit rating and the tenure of the debt being rated. The longer the tenure of the debt and lower the credit rating, the higher would be the PD of the

- Build-up of PD: All the underlying loans are not expected to default simultaneously if the borrowers are not highly correlated. Indeed, certain loans may pay for a certain period and default later. Thus, another input in the model is the timing of the default. ICRA’s assumption on the PD build-up typically depends on the historically observed trends. 

- Loss-given default (LGD): It is based on the seniority of the debt and the nature of the security against the loan sanctioned. While evaluating the LGD, ICRA factors in the possibility of actual recovery through the sale of the underlying security during the tenure of the loan. For example, for a short-tenure loan (2-3 years), the LGD is taken as 100% as it is unlikely that any recovery would happen through the sale of the underlying security within the tenure. 

- Recovery probability: This factors in the probability and time frame (i.e. months) by which the entity in the PLI structure would be able to recover post default. 

Based on the above inputs, ICRA simulates various scenarios of payouts/repayments to the investors/lenders. In some of these scenarios, the cashflow model might indicate a shortfall in cashflow (even after the full utilisation of the common partial guarantee) or a default to the investor/lender. The number of such scenarios relative to the total number of scenarios simulated gives the PD for the lender/investor in that transaction. This simulated PD is then compared with ICRA’s internal benchmarks for the assigned rating. The higher the assigned rating for the instruments in the PLI structure, the lower is the associated default probability. While assigning the rating, ICRA also considers the extent of individual entity defaults that the guarantee covers initially, and the build-up of guarantee cover as the loans amortises for determining the rating and credit enhancement / guarantee cover in the transaction. 

# **Summing up** 

The approach used by ICRA to evaluate the credit rating of the PLI structure incorporates the assessment of the credit risk of the underlying entities and the guarantor, the correlation between the borrowers as well as the coverage provided by the partial guarantee and modelling the likely PD using statistical techniques. The various assumptions made while modelling the default probability may change from time to time based on the specific structure of a transaction and the credit quality of the underlying borrowers.
