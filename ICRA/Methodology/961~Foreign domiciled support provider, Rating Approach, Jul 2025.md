This rating methodology outlines ICRA’s approach to assessing the credit profile of a foreign domiciled parent or group entities<sup>1</sup> , when a domestic entity’s credit profile is influenced by either an implicit or explicit support from such a parent. 

# **Overview** 

ICRA’s credit rating is a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risk associated with an entity’s ability to timely service its debt obligations. Typically, an entity’s rating is based on its standalone credit profile, which reflects its exposure to industry, business, financial, and management risks<sup>_2_</sup> . However, in certain cases, the credit quality of an entity may also be influenced by the strength of its parent and the nature of support, either explicit or implicit, available from the parent. 

Explicit support is in the form of a contractual commitment, such as a guarantee, while the implicit support is non-contractual and based on the expectation of support from the parent<sup>_3_</sup> . In the case of implicit support, ICRA evaluates the likelihood of timely support based on qualitative factors such as the extent of business linkages, strategic importance, or reputational considerations from the parent’s perspective.  The presence of parental support from a stronger parent typically leads to an uplift in an entity’s rating compared to its standalone rating due to reduced probability of default. 

To factor in both explicit and implicit support, it is essential to evaluate the parent’s credit profile to assess its ability to extend support to the entity.  For instance, a parent which is rated [ICRA]AAA is expected to have greater financial capacity and flexibility to extend support to a weaker subsidiary, compared to a parent which is rated [ICRA]BBB. Therefore, between two entities with similar standalone risk profiles, the one backed by a stronger parent is likely to receive a higher rating uplift over its unsupported rating. 

However, when the parent is foreign-domiciled, ICRA may face limitations in accessing detailed information about its financials, strategic plans, and objectives, making it difficult to evaluate the parent’s credit worthiness. These limitations raise a pertinent question for stakeholders: how does ICRA assess the credit profile of a foreign parent for evaluating the credit profile of its Indian subsidiaries and group companies? This document outlines ICRA’s analytical approach for evaluating the credit profile of a foreign-domiciled parent that has either provided explicit support or is expected to extend extraordinary support during periods of financial stress, based on implicit support considerations, to its Indian subsidiary.

> **_2_** ICRA's rating methodologies, sector-specific and cross-sector, provide a detailed description of the key drivers of an entity's credit rating. These are available on ICRA’s website www.icra.in.

# **Approach to evaluating a foreign domiciled parent** 

ICRA seeks to assess the credit risk profile of a foreign parent using the first principles, through the application of its established methodologies based on available information and discussions with the management. However, this approach may not always be feasible due to constraints such as limited access to information and the complexity of evaluating diverse regulatory, economic, and political risks associated with the parent’s geographical footprint. 

In such cases, ICRA relies on the credit rating assigned to the foreign parent by Moody’s Investors Service (Moody’s) under its global rating scale and maps it to ICRA’s national rating scale. Where Moody’s rating corresponds to multiple possible ratings on ICRA’s scale, ICRA determines the appropriate mapped rating by evaluating the parent’s relative positioning on the Moody’s rating scale, taking into account its rating outlook (Positive, Stable, or Negative) or review status. 

This mapping-based approach is adopted while being cognizant of its inherent limitations, which are discussed in the following section. 

# **Differences between global and national rating scales** 

Credit ratings are relative indicators of credit risk. On a national rating scale, an entity with the strongest credit profile within a country is assigned the highest rating. However, this does not necessarily imply that the entity has the lowest credit risk in a global context. On a global rating scale, the same entity may be rated lower, as its credit risk is evaluated relative to the strongest entities worldwide. 

Global rating scales, such as Moody’s, are designed to differentiate credit risk by considering not only an entity’s fundamental credit profile but also its exposure to sovereign risk. Sovereign risk varies across countries due to differences in economic strength, fiscal health, institutional maturity, and vulnerability to external shocks. In contrast, national rating scales, such as ICRA’s, are intended solely for intra-country comparisons and hence sovereign risk is not a differentiating element. As a result of this fundamental difference in scope and design, the credit rating of a given entity may differ significantly between the global and national rating scales. 

# **Mapping global ratings to ICRA’s rating scale** 

Credit ratings represent relative credit risk, where higher ratings correspond to lower probabilities of default. This ordinal relationship is consistent across both global and national rating scales. However, the absolute default rates associated with each rating level can vary significantly between the two scales, due to which the global ratings are not directly comparable to ICRA’s national scale ratings. 

To map a rating on Moody’s global scale to ICRA’s national scale, ICRA performs a comparative analysis of long-term historical default rates across corresponding rating levels. This analysis also incorporates Moody’s ratings for entities that are commonly rated by ICRA, allowing for adjustments that reflect differences in scale, market presence, competitive dynamics and financial flexibility withing the Indian context. 

It’s important to note that this mapping is not uniform. The divergence between Moody’s global ratings and ICRA’s national ratings is more pronounced in the lower investment-grade segment and gradually narrows as one moves toward higher investment-grade and lower non-investment-grade categories.

## **Would an entity rated at par with India’s sovereign rating on the global scale automatically map to [ICRA]AAA?** 

An entity rated at the same level as India’s sovereign rating on the global rating scale does not necessarily map to an [ICRA]AAA rating on the national scale. There are two reasons for this: 

- **Mapping Methodology** : ICRA uses a mapping heuristic to translate global ratings to its national scale. This involves comparing the historical default rates of Moody’s global ratings with those of ICRA’s national ratings. However, data indicates that the default rates and other performance metrics for corporates and sovereigns are not directly comparable. As a result, sovereign ratings cannot be used as a basis for mapping. Consequently, even if a corporate entity shares the same global rating as the Indian sovereign, it may not warrant an [ICRA]AAA rating on the national scale. 

- **Analytical Consistency** : Consider a scenario where both the Indian sovereign and a corporate entity are rated Baa2 (or BBB) globally. While the sovereign would typically be rated [ICRA]AAA on the national scale, assigning the same rating to the corporate entity solely based on its global rating parity could lead to future inconsistencies. For instance, if the sovereign is later upgraded to Baa1 (or BBB+), but the corporate entity remains at Baa2, would the corporate’s national rating be downgraded simply because India’s sovereign rating has improved? Such an action would lack analytical coherence. 

# **Limitations of the mapping-based approach** 

Global ratings are typically anchored to a country’s sovereign rating. As a result, entities domiciled in countries with a lower sovereign rating, or those that are relatively small or less prominent in the global context, often receive ratings clustered at the lower end of the global scale. This leads to the mapped ratings being concentrated within a narrow band, limiting meaningful credit differentiation on the national scale, the very purpose of having a separate scale. In contrast, an independent assessment enables a more granular evaluation of credit risk relative to domestic peers. 

Moreover, the mapping-based approach does not capture the specific nuances of an entity’s business profile or its relative positioning with respect to the domestic peer group. While the approach may be analytically sound when applied in aggregate, its effectiveness can be constrained when used to assess individual entities. 

# **Summing Up** 

When an entity’s credit profile incorporates implicit or explicit support from a foreign-domiciled parent, it becomes essential to assess the parent’s creditworthiness to evaluate its capacity to provide such support. While the mapping-based approach can be a helpful tool in this context, it is not ICRA’s primary method for forming rating opinions. 

ICRA’s preferred approach is to independently assess the foreign parent’s credit profile using its established analytical frameworks, wherever sufficient information is available. The mapping approach is employed only in cases where such information is limited, and even then, with full awareness of its inherent limitations.
