This rating methodology describes ICRA’s approach to assessing structural features associated with a debt instrument, and supersedes ICRA's earlier methodology document on this subject, published in September 2023. While this revised document incorporates a few clarifications and editorial changes, ICRA's overall analytical approach to evaluate the structural features remains materially similar. 

# **Overview** 

ICRA’s credit rating is a symbolic representation of its opinion on the relative credit risks associated with timely debt servicing by an entity. One debt instrument of an entity may be rated higher than its other debt instruments if it possesses attributes that increase the likelihood of investors receiving full and timely payments, relative to those holding the entity’s other debt instruments. This could be possible if there exists some form of explicit credit support for a rated debt instrument from a stronger thirdparty through support mechanisms like guarantees, shortfall undertakings and standby letters of credit, or if there are structural features associated with a debt instrument, such as a defined cash flow waterfall through an escrow mechanism or a security/ asset that provides a pre-default utility. 

This methodology document describes how ICRA evaluates the structural features associated with an instrument while assigning ratings in cases where the presence of such structural features is a credit differentiator<sup>**_1_**</sup> . The note is organised into the following three sections:

# **Types of structural features** 

The structural features associated with a debt instrument either: 

- Prevent the mingling of cash flows that are earmarked for the servicing of the rated debt instrument with the entity’s other cash flows, by way of an escrow mechanism ( _this may or may not be accompanied by other features such as cash sweep or cash trap_ ), or 

- Involve a security/asset that can be monetised or utilised to ensure timely debt servicing. 

The debt instrument of an entity that is supported by structural features may be rated higher than its other debt instruments, reflecting a lower probability of default. However, the difference between such ratings is generally limited, as these structural features typically represent on-balance sheet support mechanisms. Consequently, the debt instrument remains exposed to risks, such as bankruptcy risks, faced by the entity as a whole, which may arise upon the initiation of insolvency proceedings by operational creditors or financial creditors of the entity. In other words, the presence of structural features can generally provide credit enhancement to a debt instrument only to a limited extent, unlike the case of a bankruptcy remote third-party explicit credit support, based on which the extent of credit enhancement may be, though not necessarily, much higher. 

The structural features could be associated with all debt instruments of an entity or with only a few specific ones. Moreover, the characteristics of the structural features associated with different debt instruments could also differ. As a result, the credit risks associated with different debt instruments of the same entity could vary based on the attributes of the associated assets and the specific structural features of each instrument. Ratings based on structural features are not accompanied by any suffix.

Apart from the above, there may be certain features associated with debt instruments, such as covenants in loan documents, restricting an entity from taking additional borrowings or provisions to regularly fund a reserve to ensure adequate funding availability for a scheduled large expenditure. However, such features impact the credit profile of all debt instruments of an entity equally and thus, are not credit differentiators among the various debt instruments of an entity. 

A DSRA, either in the form of (1) cash (generally a fixed deposit with a financial institution) or (2) a non-fund-based facility (from a financial institution), equivalent to debt repayment obligations over a specified period (typically three to six months), is considered for liquidity assessment rather than as a structural feature, if the DSRA can be utilised before the due date of debt servicing. In such cases, the DSRA reduces the risk of default arising from temporary cash flow mismatches. Moreover, the DSRA is generally associated with a specific debt instrument and is maintained alongside an escrow mechanism, and it can be utilised only in case of a shortfall in servicing that particular debt instrument. Thus, the DSRA is considered in the liquidity assessment of only the instrument(s) for which it is available, and not as a general liquidity support for the entity. More details on ICRA’s approach to liquidity assessment are available in the methodology document titled Rating Approach – Liquidity Analysis (Non-Financial Sector) on ICRA’s website.

# **Key attributes of various structural features** 

The presence of structural features enhances the credit profile of a debt instrument. However, only if the features have a utility in substance rather than being available just in form. The attributes which may be associated with the various structural features are described below: 

## **Escrow mechanism** 

## • <u>Cash flow waterfall</u> 

An escrow mechanism prevents the co-mingling of an entity’s cash flows from identified assets or revenue sources with its other cash flows. As the escrow account is monitored by the lender, investor, or trustee, withdrawals by the borrower are generally permitted either with the former’s consent or in line with a defined sequence for cash flow utilisation. Hence, the borrower’s discretion in deploying cash flows is restricted. An escrow mechanism, characterised by a pre-determined sequence for the utilisation of cash flows (cash flow waterfall) such that these are first utilised for incurring the necessary operating expenses and debt servicing before being available for other purposes (including distribution to shareholders), provides comfort that the necessary expenditure and debt servicing are prioritised over other sundry requirements and shareholder payout. 

## • <u>Cash trap</u> 

In addition to defining the sequence of cash flow utilisation, escrow waterfall terms may also mandate that surplus cash flows are directed towards creating a liquidity reserve, either by default or in the event of covenant breaches. Such provisions ensure pre-emptive cash preservation when early signs of cash flow weakness emerge. 

## • <u>Cash sweep</u> 

The cash-flow waterfall could also include a mechanism to ensure that the surplus cash flows, after meeting the defined sequence of cash flow utilisation, are used for mandatory debt pre-payment to ensure that the surpluses are only used for debt servicing. This mechanism ensures that the amortisation of the debt is accelerated during periods when the surpluses are strong, thereby paring down the debt servicing obligation (due to faster run-down of the debt) in years when the surpluses are subdued. 

## **Security/ asset back-up** 

## • <u>Security/asset cover</u> 

The higher the security or asset cover relative to debt obligations (beyond 1.0 times), the stronger the likelihood of realising full debt value upon monetisation or utilisation of the security. The security cover is seen in conjunction with the volatility in the value of the security, as a high volatility would necessitate a higher cover and vice-versa – to achieve a similar volatility-adjusted factor of safety. 

## • <u>Liquidity</u> 

A better liquidity position of a security enhances the prospects of monetising/ utilising it in a timely manner at a value closer to its assessed value. Liquidity is generally a function of the asset class to which the security belongs (a liquid balance like a fixed deposit versus an investment in real estate, an illiquid asset class), the demand-supply dynamics and the cost of monetising the security.

- <u>Granularity</u> 

Granularity refers to the ability to monetise the security/ asset in tranches/ piece-meal rather than as a whole. Higher granularity increases the flexibility to monetise the asset at a value closer to its fair value, given the option to attract a diverse set of buyers for various sub-parts and the option to gradually monetise the asset, thereby reducing the cost of monetisation. 

# • <u>Mechanism for initiation of security monetisation</u> 

Comfort can be derived from a given structure only if there is a time-bound mechanism for mandatory monetisation of the security before the due date. In the absence of such a mechanism, the timely monetisation of the security remains at the discretion of the lender or investor, which may result in delays in debt servicing and consequently limit the utility of such a structure. 

# • <u>Cushion between the due date of debt servicing and initiation of security monetisation</u> 

The cushion between the date when the monetisation of the security is contractually stipulated to be initiated by the lender or investor and the due date for debt servicing should be such that it provides adequate time for enforcement of the lenders’ or investor’s right to monetise the security. The adequacy is assessed by considering the nature of the security in terms of its liquidity, besides the estimated procedural time for monetisation. The higher the above cushion, greater is the likelihood of the structure supporting timely debt servicing. 

- <u>Credit profile of the financial institution maintaining the security/ asset or the underlying fixed income security (in case</u> - 

- <u>the security is a non equity financial instrument)</u> 

The efficacy of the security lies in its adequacy and timely availability to ensure timely debt servicing in full. As a result, the credit profile of the financial institution holding the security is an important determinant for assessing the timely availability of the security in case the security is a financial instrument such as cash or a fixed deposit. This is because any distress at the financial institution could limit the access to such security. In case the security is in the form of investments in fixed-income securities, the credit quality of investments is important to ensure the realisation of the full value of the underlying investment. Thus, the credit rating of a debt instrument, backed by some security cover, is linked to the rating of the financial institution maintaining the security ( _cash balances or fixed deposit_ ), or the rating of the security itself ( _when the security is in the form of investments in fixed-income securities_ ). In case the cash balance/fixed deposit is held with different financial institutions, or the back-up held is in the form of various fixedincome securities having different credit worthiness, then the lowest credit rating among them is taken as an anchor for credit enhancement. 

# • <u>Bankruptcy remoteness</u> 

A security directly held by the entity would not be available to support debt servicing in case the entity enters bankruptcy proceedings due to default on operational or financial creditors. This is because upon the initiation of bankruptcy, the security would be considered as part of the entity’s estate and its utilisation could be put under a moratorium as per insolvency law. As a result, the efficacy of such a security would be limited, which would constrain the extent of credit enhancement for the debt instruments backed by such securities. However, if the terms of the rated debt structure involve a mandatory squaring-off of the debt outstanding against the security before the initiation of the bankruptcy, irrespective of the debt falling due, such a security would ensure timely debt servicing. Such a structure could thereby typically result in a higher credit enhancement.

# **Approach to assess the credit profile of debt instruments backed by structural features** 

ICRA’s approach to assess the credit profile of the debt instruments backed by structural features is described below: 

## **Escrow mechanism** 

In cases where cash flows relating to an asset are ringfenced through an escrow mechanism, the business and financial profiles of such an asset are analysed independently of the overall entity<sup>_4_</sup> . Attributes such as cash trap and cash sweep, if applicable, are also assessed for analysing the financial profile in terms of their impact on debt coverage, leverage and liquidity. ICRA also evaluates the adherence of the structure to contractual terms over the tenor of the instrument, as a lack of adherence is a rating sensitivity factor. 

However, in case there are operational and financial creditors at the entity-level who are outside escrow mechanism and could pose a bankruptcy risk, the extent of rating upgrade/ credit enhancement for debt backed by the escrow mechanism would be limited relative to the entity’s base rating. In such cases, the rating of the escrow mechanism-supported debt instrument would be closer to the entity’s base rating, even if not exactly the same. 

**_Assessing the entity’s base rating when surpluses from the escrow could be distributed for meeting any sundry expenses/requirements at the entity level:_** For evaluating the entity’s base rating, a consolidated analysis of the entity is conducted by including the assets whose cash flows are escrowed, while excluding the DSRA or any other liquidity reserve available only to service a specific debt. 

## **_Assessing the entity’s base rating when surpluses from the escrow cannot be distributed for meeting any sundry expenses/_** 

**_requirements at the entity level:_** The escrowed assets are excluded while evaluating the entity’s base rating, as cash flows from such assets are restricted and not available at the entity level. 

Theoretically, the rating of the debt instrument based on the analysis of a specific asset whose cash flows are escrowed could be lower than the entity’s base rating. Nonetheless, the entity’s base rating generally acts as the rating floor for all of its debt instruments (except those that have equity-like features) as the issuer is expected to support the servicing of all its debt instruments. As a result, while surplus from assets with restricted cash flow is not considered for assessing the entity’s base rating, the expected shortfalls in the corresponding debt instrument are factored, while assessing the entity’s base rating. 

## **Security/ asset back-up** 

Debt instruments expected to be serviced through the monetisation of security or assets are assessed based on the likelihood of the timely realisation of an amount at least equal to the outstanding debt obligations. Accordingly, credit enhancement is considered only if the security offers adequate coverage, liquidity, and granularity, and if the time gap between the initiation of monetisation and the debt servicing due date is adequate in relation to the nature of the security. If the security/ asset is a financial instrument, such as a fixed deposit with a financial institution or a fixed-income security, the rating of debt instruments backed by such security/asset could be theoretically at par with the rating of the financial institution holding the deposit, or the credit quality of the underlying fixed-income security. However, even in such cases, the final rating would be typically constrained to remain closer to the entity’s base rating due to the bankruptcy-related risks. These risks may limit access to the asset in the event of bankruptcy proceedings against the entity. Conversely, if the security or asset is bankruptcyremote, either by its inherent nature or through structural arrangements, the rating of the supported debt instrument could be closer to that of the financial institution or the underlying asset.

**Rating approach in the case of contractual-surplus-sharing arrangement between entities** : There could be the presence of a contractual-surplus-sharing arrangement between various SPVs in a Group, whereby each SPV contributes its surplus cash flows (after adhering to its own specified cash flow waterfall and other features) to support the debt servicing of the other SPVs. In such cases, in addition to the SPVs’ own cash flows, any surpluses available from the other SPVs may also be considered to evaluate the SPVs’ credit profile, depending upon the invocation and payment timelines for surplus sharing between the SPVs. For more details on the rating approach under various scenarios, please refer to the ICRA’s methodology titled ‘Rating Approach – Consolidation’ available on ICRA’s website. 

**Rating approach in case of debt instruments with cross-default linkages** : In case of cross-default linkages among the various debt instruments of an entity, then the rating of these instruments will generally be capped at the base rating of the entity. 

# **Summing Up** 

If a debt instrument has structural features with specific attributes, it reduces the default probability by preventing mingling of cash flows from a specified asset with the entity’s other cash flow streams or through pre-funding the debt servicing through security creation. This document outlines the various structural features and their attributes, along with ICRA’s approach to assess the credit profile based on such features. 

However, structural features generally provide only limited credit enhancement to a debt instrument compared to the entity’s base rating, unlike explicit third-party credit support, which can result in significantly higher credit enhancement. This is because the credit risk associated with a debt instrument with structural features is not fully delinked from the entity’s overall credit risk profile, due to the bankruptcy risks that may arise from defaults on other operational and financial creditors.
